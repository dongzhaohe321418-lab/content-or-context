**The Wrong Way Up**

The bus left them at the turning for Elphin and went on north without them, and for a while the only sound was the wind moving through the heather like someone searching a coat for keys.

"You said there'd be a car," Callum said.

"I said there'd be a way." His great-aunt Morag was already shouldering her pack, an ancient canvas thing with a leather patch where a hammer had worn through it decades ago. "This is the way. It's four miles. You're nineteen."

"You're eighty-one."

"Then I'll set the pace and you'll have nothing to complain about."

She did set the pace, and it was faster than he expected. The road ran along the bottom of a wide, bare glen, and on either side the land rose in shapes that didn't look like the hills he knew from the train window at home: blunt, isolated, standing up out of the moor as if they had been dropped there rather than grown. One of them, off to the west, had a long tilted back and a sheer grey prow, like a ship that had run aground and been left to rot over ten thousand winters.

"Cul Mor," Morag said, without his asking. "And the sugarloaf behind is Suilven. You'll see it better tomorrow if the cloud lifts."

"Are we climbing them?"

"God, no. We're going to look at a line."

He didn't ask what she meant. He hadn't asked much of anything in the three weeks since he'd come to stay with her in Inverness, which had been his mother's idea and nobody else's. His mother had phoned Morag on a Tuesday and Callum had been on the train by Thursday, carrying a rucksack and the letter from the university that said, in the warmest possible language, that he would not be returning for second year. Morag had read the letter once at her kitchen table, folded it into quarters, and used it to level the leg of the table that rocked. Then she'd made him an omelette. That had been the whole conversation.

---

The hostel was a converted schoolhouse with six beds and a wood stove that took Morag twenty minutes and most of a newspaper to persuade. Nobody else was staying. Outside, the light went on and on, the long northern dusk that never quite became night in June, and Callum sat on the step with his phone held up toward a single bar of signal that came and went like a bird landing and leaving.

"Who are you trying to reach?" Morag asked from the doorway.

"Nobody. Just checking."

"Checking what?"

He lowered the phone. "If anything's happened."

She considered this. "Something's always happened. That's not a reason to look." She came and sat beside him, slowly, one hand on the doorframe. Her knees cracked. "Your grandfather used to do that with the shipping forecast. Every night, even when he hadn't been near a boat in thirty years. Said it calmed him to know where the weather was."

"Did it?"

"No. But he liked knowing he'd been told."

---

In the morning the cloud had lifted, and she was right: Suilven stood at the far end of the glen exactly like a sugarloaf, or a thumb, or the last tooth in an old mouth. It made no sense to him. Everything around it was low and broken and wet, and there it was, two thousand feet of red stone sitting on top of nothing.

"How does that happen?" he said, before he could stop himself.

Morag smiled for the first time in days. "Now we're getting somewhere."

They walked to a place she called the Crag, a long escarpment above the road with a path cut into its face and a few weathered interpretation boards that she ignored completely. She led him past them, off the path and up a grassy gully, and stopped where the slope steepened into bare rock. She put her hand flat against it, the way you might steady a horse.

"Look at this," she said. "Not at me. At the rock."

He looked. Down low, near their feet, the stone was pale and gritty, layered in thin sheets, with faint tubes running through it like the marks of a pencil pushed into clay.

"Pipe rock," she said. "Those are burrows. Worms, or something like worms, digging in sand on the bottom of a shallow sea. Five hundred million years ago, give or take. That's the Cambrian. Some of the first animals that ever lived in any numbers, and they left their front doors behind." She moved her hand up, perhaps a metre, to where the rock changed. It was darker above, grey-green, and it had a strange, ground-down look, smeared and streaky, almost glossy in places. "And this is the Moine. Schists. Metamorphosed sediment."

"Right," Callum said.

"How old do you think it is?"

He shrugged. "Younger? It's on top."

"That's the rule, isn't it. Things settle in order. The bottom layer goes down first, then the next on top of it, and so on. You read a cliff like a book, oldest at the bottom." She tapped the darker rock. "This is older. Hundreds of millions of years older than the worms underneath it."

He stared at the line where the two met. It was sharp, almost polished, a seam you could have run a fingernail along.

"So it's upside down."

"Not upside down. Just in the wrong place. Something took a slab of very old rock, miles of it, and shoved it sideways and up and over the top of the young stuff. Pushed it west for fifty, sixty miles maybe. This line is where it slid." She pressed her thumb into the seam. "That's the Moine Thrust. You can follow it from here nearly to Skye."

"What pushed it?"

"Continents. An ocean closing. Two great pieces of the world coming together very, very slowly, about as fast as your fingernails grow, and something had to give." She took her hand away and wiped it on her trousers. "There was a war over this, you know. In the eighteen-hundreds. Gentlemen with beards writing furious letters to each other. The Director of the Geological Survey looked at this and said, plainly, the old rock is younger, because it's on top. He'd built a whole map of Scotland on that idea and he wasn't going to let a hillside in Sutherland tell him otherwise."

"Who was right?"

"The hillside, eventually. It took two men from the Survey walking every yard of this ground with their notebooks for years before anybody would listen. Peach and Horne. They're on a monument down the road. They worked it out the way you work anything out: by refusing to stop looking at what was actually in front of them."

---

They ate their sandwiches sitting on a flat boulder with Suilven floating in the distance. A pair of wheatears worked the scree below them, bobbing and flicking their white tails.

"You were a geologist," Callum said. It wasn't quite a question. He knew it the way you know family facts, as something that had been said at Christmases.

"I was a field mapper. Thirty-four years with the Survey, mostly in places like this. Before that I was a typist at the Survey, because that's what they'd let women be in 1963, and before *that* I was a girl from Dingwall who failed her Highers."

He turned to look at her.

"Failed," she said. "Proper failed. Maths and Chemistry both. My father didn't speak to me for a fortnight. I was meant to be a teacher. My sister was going to be a teacher, and her friends, and the girl down the road. And I sat in that exam hall and looked at the paper and something just—" she made a gesture with her hand, a flat sliding motion, "—went sideways. I couldn't make it mean anything. I walked out after forty minutes."

"Why?"

"I've had sixty years to think about it and I still don't entirely know. I was frightened. I didn't want the life it was going to give me, and I didn't have the words to say so, so my body said it for me." She broke off a corner of her sandwich and threw it toward the wheatears, who ignored it. "I took the typing job because it was in Edinburgh and it was far away. And then one day a man came back from the field with his notebooks soaked through, and they gave them to me to type up because nobody could read his hand, and I sat there with these drawings of folds and faults and dips and strikes, and I thought: oh. *Oh.* This is a way of reading that makes sense to me."

Callum didn't say anything for a while. The wind came over the ridge and pressed his jacket against his ribs.

"Mum told you," he said finally. "About why I left."

"Your mother told me you'd had a difficult year. The letter told me you'd failed your resits. You haven't told me anything."

"There isn't anything to tell."

"Fine."

"I just stopped going. That's it. That's the whole story. I didn't get ill, nothing happened, nobody died. I just woke up one morning in November and didn't go to the lecture and then I didn't go to the next one, and then it was easier not to go than to explain why I hadn't been, and then it was March." He was talking too fast, and he knew it, and he couldn't slow down. "Everyone keeps waiting for the reason. Mum keeps asking if something happened. The counsellor asked if something happened. And I want to say yes, so they'll stop looking at me like that, but nothing did. It was just—"

"Pressure," Morag said.

"I suppose."

"No, I mean it." She pointed back up at the seam in the rock. "That's what did that. Not an event. There was no moment where the mountain broke. Pressure, from a long way off, for a very long time, building up in a place where it couldn't go anywhere. And then one day the rock that had been holding still was simply not holding still anymore, and it slid, and it didn't stop sliding for millions of years." She lowered her hand. "Nobody looks at the Moine Thrust and asks what happened on the Tuesday."

He laughed, despite himself. It came out cracked.

"I'm not saying you're a mountain," she said. "You're a nineteen-year-old who should eat more vegetables. I'm saying I don't believe in the Tuesday either."

---

In the afternoon she took him further along the escarpment, to a place where the thrust plane was exposed in a little cove of rock, and she showed him how to see it properly. The smeared texture of the old rock was called mylonite, she said, from a Greek word for a mill, because it had been ground so finely under the weight of the slab passing over it. She showed him how the minerals were stretched into thin streaks, all pointing the same way.

"Lineations," she said. "They tell you the direction it moved. Look—they all run west-northwest. The rock's been dragged, and it remembers the direction of the drag. Every grain of it."

"It remembers," he repeated.

"Rocks are nothing but memory. That's all geology is. Asking the stone what it went through and having the patience to wait for the answer." She knelt, carefully, and pulled a hand lens on a cord from inside her jacket and gave it to him. "Go on. Close to it. Closer than you think."

He knelt beside her and pressed the lens to his eye and brought his face to within a couple of inches of the rock, and the world rearranged itself. The grey smear became a landscape: tiny glittering flakes of mica, little rounded grains of quartz pulled out like taffy, a garnet the size of a pinhead sitting in the middle of it all, dark red and perfect, with tails of stretched mineral trailing off either side of it like the wake of a boat.

"There's a—" He didn't know the word. "There's a red thing."

"Garnet. It grew when the rock was deep and hot, before it was pushed. Then the movement went past it and around it. See how the other grains flow round it? It stayed whole. It got carried sixty miles and it stayed whole."

He watched the garnet for a long time. Somewhere behind them a raven called, a deep wooden knock, twice.

"Why are you showing me this?" he said, still looking through the lens.

"Because I wanted to come back here once more before my knees stop letting me, and I didn't want to come alone." She paused. "And because I thought you might like it. That's allowed, you know. Doing something because you might like it. It doesn't have to be a lesson."

"It feels like a lesson."

"Well. I'm eighty-one. Everything I say sounds like a lesson. It's a side effect. Like the knees."

---

They walked back down to the road in the long gold light. Suilven had turned a deep rust colour, and its shadow ran out across the moor toward them for what seemed like miles.

"That one," Callum said. "You never told me how it happens. Sitting there on its own."

"Ah. That's the best one." She slowed, and he slowed with her. "Underneath all this, underneath everything you walked on today, there's Lewisian gneiss. Three billion years old. Some of the oldest rock in Europe. Lumpy, worn-down, like the stumps of hills. And on top of that, a long time later, rivers came down out of a mountain range that doesn't exist anymore and dumped sand and gravel, thousands of feet of it. That's the red stuff. Torridonian sandstone. It covered the whole country once. And then the ice came, again and again, and scraped almost all of it away. Except here and there. Except where it didn't."

"So Suilven's what's left."

"Suilven's what's left. Everything around it got carried off to the sea, and it stayed. Not because it was stronger, particularly. Just because of where it happened to be standing when the ice came through."

They walked on. After a while he said, "That's quite sad, actually."

"Is it?" She looked at the mountain. "I've always thought it was the opposite. Everything that was ever going to happen to it has happened, and it's still there. You can climb it. People get married on top of it, I'm told, the fools."

---

That night the signal on the step came and went as before, and Callum held the phone in his hand for a while without looking at it. Then he put it in his pocket.

Inside, Morag had laid her old field notebooks out on the table under the lamp. Six of them, green clothbound, the corners soft as cloth. She'd brought them all this way in the canvas pack, it turned out, along with the sandwiches.

"These were from here," she said. "1971 to '74. My first proper mapping. They gave me the worst sheet, all bog and midges, because nobody else wanted it."

He turned the pages. Her handwriting was small and upright and very young. There were sketches of crags with arrows on them, numbers scattered like seeds, *dip 15° ESE*, *strong lineation*, *mylonite, v. fine*, and in the margin of one page, in the same pencil, almost hidden: *Wet. Cold. Happy.*

"You could do this," she said. "If you wanted."

"I failed first year of a computing degree."

"I failed my Highers." She shrugged. "I'm not saying you should. I'm saying it's not closed. The old rock's on top of the young rock up there and the whole country argued about it for twenty years, and it was still true. Things aren't always in the order you were told they'd be in."

He kept turning the pages. Near the end of the last notebook he found a pressed flower, brown and flat, some tiny alpine thing, and a line in different ink, added later: *M.S. — remember this is why.*

"What's this?"

Morag took off her glasses and rubbed her eyes. "That," she said, "was for the days I forgot."

She went to bed early. Callum stayed up under the lamp, reading the notebooks from the beginning, slowly, the way she'd taught him to look at the rock. Not for the Tuesday. Just for what was there. Outside the window, at the end of the glen, Suilven held the last of the light long after everything around it had gone dark, the way some things do, simply because of where they happen to be standing.
