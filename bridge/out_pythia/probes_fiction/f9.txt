**The Woman Who Died on Tuesdays**

Fumiko Arai had died one hundred and fourteen times, and she remembered most of them.

She had been strangled in a ryokan in Hakone and drowned in a koi pond in Kamakura. She had been pushed down the stairs of a Shinjuku apartment building that did not exist, its stairwell built from plywood and painted to look like concrete. Twice she had been poisoned with tea, once with curry, and once, memorably, with a slice of castella cake that the props department had sprayed with a lacquer so foul she could still taste it when she thought about it too hard. She had been found in bathtubs, in closets, at the bottoms of ravines, slumped across steering wheels, and on one occasion folded into a trunk at the back of a rehearsal room while the crew argued for forty minutes about the lighting.

For twenty-two years she had been the most reliable corpse in Japanese television.

The drama was called *Inspector Kaga's Long Rain*, and it aired on Tuesday nights at nine. Every week Inspector Kaga, a melancholy man in a beige coat, arrived at the scene of a crime, looked down at the body with an expression of profound sorrow, and said, "Who were you, before this?" Then he spent fifty minutes finding out. The line had become famous. Schoolchildren said it to dead beetles. Comedians said it to empty beer cans. A politician had once said it in the Diet about a failed pension bill, and been forced to apologize.

Fumiko had been the answer to that question more often than anyone. She had been a widow, a pharmacist, a retired schoolteacher, a woman who ran a bathhouse, a fortune teller, the secret mother of a famous cellist. She had been, on the show's internal records, seventeen different grandmothers. The audience never seemed to notice that the same woman kept dying. Or perhaps they noticed and did not mind. Fumiko had a theory about this. She believed that people did not really look at the dead on television. They looked at the living looking at the dead. Her job was to be the thing that made Inspector Kaga's face do what it did.

She was good at her job.

---

On the morning of the final shoot, she arrived at the studio in Setagaya an hour early, as she always did. The security guard, a young man named Oda who had only been working there a year, waved her through without checking her pass.

"Arai-san. Last day."

"Last day," she agreed.

"Are you dying today?"

"I'm afraid so."

"How?"

"I don't know yet. They didn't send me the pages."

This was unusual. In twenty-two years, she had always received her pages a week in advance, even when her only stage direction was *she lies still*. She had a habit of reading the whole script anyway, so that she would know who she was, so that when Kaga asked his question she would have an answer somewhere in her body, even if no one would ever hear it.

Oda frowned. "That's strange."

"It's the last episode," Fumiko said. "Everyone is being secretive."

She went down the long corridor toward Stage Four, past the framed posters of the show's early seasons. In the first one, Kaga's actor, Tetsuya Mori, had been thirty-eight and lean, with black hair and a jaw like a closed door. Now he was sixty and his hair was white, and the network had decided, after many meetings, that the inspector would finally retire. The final episode would be two hours long. There had been articles about it in every newspaper.

Fumiko had read the articles. None of them had mentioned her.

---

The makeup room smelled of powder and coffee. Kayo, who had done Fumiko's death pallor for the last nine years, was already there, setting out brushes.

"You're early," Kayo said.

"I'm always early."

"I know. I meant it as a compliment." Kayo hesitated. "Did they talk to you?"

"Who?"

"Ishida-san. The director."

"No. I haven't seen the pages."

Kayo opened her mouth, then closed it, then picked up a sponge and began to dab at Fumiko's forehead with unusual concentration.

"Kayo-chan."

"I'm not supposed to say anything."

"Am I being killed in a terrible way? Is it the trunk again?"

"It's not the trunk."

"Is it a bathtub? I'm too old for the bathtub. The water is never warm."

"It's not a bathtub." Kayo put the sponge down. Her eyes were wet, which was alarming, because Kayo was not a sentimental person. She had once applied a fake bullet wound to a child actor's forehead while discussing her tax return. "Just wait for Ishida-san. Please."

So Fumiko waited, and Kayo worked in silence, and when it was finished Fumiko looked in the mirror and saw that she had not been made pale at all. Kayo had given her color in her cheeks. A little on the lips. She looked like a woman of sixty-eight who had slept well.

"This is wrong," Fumiko said. "I look alive."

"Yes," said Kayo. "I know."

---

Ishida found her in the corridor ten minutes later. He was the third director the show had employed and the youngest, only forty-one, with a habit of wearing the same grey sweater every day so that he did not have to make decisions about clothing. He was holding a script, and he held it out to her as though it were something fragile.

"Arai-san. I'm sorry I didn't send this. I wanted to give it to you myself."

She took it. The cover said *Final Episode: The Last Question*. She opened it to the page he had marked with a yellow tab.

The scene took place in a small apartment in Nerima. Inspector Kaga, on his last day before retirement, is called to the scene of a death. An old woman has been found by a neighbor. The police assume it is a murder. Kaga kneels beside the body and asks his question.

*Who were you, before this?*

And then, under that, a stage direction she had never seen in twenty-two years of scripts:

*The woman opens her eyes.*

Fumiko read it twice. Then she read the dialogue that followed, which was long. Pages long.

"I don't understand," she said.

"She isn't dead," said Ishida. "She was never dead. She fell asleep. The neighbor panicked. It's the only case in the show's history where there's no murder at all. And Kaga, who has spent twenty-two years asking the dead who they were, finally gets to ask someone who can answer."

"And the answer is me."

"The answer is you."

She looked at the pages again. The woman's name was Hanae. She was a widow. She had been a seamstress. She had a son who lived in Sapporo and called every Sunday. She had once, as a girl, wanted to be an actress.

"You wrote this," Fumiko said.

"Mori-san and I wrote it together. He asked for you." Ishida rubbed the back of his neck. "He said—he said you'd been answering his question for twenty years and nobody had ever let you speak."

Fumiko did not know what to do with her hands. She held the script against her chest.

"I haven't spoken a line on camera since 1991," she said.

"I know. I looked it up. It was a shampoo commercial."

"It was a conditioner commercial."

"I'm sorry. Conditioner."

"I said, *Silky, isn't it?*" She laughed, a strange small sound. "That was my whole career, before. *Silky, isn't it?* And then I got older and they stopped calling, and then someone at this show needed a woman to lie in a garden and not move, and I was very good at not moving."

"You were the best at it," Ishida said. "Everybody says so. The lighting crew used to time you. They said you could go eleven minutes without blinking."

"Twelve," said Fumiko. "Once. In Kamakura."

"We start at two," Ishida said gently. "Take the morning. Read it. If you can't do it, we'll—" He stopped. "No. I don't have a backup. You're the only one."

---

She read the pages in the empty canteen, at a table by the window where the light came through the smeared glass in long strips.

Hanae's monologue came near the end of the scene. Kaga, kneeling on the floor beside her, apologizes for frightening her. She sits up slowly and says that she isn't frightened. She says she has been waiting a long time for someone to ask her a question that wasn't *Are you all right?* or *Have you eaten?* or *Do you need anything?* She says that when you get old people stop asking who you were and start asking only how you are, and those are not the same question, and the second one is lonelier than it sounds.

Then she tells him. She tells him about the sewing shop in Asakusa and the man who came in every Thursday with a torn button so that he would have a reason to talk to her. She tells him about the son, and about a daughter who died very young, which nobody in the building knows. She tells him that when she was nineteen she auditioned for a theater company in Shimokitazawa and the director told her she had a face that was easy to forget.

*I thought it was an insult,* Hanae says. *I cried on the train all the way home. But now I think he meant it kindly. He meant that people could look at me and see themselves. That's a kind of face, too.*

Fumiko put the script down on the table.

No one had ever said that to her in an audition. But someone had said something close. In 1979, at a small company in Shimokitazawa, a man with a cigarette and a red scarf had told her she had *a quiet face*. She had cried on the train.

She had never told anyone that. She was nearly certain she had never told anyone.

---

Tetsuya Mori found her in the canteen at noon. In person he was smaller than Kaga, and less sad. He was eating a rice ball out of its plastic wrapper and had a grain of rice on his chin.

"Did you read it?" he said.

"How did you know about Shimokitazawa?"

He sat down across from her and looked guilty. "You told me."

"I did not."

"You did. 2009. We were shooting the episode at the onsen. You were the innkeeper who'd been hit with the ice bucket. We were stuck in a hallway for two hours because the generator broke, and you were wearing that wet kimono, and you were shivering, and I gave you my coat. And we talked. You told me about the theater company and the man in the scarf."

Fumiko tried to remember. She remembered the onsen. She remembered the cold. She remembered the coat, vaguely—the beige coat, Kaga's coat, heavy and smelling of cigarettes that Mori did not actually smoke but that the costume department sprayed onto the fabric so it would seem lived in.

"I don't remember telling you," she said.

"You were half-frozen. I'm not surprised." He put the rice ball down. "I remember everything you've told me, Arai-san. Over the years. In hallways, in vans, waiting for the rain machine to be fixed. You told me about your husband. You told me about the conditioner commercial. You told me your mother used to make you practice walking with a book on your head. I've been collecting it."

"Why?"

He looked at her for a long time. When he spoke again his voice dropped, and for a moment it was Kaga's voice, low and careful.

"Because every Tuesday for twenty-two years I've knelt down beside you and asked who you were. And every time, I was actually asking you. Not the character. You. It was the only way I could make my face do the thing. I'd look at you lying there and I'd think, *Who is this woman, really? What did she want? What did she give up?* And I knew a little, because you'd told me in the hallways. That's what people saw on screen. That wasn't acting. That was me wondering about you."

Fumiko felt something rise in her chest that she had not felt in a very long time, possibly not since a train ride in 1979.

"That's a strange thing to say to someone," she said.

"I know. I've been working up to it for about fifteen years."

"You could have just asked me to dinner."

"I'm married," Mori said. "And so were you, for most of it. And anyway, dinner wouldn't have been the same. I wanted to ask in the place where I'd been asking all along."

---

They shot the scene at two-forty, after a delay with the lights.

The set was a small apartment, very ordinary, with a low table and a calendar from a local bank and a sewing machine under a dust cover by the window. Fumiko lay on the tatami beside the table with her eyes closed. She heard the crew settle. She heard the particular silence of a studio before the camera turns, the silence of forty people holding their breath together.

"Rolling," someone said.

She did what she had always done. She made herself still. She slowed her breath until her chest barely moved. She let her hands go loose. She went down into the place where she had always gone, the dark warm place where she was nobody, a body, a question waiting for someone else to answer it.

She heard Mori's footsteps. Heard his knees creak as he knelt—they had begun creaking sometime around the fifteenth season, and the sound team had stopped trying to remove it. She felt the small shift of air as he leaned over her.

"Who were you," Inspector Kaga said, "before this?"

And for the first time in one hundred and fifteen deaths, Fumiko Arai opened her eyes.

The light was very bright. She had forgotten how bright it was from this angle. She had spent twenty-two years with her eyes closed while the lights were on. Mori's face was above her, white-haired, close, and his expression was not the expression she had imagined all these years from the inside of her closed eyelids. It was not sorrow. It was something gentler and more surprised, the face of a man who had knocked on a door for a very long time and had finally heard someone on the other side say *yes?*

"Oh," Hanae said. "You startled me."

Kaga sat back on his heels. "I'm sorry. The neighbor thought you were—"

"Dead. Yes. Mrs. Watanabe thinks everyone is dead. She called an ambulance for her cat last spring." Fumiko pushed herself up on one elbow. Her voice sounded strange to her, too loud, but she kept going. "You asked me a question."

"I did."

"Nobody's asked me that in years."

"Would you answer it?"

So she answered it. She told him about the sewing shop and the man with the torn button. She told him about the son in Sapporo and the daughter nobody knew about. She told him about the audition in Shimokitazawa and the man with the scarf, and when she got to the line—*I thought it was an insult*—her voice broke on its own, without her deciding, and she let it.

"But now I think he meant it kindly," she said. "He meant that people could look at me and see themselves. That's a kind of face, too."

There was supposed to be a line after that, for Kaga. Something about how he had spent his whole career looking at faces like hers. But Mori didn't say it. He just looked at her, and she looked back, and the silence went on far longer than the script allowed, long enough that she could hear the hum of the lights and someone shifting their weight behind the camera.

Then Mori said, not in Kaga's voice but in his own, "Thank you for telling me."

And Ishida, very quietly, said, "Cut."

Nobody moved for a moment. Then Kayo, standing by the monitors with a powder brush in her hand, started to clap, and then the lighting crew, and then everyone. Fumiko sat on the tatami of an apartment that did not exist and listened to forty people applaud a woman who had finally been allowed to stay alive.

---

The episode aired on a Tuesday in March. It was the highest-rated broadcast of the year.

The next morning there were articles. Some of them were about Mori's retirement and some were about the clever twist and a few were about the old actress who had played the woman on the floor. One reporter called her *the face Japan never noticed it knew*. Another dug up the conditioner commercial and posted it online, and for a week young people on the internet said *Silky, isn't it?* to each other for reasons Fumiko did not entirely understand.

She was offered three roles in the following month. In all three, her character lived to the end.

She took the smallest one, a grandmother in a family drama who had six lines and a scene where she taught her granddaughter to sew a button. On the first day of shooting, a young assistant director, nervous and polite, asked whether she needed anything.

"No, thank you," said Fumiko.

"Are you comfortable? Have you eaten?"

"I'm fine."

The assistant director nodded and turned to go, and then turned back, and seemed to struggle with something.

"I'm sorry," she said. "I saw the Kaga finale. I just wondered—who were you? Before?"

Fumiko smiled. She patted the chair beside her.

"Sit down," she said. "It's a long answer. We have until they fix the lights."
