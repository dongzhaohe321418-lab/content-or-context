**The Plaque**

The museum wanted four hundred words. Nora had the email printed and folded in her jacket pocket, and she took it out on the pier while she waited for the man to arrive, as if the number might have changed since morning. *Four hundred words, accessible to a general audience, suitable for a bronze plaque at the head of the brow. Please cover commissioning through decommissioning.*

Behind her, the destroyer sat low and gray against the river. Someone had painted her recently, a fresh haze gray that looked almost blue in the October light, and the hull number on her bow stood out in white so clean it seemed fake. USS *Abner Kell*. Fletcher class, more or less, though the volunteers who ran her would correct you on the "less." Commissioned 1943. Three wars, if you counted the way old sailors counted. Seventy-odd years of being a ship, and then forty more of being a memory of one.

Nora had written plaques before. She had written the one for the covered bridge in Halsey County and the one for the textile mill that was now apartments, and she knew the shape of the thing: a date, a name, a sentence about sacrifice, a sentence about community. She was good at it. She didn't love it, but she was good at it.

The man was late. When he came, he came slowly, on two canes, and a younger man walked beside him with one hand hovering near his elbow, not quite touching.

"You're the writer," the old man said. It wasn't a question.

"Nora Bettencourt." She held out her hand, then realized he had no hand free, and lowered it. "You must be Mr. Ostrowski."

"Walt." He looked past her at the ship. He looked for a long time. The younger man, a grandson she guessed, glanced at Nora with an expression that said *give him a minute,* and she did.

"They painted her," Walt said finally.

"In the spring, I think. The volunteers did it."

"She never looked like that. Not once." He shook his head, and she couldn't tell if he was pleased or offended. "Rust streaks from the hawsepipes all the way down. Chief Dunleavy used to say we were the only ship in the fleet that came pre-weathered."

---

They didn't go aboard right away. There was a bench on the pier, and the grandson, whose name was Danny, helped Walt lower himself onto it, and Nora sat at the other end with her notebook on her knee.

"The museum gave me the official history," she said. "The dictionary entry, the deck logs they have, the cruise books. I've got the dates. What I don't have is—"

"The ship," Walt said.

"I was going to say *color*."

He laughed, a short dry sound. "Color. All right." He thought about it. "You know what she smelled like?"

"No."

"Fuel oil and coffee and wet wool. And something underneath it, something metal, like a penny in your mouth. You'd come back from liberty and you'd smell her before you saw her. Danny, what's that word. When you know a place by the smell."

"I don't know, Grandpa."

"Well, there's a word." He tapped one cane against the planks. "Put that in your plaque."

Nora wrote *smell — fuel oil, coffee, wet wool, penny* and underlined nothing.

"When did you report aboard?"

"Nineteen sixty-six. January. I was nineteen and I'd never seen the ocean. Grew up in Hamtramck. The biggest water I'd seen was the Detroit River, and I thought that was something." He turned to her. "You know she'd already been through a war by then? The big one. She was older than me. That was the thing that got me, first week. I'm walking around and there's this patch welded on the bulkhead in the after mess, and I ask a guy what it is, and he says, that's where the kamikaze came in. Off Okinawa. Nineteen forty-five. And I put my hand on it." He held up his right hand, spotted and knuckled, and looked at it as though it might still be warm. "Twenty-one years before, some kid my age from some town in Japan flew his plane into that exact spot. And some other kid from some town in Ohio, or wherever, was standing there. And now there's me."

Nora had the Okinawa date in her notes. *Struck by a suicide aircraft, 4 May 1945; 11 killed, 23 wounded.* One line in the official history. She'd planned to give it a sentence on the plaque.

"Did you know any of the names?" she asked. "From forty-five?"

"There was a list. Somebody'd typed it up and framed it, and it hung in the passageway outside the wardroom. Eleven names. I used to read them on mid-watch when I couldn't sleep. Kowalczyk was one. Polish kid, like me. I always said his name out loud when I passed, just quiet. I don't know why. Felt like somebody ought to."

"Is the list still aboard?"

"I don't know. That's what I want to find out."

---

The brow was steep and Danny went first, backwards, holding both of Walt's canes while Walt gripped the rails. It took a long time. A volunteer in a ball cap that said *ABNER KELL — PROUD TIN CAN* came hurrying across the quarterdeck and then stopped, uncertain, and Nora watched him decide not to help, which she thought was the right decision.

At the top, Walt let go of one rail and turned aft, toward where a flag would have flown, and raised his hand to his brow. It wasn't crisp. His arm shook. He held it anyway.

"Request permission to come aboard," he said, to nobody, to the ship.

The volunteer, after a second, said, "Permission granted, sir." His voice cracked a little on *sir*.

---

They went slowly through the main deck spaces. The museum had done what museums do: there were placards on easels, a mannequin in dungarees at the forward five-inch mount, a case of cruise books under plexiglass. Walt ignored most of it. He walked with his head turned slightly, like a man listening for a sound in another room.

"Here," he said, outside a hatch. "This was my rack. Not here, down there. Second level, outboard. Top rack. You had about eighteen inches between your nose and the overhead, and there was a pipe right above me that sweated. Every night, drip, drip. I put a sock on it."

"What were you? Your rate, I mean."

"Radarman. RD3 when I got out. CIC. Combat Information Center. Dark room full of green screens and guys smoking." He smiled. "You could smoke anywhere then. The air in CIC, you could cut it."

"What was it like? Off Vietnam?"

He was quiet for a while. They had stopped near the rail, and he leaned on it and looked at the river, at a tug pushing a gravel barge upstream.

"People think it was all action," he said. "Some of it was. We did gunfire support, sixty-seven, up near the DMZ. They'd call in and we'd shoot. You never saw what you hit. You'd hear the mount go, boom, boom, and the whole ship would shudder, and you'd feel it in your teeth, and then nothing. A spotter on the radio saying *good effect* or *add two hundred*. That's all. You never saw it."

"Did that bother you?"

"Then? No. Later." He didn't elaborate, and she didn't ask. "But mostly it was plane guard. You know what that is?"

"No."

"Carrier's launching and recovering aircraft, you steam behind her, a couple thousand yards back, in case one goes in the water. You're there to pick up the pilot. Hours and hours of that. Following that big gray thing around in circles in the Tonkin Gulf. Hot as hell. We'd be on station so long the cooks ran out of everything but powdered eggs and baloney. Baloney three times a day. A guy named Fishburne made up a song about it. I don't remember how it went." He frowned. "I wish I did."

"Did you ever pick anyone up?"

"Once." He was still looking at the river. "August of sixty-seven. An A-4 came off the cat and just—didn't. Lost power, I guess. Went in right off the bow. We were there in, oh, four minutes. Maybe less. Whaleboat went over and they found him. He'd gotten out." He nodded slowly. "He'd gotten out. Twenty-four years old, a lieutenant j.g., and he came up over the side with his flight suit pouring water and he was laughing. Laughing his head off. Shock, I suppose. And the captain came down from the bridge to shake his hand and the pilot hugged him instead. Just grabbed him. Our captain was a very formal man, Commander Thibodeaux, Louisiana, said maybe ten words a day, and this pilot had him in a bear hug and was dripping all over his khakis, and the captain just stood there and patted his back. Like a dad."

Nora was writing fast. "Do you know the pilot's name?"

"Somebody did. I didn't." He shrugged. "We sent him back by highline the next day. We got a sheet cake from the carrier. That was the trade. One pilot, one sheet cake. Best cake I ever ate in my life."

---

Below decks it was close and dim, even with the museum's lights. The ladders were nearly vertical, and Walt stopped at the top of the first one.

"I can't," he said.

"We can stay up here, Grandpa."

"No." He looked down the ladder, at the bright worn edges of the treads. "No, I want to see if it's there. The list." He turned to Nora. "Would you go? You and Danny. I'll wait. Tell me what you see."

So she went down, backwards, the way Danny showed her, with the rails cold in her palms, and found herself in a narrow passageway that smelled—faintly, impossibly—of fuel oil. Not coffee. Not wool. But under the fresh paint and the museum dust, something old and metallic, like a penny in the mouth.

They found the wardroom. Danny pointed out a frame on the bulkhead outside it, and for a moment her heart lifted. But it was a photograph: the ship's softball team, 1971, a dozen men in matching T-shirts squinting in the sun. Next to it was a brass plate listing commanding officers. *CDR R. J. Thibodeaux, 1966–1968.* She took a picture of it.

There was no list of eleven names.

They asked a volunteer, an older woman with reading glasses on a chain who was dusting the wardroom table. She thought about it.

"There's a memorial," she said. "On the fantail. They put it up in the nineties when the ship came here. All the names, from all the wars. It's a big bronze thing. Very nice."

"But not the original? A typed list, framed?"

"Oh, honey." She shook her head. "Everything loose got stripped before they towed her. People took souvenirs. Shipyard workers. Some of it ended up in antique shops. I've seen ship's clocks from this class go on eBay for a fortune." She looked genuinely sorry. "It might be anywhere."

---

When they came back up, Walt was sitting on a coil of line someone had flaked down for display, both canes across his knees. He looked at their faces and knew.

"Gone," he said.

"There's a memorial on the fantail," Nora said. "With all the names."

"Bronze," Danny added.

"Bronze," Walt repeated. He didn't say anything else for a while. Then he said, "Help me up," and they did, and the three of them walked aft along the main deck, past the torpedo tubes, past the after mounts, in the slanting light.

The memorial was where the volunteer said it would be, a broad bronze tablet set into a granite base on the fantail. It had a ship's silhouette at the top and an eagle and, underneath, columns of raised names, divided by conflict. *World War II. Korea. Vietnam.* At the bottom, a line about the ultimate sacrifice.

Walt found Okinawa. He ran his finger down the names. Kowalczyk was there.

"Edward Kowalczyk," he said out loud, quietly. "Seaman First." Then he read the other ten, one after another, his finger moving slowly over the cold letters, his voice barely louder than the wind off the river. Nora found she was holding her breath.

When he finished he stood there for a moment. Then he moved his finger down to the Vietnam section. There were two names.

"Carl Reese," he said. "Lonnie Watts." He looked at Nora. "You won't find them in the history. Not like Okinawa. They weren't killed by anything you'd write about. October of sixty-seven we had a fire in the after fireroom. Fuel line. Middle of the night. They got everyone out but those two. Carl was from Tennessee and he played the harmonica so bad you wanted to throw him over the side. Lonnie was going to marry a girl in San Diego. He had her picture taped up in the head where everybody could see it, and if you said anything about her he'd fight you." He laughed and it turned into something else and he waited for it to pass. "One line. 'Engineering casualty, two killed.' That's what it says. I looked it up once at the library."

"I can put their names on the plaque," Nora said. She didn't know if she could. She didn't know what the museum would say.

"It's four hundred words," Danny said, gently. "Grandpa told me. You can't fit everybody."

"No," Walt agreed. "You can't."

He turned away from the memorial and looked down at the wake that wasn't there, at the flat brown river where the screws would have churned it white.

"You know what the history doesn't have?" he said. "Any of it. The cake. Fishburne's song. The sock on the pipe. Thibodeaux patting that pilot on the back. Carl's harmonica. You can't put that in bronze. It doesn't go." He shrugged one shoulder. "So write the dates. That's fine. The dates are true. Just know that's not the ship."

"What is the ship?"

He considered the question as though it deserved an honest answer, which she supposed it did.

"Guys," he said finally. "A lot of guys, one after another, for a long time. Most of them dead now. Me soon. That's all a ship ever is. You just keep pouring men into the same steel and the steel stays and the men go."

---

That night Nora sat at her kitchen table with the official history on one side and her notebook on the other and the email on top, folded and refolded until it was soft.

She wrote the plaque. It took her until two in the morning. She put in the commissioning date and the shipyard. She put in the Philippine Sea and Okinawa and the kamikaze and the eleven dead. She put in Korea, which Walt hadn't seen, and the Tonkin Gulf, which he had, and the decommissioning in 1974 and the long tow upriver and the volunteers with their paint. She put in the word *sacrifice* once, because the museum would want it, and the word *community* once, because she'd promised herself she'd stop using it and then couldn't think of anything better.

It came to four hundred and twelve words. She cut twelve.

Then she opened a new document and kept going. She wrote about the patch on the bulkhead in the after mess and a nineteen-year-old from Hamtramck putting his hand on it. She wrote about baloney three times a day and a song nobody could remember. She wrote about a pilot laughing in a wet flight suit and a man from Louisiana who said ten words a day standing very still while he was hugged. She wrote about a fire at night. She wrote down Carl Reese's harmonica and Lonnie Watts's girl in San Diego, whose name Walt didn't know, and the sock on the pipe that sweated above the top rack, second level, outboard.

She didn't know what it was for. It wasn't for bronze. Maybe she would print it and mail it to Walt, so he would know somebody had written it down. Maybe Danny would keep it after. Maybe it would end up in a box in somebody's attic, and someday somebody would find it the way you find a ship's clock in an antique shop, and wonder whose it was.

At four in the morning she read it over once, out loud, quietly, the way you might read names in a passageway on the mid-watch when you couldn't sleep. It felt like somebody ought to.

Then she saved it, and turned off the light, and in the dark she could almost smell it: fuel oil, coffee, wet wool, and something underneath, metal, like a penny in the mouth.
