# The Last Over at Hollins Lane

The roller lived in a shed behind the pavilion, and on the morning of his last match Teddy Marsh went to see it before he went to see anyone else.

It was a heavy old thing, green paint flaking off the drum in long curls, the handle worn silver where a hundred years of hands had pushed it. Nobody used it any more. The club had a motorised one now, bought with lottery money in 2009, which the groundsman rode up and down the square like a man mowing his own lawn on a Sunday. But the old roller stayed in the shed, and Teddy liked to put his hand on it before a game. He'd never told anyone why. He wasn't sure he knew.

"You're early," said Pauline from the tea hut. She was unwrapping clingfilm from a tray of egg sandwiches, and she didn't look up.

"Couldn't sleep."

"Nerves?"

"At my age?"

"Especially at your age." She looked up then. "Thirty-nine years, Teddy. You're allowed nerves."

He was forty-four. He'd played his first game for Hollins Lane at fifteen, a gangly boy in borrowed whites that flapped at the ankles, and he'd taken a wicket with his third ball: a long hop that the batsman, a builder called Frank Moss, had smashed straight to square leg. Frank had walked off laughing. Teddy had stood in the middle of the pitch not knowing what to do with his hands.

"Is the boy coming?" Pauline asked.

"Said he would."

"Well. That's something."

---

The boy was his son, Callum, who was nineteen and lived in Leeds and studied something to do with computers that Teddy had stopped pretending to understand. They had not spoken properly since Easter, when Callum had come home for four days and spent three of them in his room, and on the fourth had said, over toast, that he wasn't sure he was coming back for the summer. He'd got a job. A placement. He'd said it like an apology and Teddy had heard it like an accusation, and somewhere in between those two things there'd been a silence that neither of them had found a way to fill.

Teddy had texted him in June. *Last game is the 14th of September. Against Brierfield. Would be good to see you.*

Three days later: *I'll try.*

Teddy had read that message more times than he'd read anything in his life.

---

The thing about a cricket career, if you could call it that, was that nobody watched it. Not really. Teddy had played seven seasons of Minor Counties cricket in his twenties, for Lancashire's second-string side and then for Cumberland, and there had been a summer, 2004, when a man from the county had come to watch him three games running and stood behind the bowler's arm with his hands in his pockets. Teddy had taken eleven wickets in those three games. He'd bowled the best he had ever bowled, before or since, a heavy ball that hit the seam and jagged away from the right-hander late, and he'd seen batsmen look down at their pads afterwards as though the ball had done something rude.

The man from the county had shaken his hand after the third game and said, "Good lad. Keep at it." And Teddy had waited for the phone call, and the phone call hadn't come, and by the following spring his knee had started clicking on the landing stride, and that was more or less that.

He'd come back to Hollins Lane. He'd married Joanne, whose father did the scoring. He'd got a job at the tile warehouse on the ring road and then a better one as the warehouse manager, and he'd played every Saturday from April to September for twenty years, opening the bowling until he was thirty-six, then first change, then whatever the captain needed. He'd taken, according to Joanne's father's ledgers, 1,412 wickets for the club. It was a club record. There was a board in the pavilion that said so, in gold letters that had been added in 2019 and already looked slightly crooked.

Joanne had died in 2021. It was quick, which people kept saying as though it helped.

---

By half-twelve the car park was filling up. More people than usual: men he'd played with in the nineties, gone grey and soft, standing with pints by the boundary rope. Old Frank Moss in a wheelchair, eighty-one now, being pushed by a granddaughter. Somebody had put bunting up along the pavilion rail. Teddy went into the changing room and found that the lads had hung a banner over his peg — TEDDY 1,412 — NOT OUT — which didn't make sense, bowling-wise, but he understood the thought.

"Speech later," said Asif, the captain, who was twenty-eight and played like he was angry at the ball. "Don't worry. Short one."

"I'm not making a speech."

"You're making a speech."

Teddy changed slowly. Left boot first, always. The knee strapping, which took longer every year. He checked his phone, which he never did before a game, and there was nothing, and he put it face down in his bag and felt foolish.

Brierfield won the toss and chose to bat.

---

He opened the bowling. Asif insisted. "Your day," he said, and threw him the new ball, and Teddy caught it with both hands like a schoolboy, and the crowd — it was a crowd, today, a hundred and fifty maybe — gave a cheer that made his face go hot.

He marked out his run. Eleven paces, these days. It had been eighteen once.

The first ball was a wide down the leg side. The keeper dived for it and missed and it went for four byes, and somebody on the boundary laughed, not unkindly, and Teddy laughed too, standing at the end of his run with his hands on his hips. All right, he thought. All right. That's the nerves gone, then.

The second ball he pitched up, and it swung a little, and the batsman — a kid of maybe seventeen, all elbows and expensive kit — left it alone.

He bowled six overs on the trot and took nothing. He beat the bat twice. He bowled a maiden. His knee held. On the boundary, between overs, he looked toward the car park and then made himself stop looking.

---

At tea, Brierfield were 141 for 4. Pauline's egg sandwiches went quickly. Teddy sat at the end of the long table with a cup of tea and a slice of Victoria sponge he didn't want, and old Frank Moss's granddaughter wheeled Frank over to him.

"Edward," said Frank. He'd always called him Edward. Nobody else did.

"Frank."

"Third ball. Long hop. I've never forgiven you."

"You hit it to square leg, Frank. Bit of a stretch to blame me."

"I was set." Frank's eyes were watery and very bright. "Forty-odd. I was set and you were a child and you bowled me the worst ball I'd ever seen and I got out to it. You know what I learned from that?"

"Go on."

"Nothing. Not a thing. I did it again three weeks later off some lad from Colne." He laughed, and it turned into a cough, and the granddaughter put a hand on his shoulder. When he'd recovered, he said, "Your Joanne would've loved today."

"She'd have hated it. All this fuss."

"She'd have hated it and loved it," Frank said. "That's what women do with us. Didn't you know?"

Teddy didn't answer. He looked at his tea.

"Is your lad here?" Frank asked.

"Not yet."

Frank nodded slowly, the way old men nod when they've heard more than you've said.

---

After tea, Teddy fielded at mid-on and watched the game drift. Brierfield's number five, a heavyset man who batted like he was chopping logs, got going, and the score climbed to 190, to 210. Asif rotated the bowlers and swore under his breath. The sun moved behind the poplar trees on the far side and long shadows came out across the outfield, the way they did in September, as though the whole ground were slowly being drawn into evening.

He thought about the man from the county. He thought about him more than he'd admit. Not with bitterness, exactly — he'd got past bitterness somewhere in his thirties — but with a kind of curiosity. What had the man seen? What had he written down in whatever notebook such men carried? *Good engine, limited pace, knee a concern.* Or nothing at all, perhaps. Perhaps he'd forgotten Teddy by the time he reached his car.

That was the thing nobody told you. You could give your whole life to a game and the game would never know your name. It went on. It had gone on for two hundred years before you and it would go on two hundred after. You were a small weather system passing over it.

And yet. And yet there was a ledger in the pavilion with 1,412 tally marks in Joanne's father's careful hand. And there was a builder from 1997 in a wheelchair who still remembered a long hop.

The ball came to him, a firm drive along the ground, and he bent to stop it and his knee told him something unprintable, and he threw it back to the keeper and walked in with the bowler as though nothing had happened.

---

At 228 for 7, with four overs left, Asif threw him the ball again.

"Finish it," he said.

"They're not all out, Asif."

"Then get them all out." Asif grinned. "Go on. Last over of your life. Make it a good one."

Teddy walked back to his mark. Eleven paces. He scuffed the turf with his boot, the way he always did, and turned, and that was when he saw the car.

It was a small blue hatchback he didn't recognise, pulling into the far end of the car park where the gravel turned to mud. The door opened. A tall figure got out, hood up against nothing in particular, and stood for a moment by the car without moving, and then began walking toward the boundary rope.

Teddy knew the walk. He'd have known it from a mile off. He'd taught it, more or less — a slight drop of the left shoulder, a habit Callum had picked up at six years old from watching his father come in off the field.

The umpire said, "When you're ready, Teddy."

He wasn't ready. He was not remotely ready. He stood at the top of his mark with the ball in his hand and his heart doing something it hadn't done since 2004, and he thought, absurdly, of the old roller in the shed, its silver handle, all the hands.

He ran in.

---

The first ball was full and straight and the log-chopper blocked it.

The second ball he dropped a fraction short, and it hit the seam and moved away, and the batsman pushed at it and missed, and the keeper whooped.

The third ball the man clubbed through midwicket for four. The crowd groaned. Teddy didn't look at the boundary. He didn't look at where Callum might be standing.

The fourth ball was the one. He'd know it later, replaying it in the dark of his bedroom, the way he'd replayed perhaps a dozen balls across his life. He came in off his eleven paces and his knee held and he got his front foot down hard and his arm came over high, higher than it had in years, and he let go of it with his fingers down the back of the seam and felt it leave his hand clean, the way the good ones did. It pitched on a length outside off stump. It hit the seam. And it came back — not away, the way his stock ball went, but back in, the other way, sharp, an off-cutter he'd never really been able to bowl on purpose — and it went between the log-chopper's bat and pad and took out middle stump.

The noise was enormous. It was out of all proportion to the moment. It was Saturday afternoon league cricket on a ground with a corrugated-iron pavilion and a tea hut, and a hundred and fifty people roared like it was the last day of a Test. Asif sprinted from slip and jumped on him, and then the keeper, and then the rest of them, and for a moment Teddy was underneath a pile of young men who smelled of sweat and suncream, and he was laughing, and his knee was screaming, and his eyes were stinging, and he didn't care about any of it.

1,413.

When they pulled him up, he looked for Callum.

---

His son was standing by the sightscreen, hood down now. He wasn't cheering. He was just standing there with his hands in his jacket pockets, watching, and when Teddy found him across the field, Callum lifted one hand from his pocket, a half-wave, almost shy.

Teddy lifted his own hand back.

He bowled the last two balls of the over. One went for a single. The other the new batsman left alone. He couldn't have told you anything about either of them.

---

Hollins Lane chased down 233 with three overs to spare. Asif made 84 and was furious with himself for getting out. Teddy didn't bat. There was a speech, in the end, on the pavilion steps — short, as promised, mostly about Joanne's father's handwriting and Pauline's sandwiches and the old roller in the shed, which made people laugh because they didn't know it meant anything. Frank Moss called out "Long hop!" from his wheelchair at an inopportune moment, and everyone laughed again.

Afterwards, when the bar was loud and the light was going, Teddy went out through the side door and found Callum sitting on the bench by the scoreboard, where the numbers still hung on their nails: 233 for 4. Teddy sat down beside him. The bench creaked. For a while neither of them said anything.

"I nearly didn't come," Callum said eventually.

"I know."

"I sat in the car for twenty minutes at the services. On the M65." He picked at a thread on his jacket. "I didn't know if you'd want me to. After Easter."

"I texted you."

"You text everyone. You texted the window cleaner happy birthday."

Teddy laughed despite himself. "He's a good window cleaner."

Callum smiled, briefly, and then the smile went. "I didn't know how to be here," he said. "Without Mum. It's so — everything here is her. The house, the club. Grandad's books. I come home and it's like there's a hole in the middle of everything and you just keep — bowling. Every Saturday. Like nothing happened."

Teddy looked out at the darkening field. The groundsman was already out with the motorised roller, puttering up and down the square.

"I kept bowling," he said slowly, "because I didn't know what else to do with my hands."

Callum turned to look at him.

"That's all it was. I'm not — I'm not good at this. Talking. Your mum did the talking. I did the bowling." He stopped. Started again. "But I should've asked you. How it was for you. I should've come up to Leeds. I kept thinking you didn't want me to."

"I didn't know what I wanted."

"No. Me neither."

The motorised roller reached the far end of the square, turned, came back.

"That last wicket," Callum said. "Was that on purpose? The one that came back in?"

Teddy thought about lying. He thought about telling his son that he'd planned it, set the batsman up, worked him out over three balls like a chess player. It would've been a good story.

"No," he said. "No idea where that came from. Never bowled one like it in my life."

Callum laughed, properly this time, his head going back. It was Joanne's laugh. It had always been Joanne's laugh.

"Thirty years," he said. "And you finally bowled a good one by accident."

"Twenty-nine. And I bowled plenty of good ones."

"Name one."

Teddy opened his mouth, and then closed it, and then started to tell him about a summer in 2004, and a man in a car park with his hands in his pockets, and eleven wickets in three games — and Callum listened, leaning forward on the bench with his elbows on his knees, the way Teddy had once listened to Frank Moss, the way you listen to a story that turns out, without anyone planning it, to be partly about you.

Behind them, in the bar, someone started singing. Out on the square, the lights of the roller went off, and the field went dark, and for a long time the two of them sat on the bench, and neither of them got up to leave.
