**The Smell of Money**

The summer the old man died, I was twenty-two and kept the ledgers for the urine.

That is not how my mother told it to the neighbors. She said I was a clerk in the imperial fisc, which was true. She said I worked under the procurator of revenues, which was also true, though the procurator had never learned my name. What she did not say was that my ledgers recorded the fees paid by the fullers and tanners of Rome for the right to collect what the city's public latrines produced. They needed it for their trades. The Emperor needed the money. Between those two needs sat me, with a stylus and a stack of wax tablets, adding up the city's piss by the amphora.

I was good at it. That is the only reason I was brought to Reate.

---

The procurator's messenger found me in the counting room at the third hour, sweating through my tunic. He was a narrow man with a cavalryman's bowed legs, and he looked at my tablets as if they might stain him.

"You're Felix? The one who did the summary for the last quarter?"

"Yes."

"Pack whatever you need. The Emperor wants to see the accounts."

I laughed, because it seemed like a joke. Then I saw he was not joking, and I stopped.

"The Emperor is at his family estate," I said. "He's ill. Everyone says so."

"He's ill," the messenger agreed. "And he wants to see the accounts. Bring the urine figures. All of them, from the beginning."

---

It took two days to reach the Sabine hills. I rode in a cart with three chests of tablets and a physician's assistant who was carrying a jar of something that smelled worse than anything in my ledgers. He told me the Emperor had been taking the cold waters at Cutiliae and that they had done his bowels no good at all.

"He's been purging for nine days," the assistant said, with the cheerful detachment of a man who has seen many bodies give up. "Most men his age would be dead already. He's sixty-nine. Built like a mule, though. Farm stock."

"Then why does he want the accounts?"

The assistant shrugged. "Why does he want anything? He asked the master of the household for the price of olive oil yesterday. Then he argued with him about it."

The estate was not grand. I had expected marble, porphyry, statues of the gods with gilded hair. Instead there were long low farm buildings, a vineyard on the slope, chickens, a dog with a torn ear that followed the cart barking. The house where the Emperor had been born stood at the center of it, plastered and plain, and the Praetorians at its door looked embarrassed to be guarding something so ordinary.

They kept me waiting in a courtyard until evening. A slave brought me bread and a cup of thin wine. I arranged and rearranged my tablets. I had written the figures so many times that I knew them better than prayers. Four years of collections. Seven hundred and some public facilities. The fullers' guild had been late twice and fined once. There was a discrepancy in the Subura district in the second year which I had never fully resolved, and I prayed to every god I could think of that he would not ask about the Subura.

At dusk, a woman came out. She was perhaps fifty, gray-haired, plainly dressed, with the bearing of someone used to running a house. I learned later that she was Caenis's former maid, kept on after Caenis died because the Emperor could not bear to lose one more thing that had been hers.

"You're the urine clerk," she said.

"Yes, lady."

"I'm no lady. Come. He's awake, and he's in a mood, so don't waste his time."

---

The room was small and very hot. The shutters were closed against the evening insects, and a single lamp burned on a table. The smell was of sickness, vinegar, and something sweet that I guessed was meant to hide the other smells.

Titus Flavius Vespasianus, Emperor of Rome, conqueror of Judaea, restorer of the Capitol, was sitting up on a couch with a blanket over his knees. I had seen his face on coins all my life: the broad jaw, the creased forehead, the expression of a man straining at the latrine. The coins had been kind to him. In person he was gray and loose-skinned, the flesh of his neck fallen in folds, but his eyes were sharp and wet and amused.

"This is him?" he said to the woman. "He's a boy."

"He's twenty-two, they tell me."

"That's a boy." He looked at me. "Sit. No, not there, I can't see you there. Here, where the light is. What's your name?"

"Felix, Caesar."

"Felix. Lucky. Your father named you that?"

"My mother. My father was a freedman of the house of Plautius. He died before I was born."

"So she thought you were lucky to be born at all." He grunted. "She may have been right. Did you bring them?"

I lifted the first chest onto the table. My hands were shaking. He watched me open it, and something like a smile pulled at his mouth.

"You're afraid I'll find a mistake."

"No, Caesar."

"Liar. Every clerk is afraid of that. I was a clerk once, did you know? Not like you. A minor magistrate. I was in charge of keeping the streets of Rome clean. Gaius Caesar, the mad one, came down a street one day and found it full of mud, and he had his soldiers pick up the mud and stuff it into the folds of my toga. In front of everyone." He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the woman came forward with a cup, and he waved her off. "I've been close to the city's filth for a long time, Felix. Don't be afraid of it. Read me the totals."

I read him the totals. Year by year, district by district. He listened with his eyes closed. Twice he stopped me and asked me to repeat a figure. Once he said, "That's low," and I told him it was the quarter of the Tiber flood, when half the latrines along the river had been unusable, and he nodded and said, "Yes. I remember. Go on."

When I finished the fourth year, he was quiet for a long time. I thought he had fallen asleep. Then he said, without opening his eyes:

"How much, all of it together?"

I told him.

"It isn't much, is it."

"No, Caesar. Against the whole revenue, it's—" I tried to think of a polite way to say it. "It is a very small stream."

He opened his eyes and looked at me, and then he laughed so hard that the woman had to come and hold his shoulders. When he could breathe again, he wiped his face with the edge of the blanket.

"A very small stream," he said. "Gods. Write that down for my biographers. No, don't. They'll think I said it, and it'll be the only clever thing in the whole account."

---

The woman made me leave so he could rest. She gave me a cot in the storeroom, among sacks of grain and hanging strings of onions. I did not sleep. I lay listening to the house, the soft movements of slaves, the doctors murmuring in the corridor, the dog with the torn ear barking at nothing.

Before dawn, someone shook me awake. It was the woman again.

"He wants you."

"Now?"

"He's been awake since the second hour of the night. He says he can't sleep and there's no point lying there doing nothing. Bring your tablets."

This time the shutters were open. The sky outside was the gray of old pewter, and the air coming in smelled of wet earth and the vineyard. The Emperor looked worse than the night before. His lips were cracked. But he was sitting up, and he had a cup of water in one hand that he was not drinking.

"Sit," he said. "I want to tell you something, and I want you to tell me if I'm wrong."

I sat.

"My son Titus," he said, "thought the urine tax was beneath us. When I first imposed it, he came to me and complained. He said it was disgusting. He said people were laughing at us in the taverns. He said an emperor of Rome should not be counting coins from the public latrines." He paused. "Do you know what I did?"

I did. Everyone in Rome knew. But I shook my head, because it seemed like he wanted to tell it.

"I took a coin from the first payment and I held it under his nose, and I asked him if it smelled. And he said no. And I said, *Atqui ex lotio est.* And yet it comes from urine." He set the cup down on the table, carefully, as though it were very heavy. "Everyone repeats that story. They think it's a joke about money. A greedy old man who'll take a coin from anywhere."

"Isn't it?" I said, and then I was horrified at myself.

He did not seem angry. He seemed pleased.

"Good. You think. That's why I wanted the clerk who did the work and not the procurator, who would only tell me what he thought I wanted to hear." He leaned forward a little. "When I came to power, Felix, the treasury was empty. Not low. Empty. Nero had spent everything on his palace and his statues and his singing tours of Greece. Then there was the year of the four emperors, and Galba and Otho and Vitellius each spent what they could find buying the loyalty of soldiers. The Capitol had burned. Half the city was still in ruins from the great fire. The legions had not been paid. Judaea was in revolt. Gaul was in revolt. There were people in Rome eating bread made from sawdust."

He coughed. The woman stepped forward; he waved her back again.

"I calculated," he said, "that we needed forty thousand million sesterces to make the state solvent. Forty thousand million. Do you know how much that is?"

"No, Caesar. Not really. Nobody does."

"No. Nobody does. It's a number that means nothing. And so I could not raise it with one great stroke. There was no single source of wealth large enough. I had to raise it from everywhere. From the provinces. From the old imperial estates. From the senators who'd been sitting on land that should have been public. From temples that had more silver than they needed. From the merchants of Alexandria, who hated me for it. And from the latrines." He smiled. "A very small stream. You're right. But it's a stream that never dries up. As long as there are people in Rome, there will be piss in the latrines. And the tanners will need it, and they'll pay for it, and that money will go to fix an aqueduct or pay a soldier or rebuild a temple. It is a small thing that is always there. That is what keeps a state alive, Felix. Not the great conquests. The small things that are always there."

I didn't know what to say. I looked down at my tablets.

"Am I wrong?" he asked.

"No, Caesar."

"Don't say that because I'm the Emperor. I'll be dead in a few days. I can't hurt you."

I thought about it. I thought about my four years in the counting room, the endless columns, the fullers' guild and its late payments, the Subura discrepancy.

"I think you're right," I said slowly, "about why it matters. But I don't think that's why people laugh."

"Go on."

"People laugh because it's true of everything. Not just the latrines. The aqueducts. The Colosseum you're building. The bread they eat. All of it is paid for with something somebody doesn't want to look at. You just said it out loud."

The Emperor looked at me for a long time. Outside, a rooster started crowing, and another answered it from farther down the hill.

"Caenis," he said at last, quietly, and I realized he was not talking to me. "Caenis would have liked you."

---

He did not want me to leave after that. The doctors objected, and the master of the household objected, and a man who I learned later was a senator sent from Rome to witness the death objected loudest of all. The Emperor overruled them all with a flap of his hand.

"I spend my last days how I choose," he said. "I have spent enough of my life listening to senators. Let the boy stay."

So I stayed. I sat in the corner of that hot room for three days, and when he was awake, he talked, and when he was asleep, I listened to him breathe. He asked me questions about my work. He asked me about the price of bread in my district, and how many people lived in my building, and whether the new public fountain on our street worked. He asked about my mother. He told me about the mule-trading business he had run as a young man when he was too poor to keep up his rank, and how the senators had called him "the muleteer" behind his back for years afterward.

"They still do," he said. "They think I don't know."

He told me about Judaea, but not much. He talked about Caenis more. She had been his mistress when they were both young, a freedwoman like my father, and then he had married a respectable woman because he needed to, and when that wife died, Caenis came back to him and stayed until her own death. He could not marry her. A man of senatorial rank could not marry a freedwoman. But she lived in his house as his wife in everything but law, and she had kept his accounts.

"Better than any procurator," he said. "She remembered everything. Every debt anyone owed me. Every favor. She could tell you what a senator had said to me at a dinner six years earlier, word for word, and what he really meant by it." He was silent for a moment. "She died the year after I became Emperor. She waited for me all her life, and then she had one year of it. That is the thing they don't tell you about getting what you want."

---

On the fourth day, the fever rose. He was in and out of consciousness all morning. The doctors moved around him with their bowls and cloths, and the senator from Rome sat on a stool by the door and looked impatient.

In the afternoon, he woke suddenly and clutched at the blanket.

"Get me up," he said.

"Caesar, you must rest—"

"Get me up. An emperor should die standing."

The doctors looked at each other. The woman, Caenis's old maid, did not hesitate. She went to one side of the couch and gestured for me to take the other. Together we lifted him. He was heavier than he looked, and his legs barely held him, and his skin was burning. But he stood.

He looked around the room. At the doctors. At the senator, who had risen from his stool. At the woman. At me.

"Well," he said, and his voice had gone thin and dry. "I think I'm becoming a god."

The senator didn't understand. I saw it on his face, the confusion, the faint offense. He thought the old man was raving, or worse, being impious. He did not realize it was a joke. The Senate always deified dead emperors; it was a political formality, a procedure, like a tax assessment. And the Emperor, standing on his ruined legs at the very end, had decided to laugh at it.

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came out of me before I could stop it, a short loud bark of a laugh, and the senator looked at me in horror.

The Emperor turned his head, slowly, and looked at me. His eyes were very wet. He smiled.

Then his weight shifted, and the woman and I lowered him back to the couch, and he did not speak again.

---

Titus became Emperor. He was popular, at first, and generous with the treasury his father had filled, and he died two years later of a fever, younger than anyone expected. His brother Domitian came after him, and Domitian was not popular, and he was killed in his bedchamber by men he trusted. By then I had risen to procurator of revenues myself, and I had learned to keep my head down.

The urine tax continued through all of it. It continues still. Every quarter, a young clerk in the counting room adds up the figures and brings them to me, and I check them, and I always find the small errors that young clerks make, and I correct them, and I send the clerk back to try again.

Sometimes they ask me why I bother with such a small stream. It is a fraction of a fraction of the revenue, they say. It isn't worth the procurator's time.

I tell them about a man who stood up to die because he would not do it lying down, and who could still make a joke about becoming a god. And I tell them that every coin that passes through my office, every single one, comes from somewhere that nobody wants to look at. And that the honest thing, the only honest thing, is to look.

Then I hold out a coin and ask them to smell it.

They never understand. Not at first. But I keep the tradition anyway. The Subura discrepancy, by the way, was a cousin of the fullers' guildmaster who had been skimming three amphorae a month for seven years. I found him eventually. It took me a decade.

The old man would have laughed.
