**The Errata**

The proofs came back on a Tuesday with the same word struck through for the third time. Hester Lyle held the sheet up to the window over the composing frame and read the margin in the poet's cramped, leaning hand: *Not "pale." Never "pale." Something that breathes.*

"He's done it again," she said.

Her father didn't look up from the case. He was setting a sermon for a Mr. Collingwood of Islington, a man who used the word *Providence* eleven times on every page, and the capital P's were running low. "Then set it again."

"With what? He hasn't given me anything. He's only told me what it isn't."

"Then it isn't anything yet, and you'll leave a space." Gideon Lyle finally turned, his spectacles pushed up into his grey hair, his fingers black to the second knuckle. "Hetty. Twelve shillings for the whole of the octavo, and he's corrected the sixth signature four times. Leave a space and send it back."

She did not leave a space. She sat with the sheet on her knees through the dinner hour, eating bread and dripping and reading the poem from its beginning, which was not the way one was meant to read proofs. One was meant to read them backward, word by word, so that the sense did not carry the eye over the errors. But she had read this one backward three times already, and she wanted, just once, to read it the way he had written it.

It was called "To a Lamp Left Burning." It was about a window seen from a street at night, a single light in an upper room, and the speaker standing below in the cold not knowing who was inside, whether they were reading or ill or dying or simply forgetting to blow out the candle. The fourth stanza ended:

*And all the city sleeps, and thou alone,*
*A* ——— *flame against the dark of stone.*

The first proof had said *pale*. The second had said *faint*, which he had struck through with such force that the nib had torn the paper. The third had said *pale* again, because Hester had assumed he had changed his mind, and he had written beside it: *Not "pale." Never "pale." Something that breathes.*

---

His name was Thomas Ashby, and he was twenty-four years old, and he lived in two rooms over a chandler's in Clerkenwell. Hester knew this because she had carried the first proofs there herself in October, when the boy who usually ran errands had gone home to Kent with a fever. She remembered the stair, which was narrow and smelled of tallow, and the door at the top, which had been opened by a man so thin she had at first taken him for someone's grandfather.

He had not been anyone's grandfather. He had red hair that wanted cutting and a coat buttoned wrong, and he had taken the proofs from her as though she had handed him a living bird.

"Is this all of it?"

"The first four signatures, sir. The rest by Friday."

"Four." He had turned the sheets over, very quickly, and she had watched his eyes move, and she had seen the precise moment he found his own words in print for the first time, because his mouth had opened slightly and then closed. "They look," he said, "like somebody else's."

"Most people say that."

"Do they?" He had looked at her properly then. "And what do you tell them?"

She had not been asked a question by a customer before. "I tell them that's what type is for, sir. So it can be anybody's."

He had laughed, and the laugh had turned into coughing, and the coughing had gone on long enough that she had looked away down the stair and pretended to examine the plaster. When it stopped he said, quite cheerfully, "I'll have them back to your father by Monday," and shut the door.

---

That was October. It was now the second week of December, and the book, which was to be called *Lamps and Other Poems*, which was to be sold by Mr. Hessey at the sign of the Cross Keys for five shillings in boards, and which had been paid for, Hester suspected, by somebody other than Thomas Ashby, was still not finished. Mr. Hessey had written twice. Her father had answered once.

"He's not well," Hester said that evening, when her father complained again. "You can see he's not well."

"Half of London's not well. The other half still pays its bills." But he said it gently, and after supper he took down the sixth signature himself and stood looking at the locked forme with the empty space where the word should be.

"*Something that breathes*," he read aloud from the margin. "What's that supposed to mean?"

"I think he means the flame. He wants a word that makes it seem alive."

"Then let him find it." Gideon set the sheet down. "You've been thinking about it."

"I have not."

"You've been thinking about it since Tuesday. You set *Providence* with a small *p* on the Collingwood twice this morning. Mr. Collingwood will think we're Unitarians."

She laughed in spite of herself. Her father put a hand on her shoulder, briefly, the way he did when he was about to say something he didn't want to say.

"Your mother used to do that," he said. "Read the proofs forward. I told her it was bad practice."

"Was it?"

"She caught more than I did." He took his hand away. "Take it round to him tomorrow. Don't wait for the boy. Ask him to make up his mind, and if he can't, ask him if he'll let you make it up for him."

---

The chandler's wife let her in. The stair was colder than it had been in October, and the smell of tallow was stronger, and the door at the top was not opened when she knocked.

"Mr. Ashby?"

"It's open." The voice came from far inside, as though from the bottom of a well.

She had not been inside the rooms before. The first was a sitting room with a fire that had gone almost to ash, and a table covered in papers, and a bookcase whose lower shelves had been emptied and whose upper shelves held only a jug and a plaster bust with its nose chipped. The second door stood open on a bedroom. Thomas Ashby was sitting up in bed in his shirt with a blanket around his shoulders and a board across his knees, writing.

"Miss Lyle." He did not seem surprised. "Is it the sixth?"

"It's the sixth."

"I'm sorry." He put down the pen. "I've been very difficult. I know I have. Your father must think I'm a lunatic."

"He thinks you owe him four days of composing." She had meant it to come out lightly, and it did not quite. "I'm sorry. That was rude."

"It was true. I'd rather true." He held out his hand for the proof, and she crossed the room and gave it to him, and he looked at the gap in the line. She had set four em-quads there, blank metal, and they printed as nothing, a little white ditch in the middle of the verse. "Oh," he said. "That's honest of you."

"My father said to ask you to make up your mind."

"I can't." He said it without any self-pity, as a fact. "I've tried. I lie here and I try every word in the language. *Pale* is what I wrote first, and it's correct, a candle seen from below at night does look pale, but it's a dead word. It's a word for a corpse. And I don't want—" He stopped. "I don't want the lamp to be dead. That's the whole point. The person outside doesn't know whether the person inside is alive. The flame has to be the one thing in the poem that's certainly alive."

"What about *living*?"

"Too plain. It tells you instead of showing you."

"*Trembling*?"

"I had *trembling* in the first draft. It's in the line two stanzas up. *The trembling glass.*" He smiled. "You've read it, then. Properly."

"I'm meant to read it backward."

"Why?"

She explained, and he listened with the fixed attention of someone who has very little else to attend to, and when she had finished he said, "So you read it the way a stranger would. Someone who doesn't care what it means. Only whether every letter is in its place."

"Yes."

"That's a better way of reading than most critics manage." He coughed, briefly, into a handkerchief that he folded before she could see it. "And then you read it forward, when nobody was looking."

"Yes."

"And what did you think?"

Nobody had ever asked her that, either. She looked at the window over his bed. It faced east, over the roofs toward St. John's, and the afternoon was already going dark, though it was barely three.

"I thought," she said slowly, "that it wasn't really about a lamp."

"No?"

"I thought it was about the person standing in the street. Wanting to go up and knock and not doing it. And the lamp being the only thing that tells them anyone's there at all." She felt her face go hot. "I don't know about poetry."

"You know about it as well as anyone. Better than Hessey, who's publishing it." He looked down at the proof again. "Sit down. Please. There's a chair under those papers. Just put them on the floor, they're nothing."

She moved the papers. They were drafts of the same line, over and over, in ink and in pencil, some crossed through, some circled. *A pale flame. A faint flame. A thin flame. A small flame. A quiet flame. A waking flame. A watching flame.*

"*Watching*'s not bad," she said.

"It makes the flame into an eye. Then it's the lamp looking at the street, not the street looking at the lamp. Wrong way round." He leaned his head back against the wall. "Do you know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking that whatever word I put there, it'll be there forever. Or as long as anyone keeps the book, which won't be very long. But in the type, I mean. Once your father prints it, five hundred copies will say that word and no other. And if it's wrong I'll have put five hundred wrong things into the world."

"There's always errata."

"Errata." He laughed. "A little slip of paper at the back saying *for 'pale' read 'something else.'* Admitting it in public."

"Most books have one. Mr. Collingwood's sermons had a page and a half."

"Did they really?"

"He'd spelled *Nebuchadnezzar* four different ways."

Thomas Ashby laughed until he coughed again, and this time it went on long enough that she got up and found the jug on the bookcase and poured him water, and he drank it with his eyes closed.

"Miss Lyle," he said, when he could. "Will you do something for me?"

"If I can."

"I'm going to Italy. Did your father tell you? There's a ship in January. The doctor says the air in Rome—" He made a small gesture with the hand not holding the cup, a gesture that dismissed the doctor and Rome and the air together. "I'd like to see the book before I go. Finished. I'd like to hold it."

"Then you'll have to pick a word."

"I know. That's what I'm asking." He opened his eyes. "Pick it for me. You've read it forward. You know what it's for. Go home and set whatever word you think is right, and don't tell me what it is, and print it."

She stared at him. "I can't do that."

"Why not?"

"Because it's yours. It's your poem."

"It was mine. Then it went into your type, and you said yourself, that's what type is for. So it could be anybody's." He held out the proof to her. "I'd rather it was yours than nobody's. I'd rather it was yours than *pale*."

---

She walked home through Smithfield in the early dark with the sheet folded inside her coat. The market was closing, and the gutters were full of straw and worse, and somewhere a boy was singing a ballad about a highwayman in a voice that cracked on every high note. She did not think about the word. She thought about the room with the emptied bookcase, and the drafts on the floor, and the fact that he had not asked her name, her first name, and that she had not offered it.

Her father was still in the shop. He had lit the lamp over the frame and was distributing type from the Collingwood back into the case, letter by letter, his hand moving between the compartments without his eyes following it.

"Well?"

"He wants me to choose."

Gideon's hand stopped over the case. "Choose what?"

"The word."

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he went back to distributing. "Then choose."

"Father—"

"He's the author. If he wants the compositor to finish his verses, that's between him and Mr. Hessey and his own conscience. I'll set a sermon with a small *p* if I'm paid for it." A pause. The click of a letter falling into its box. "Your mother would have known in a minute."

"I don't."

"No. But you will." He wiped his hands on his apron. "I'm going up. Don't burn the lamp all night. Oil's dear."

---

She did burn the lamp most of the night.

She sat at the frame with the forme of the sixth signature in front of her, the empty space in the fourth stanza waiting, and she did not try words in her head the way he had, one after another, like keys in a lock. Instead she looked at the lamp. It was an ordinary shop lamp, brass, the glass chimney sooted on one side where her father never cleaned it properly. The flame inside it was not pale. That was the first thing. Seen up close, from the same room, it was yellow at the edges and blue at the base and almost white in a thin line at the very centre, and it was never still. Even with the shutters closed and no draught at all, it moved. It drew itself up and settled, drew itself up and settled.

Like breathing, she thought. *Something that breathes.*

She thought of the man in the street in the poem, looking up. He would not see the colours. He would see a light in an upper window, and he would see that it moved, and that movement would be the only thing that told him that the room was not simply a painting of a room.

She thought of Thomas Ashby lying in the dark at that moment, in Clerkenwell, a mile away. Whether his candle was lit.

At some time past two in the morning she got up and went to the case and took out four letters from the lower case, one after another, and set them in her composing stick, and justified the line, and locked it into the forme. Then she inked a single proof, pulled it on the hand press, and held it up to the lamp.

*And all the city sleeps, and thou alone,*
*A wakeful flame against the dark of stone.*

It was not, she thought, a very surprising word. It was not the sort of word critics would quote. But it breathed, and it was awake, and it was on the side of the one who was alive in the room. It was a word that meant: someone is still here.

She blew out the lamp and went up to bed.

---

The book came from the binder's on the nineteenth of December, in grey-blue boards with a printed paper label. Hester carried the first copy to Clerkenwell herself. The chandler's wife told her at the foot of the stair that Mr. Ashby had gone down to his sister's in Hampstead the week before, to be nursed until the ship, and had left an address, and Hester sent the book there by the carrier with a note that said only *From the printer's, with compliments*, and did not sign it.

She did not hear anything. She had not expected to. In January her father read in the paper that the ship for Leghorn had sailed from Gravesend, and he read it aloud at breakfast without comment, and she said nothing either.

In April a letter came to the shop addressed to *Miss Lyle, compositor*. It had been posted in Rome and had taken seven weeks. It was very short.

*I have read it forward and backward, and it is every letter in its place, and the one you chose is the best word in the book. I am afraid I shall never write another as good. Thank you for the errata you did not need to print.*

*Your obliged servant,*
*T. A.*

And underneath, in a hand that had grown smaller and shakier even since the proofs:

*I still don't know your name. Write it here and I will imagine it.*

There was no return address beyond *Rome*. She kept the letter in the drawer under the case where her mother's composing stick still lay. She did not know where to send her name. By the time she thought of writing to Mr. Hessey to ask, Mr. Hessey had heard from the sister, and there was no longer any need.

She went on setting type for thirty years. She set sermons and almanacs and three editions of a treatise on drainage, and in 1847 she set a slim memorial volume of Thomas Ashby's collected verse, with a preface by a man who had never met him, which called "To a Lamp Left Burning" the most perfect small poem of its decade and singled out, for particular praise, *the inspired epithet "wakeful."*

She read the preface backward, the way one is meant to, letter by letter, so the sense would not carry her eye. Then, when no one was looking, she read it forward.
