**The Hidden Face**

The scaffold on the north side of the nave had been up so long that the pigeons treated it as part of the building. Maren Holt climbed it every morning at seven. Her breath smoked in the cold, and her boots rang on the boards. Eleven years on the job had taught her which planks flexed and which held. She could have walked the whole of it blind.

At the fourth lift she stopped, as she always did, and looked down the length of the nave. At that hour the cathedral was nearly dark. The great columns climbed out of the shadows like the trunks of trees that had forgotten they were stone. Above them the ribs of the vault sprang outward and met overhead in a lattice of bosses she could almost touch. She had touched most of them, over the years. Some were roses and some were green men with oak leaves spilling from their mouths. One was a pelican tearing at its own breast to feed its young. That one had needed new lead in the joints, and she had spent three weeks with her face eighteen inches from its beak.

"You're doing the thing again," said Tobias from below.

"What thing?"

"Standing there like you're about to give a sermon."

"I'm checking the light."

"It's dark."

"That's what I'm checking."

He hauled himself up beside her. He was twenty-two, and a ladder still felt to him like something to beat. He had come to her from a college course in heritage masonry, full of theory and frightened of a chisel. That had been fourteen months ago. Now he was good. He wasn't as good as he thought, but he was better than he knew, and Maren had decided that was the right place for an apprentice to be.

"Triforium today?" he asked.

"Triforium. Bay nine. The arcade's moving."

"Moving how?"

"Slowly. Like everything else here."

---

The triforium was the gallery that ran above the main arcade and below the clerestory windows. It was a narrow passage of small arches, three to a bay, set into the thickness of the wall. From the floor of the nave it looked like decoration, a band of lace between the heavy columns and the high glass. Up close it was a working space. It was a corridor the original builders had used to move along the wall, and generations of glaziers and roofers and plumbers had used it since. The floor was worn into shallow troughs. Somebody in the eighteenth century had carved his initials and a date into a pier: *W.R. 1764*. Somebody else, later, had tried to scratch them out.

Bay nine had a crack. It started at the springing of the middle arch and ran diagonally up through the spandrel, thin as a hair. Over the summer it had grown to the width of a fingernail. The surveyors had put tell-tales across it, little glass strips glued on either side. Two of them had snapped.

Maren knelt and shone her torch into the crack. "It's not structural," she said. "Not yet. The pier's sound. This is the infill working loose. Somebody packed it badly a long time ago."

"How long ago?"

"Victorian, probably. They loved to fix things that weren't broken and leave the broken things alone." She ran her thumb along the edge of the stone. "We'll have to take out the spandrel. Three, maybe four blocks. Rebed them."

Tobias whistled. "The Dean'll love that."

"The Dean loves anything that keeps the roof up."

---

They spent the first two days cutting out the old mortar. It was slow work. Maren used a plugging chisel no wider than a pencil and tapped it with a dummy mallet of turned lignum vitae. The mallet had belonged to her father. He had worked on this same building in the 1980s, on the west front, before his lungs gave out. She had been nine the first time he brought her up the scaffold. She remembered holding onto his belt and looking at the town spread out below. It had looked like a map of itself.

"Why do they make it so tall?" she had asked him.

"So you have to look up," he said.

She had thought for years that this was a joke. It had taken her a long time to understand that it wasn't.

On the third day they lifted out the first block. It came free with a dry crack, like a knuckle, and a puff of dust that hung in the torchlight. Behind it was rubble, the packing of broken stone and lime the old masons had used to fill the core of the wall. Behind the second block was more rubble.

Behind the third block was a face.

Tobias saw it first. He was holding the torch while Maren eased the block onto the boards, and he went quiet. Tobias was almost never quiet.

"Maren."

"Hold the light steady."

"Maren. Look."

It was carved into the back of the fourth block, the one still in the wall. That face of the stone should never have been seen. It had been turned inward when the block was laid and sealed behind its neighbour. The carving was small, no bigger than a palm. It was a man's face, round and cheerful. The cheeks were puffed out, the eyes squeezed half shut, and the tongue stuck out at whoever might be looking.

Maren sat back on her heels.

"Is that—" Tobias began.

"Yes."

"It's taking the piss."

"Yes." She found she was smiling. "It is."

She leaned in. The tooling was good, quick and confident. A claw chisel had roughed it out and a fine point had finished it, and the work had the economy of someone who had done a thousand of these. Beside the face was a mark: a vertical stroke with two short diagonals crossing it near the top, like an arrow with too many feathers.

"Banker mark," she said. "Every mason had one. You cut it into your stones so the master knew whose work was whose, and you got paid for what you'd done."

"So this is a signature."

"This is a signature and a joke." She touched the tongue with one fingertip. The stone was cold and very smooth. "He knew nobody would see it. He carved it for the inside of the wall."

"Why would anyone do that?"

Maren didn't answer straight away. Below them, faintly, the organist had begun to practise. It was a slow piece, and the low notes came up through the scaffold and into her knees.

"I don't know," she said. "Ask him."

---

The Dean came up the next morning. He was a tall, stooped man called Anselm Ferrars who had been at the cathedral for nineteen years. He had an academic's habit of tilting his head when he was interested in something, and he climbed the scaffold with the care of someone who knew his insurance situation intimately. When he reached the triforium he was breathing hard. He stood for a long moment looking at the face without saying anything.

"Thirteenth century," Maren said. "Probably. I'd want someone to look at the tooling, but the mark matches others on this side of the nave. We've got it on six capitals in the north aisle and on the piers of the crossing."

"The same man?"

"The same mark. Could be the same man. Could be his son. Marks got passed down sometimes."

Ferrars tilted his head. "He's sticking his tongue out."

"He is."

"At God, do you think? Or at us?"

"I think at the master mason. He's the one who'd have been checking the work." She shrugged. "Or at nobody. Maybe he just felt like it."

"Seven hundred years," said Ferrars, "in the dark."

"Give or take."

He was quiet again. Tobias, who had been pretending to sort chisels, said, "What happens to it now?"

"That's rather the question," said Ferrars. He looked at Maren. "What do you recommend?"

She had been thinking about it all night. She had lain awake in her flat above the chandler's on Cowl Street and listened to the cathedral bells mark the quarters, and turned it over and over.

"We could take it out," she said. "Cut the block, replace it with new stone. Put this one in the museum, in a case with a light on it and a card. People would come to see it. It'd be in the guidebook."

"Or?"

"Or we put it back the way he left it. Face in. Rebed the spandrel and seal it up."

"And nobody ever sees it."

"We've seen it."

Ferrars smiled faintly. "That's very nearly a theological position, Ms Holt."

"I'm not a theologian. I'm a mason."

"In this building," he said, "I'm not sure there's a difference." He looked up at the clerestory, where the morning light was coming through the old glass in pale bands of blue and amber. "Let me think about it. There will be a committee. There is always a committee."

---

The committee took three weeks. In that time Maren and Tobias rebedded the first three blocks. They used a hot lime mortar that Maren mixed herself in a steel tub on the nave floor, slaking the quicklime with water until it hissed and steamed. Tobias hated the smell. Maren loved it. It smelled of every building site she had ever stood on as a child.

"Why lime?" he asked her one afternoon. He knew the answer from college. She could tell he was asking because he wanted to hear her say it.

"Because the building moves," she said. "It breathes. It gets wet and it dries out, and it gets hot and it gets cold. Cement's too hard. It doesn't give. You put cement in an old wall and the stone cracks around it, because the stone's weaker than the joint. Lime's softer than the stone. When something has to give, the lime gives, and you can replace the lime."

"So the mortar's meant to fail."

"The mortar's meant to fail first. That's different."

He thought about that. "That's sort of bleak."

"It's sort of not," said Maren. "It's why the thing's still standing."

---

The decision came on a Thursday. Ferrars climbed up himself to tell them, which Maren hadn't expected.

"The committee," he said, "was divided."

"Of course it was."

"The archaeologist wanted it out. The cathedral architect wanted it out. The chapter clerk wanted it out, because he thinks it would sell postcards, and he isn't wrong." Ferrars lowered himself carefully onto an upturned bucket. "The canon treasurer wanted to know how much it would cost to remove the block, and when he found out, he wanted it left in."

"So it stays?"

"It's photographed. It's measured. It's recorded in every way the archaeologist could think of, which is a considerable number of ways. There will be a 3D scan. There will be a paper in a journal nobody reads." He spread his hands. "And then it goes back into the dark."

Maren let out a breath she hadn't known she was holding.

"You're pleased," said Ferrars.

"I am."

"May I ask why? You said yourself people would come to see it."

She looked at the face. In the torchlight it seemed almost to be laughing.

"My dad worked on the west front," she said. "Did you know that?"

"I didn't."

"Eighty-one to eighty-seven. He replaced most of the lower tier of saints. Did the drapery on Saint Hugh, the one with the swan. He was proud of that swan." She turned the dummy mallet over in her hands. "He used to say the best work on a building like this is the work nobody sees. The foundations, the core of the walls, the timber up in the roof. If you do it right, nobody ever knows you were there. People look at the glass and the carving. They never think about what's holding it all up."

"And this?" Ferrars nodded at the face.

"This is a man who knew that," said Maren. "He knew he was going to be part of the inside of the wall, so he left himself a joke in there. For company." She shook her head. "If you put it in a glass case, it's just a thing to look at. If you leave it where it is, it's still doing what he meant it to do."

"Which is?"

"Being there."

Ferrars was silent for a long moment. Below them, a school group had come in through the west doors. The children's voices rose into the vault and broke up there, the way all voices did in this building, into something larger and less distinct.

"My predecessor," said Ferrars eventually, "used to say that a cathedral is the only kind of building that's never finished. They started this one in 1192. The spire went up in 1311 and came down in a storm in 1548. The west front was rebuilt in the 1600s and again in the 1800s, and again, apparently, by your father. And it's being rebuilt now, by you." He nodded at the scaffold, the tubs, the chisels. "None of the people who began it lived to see it standing. None of us will see it finished. It doesn't finish."

"That's the job," said Maren.

"Yes," said Ferrars. "I suppose it is." He stood up, wincing, and brushed the dust from his cassock. "Put him back, Ms Holt. Gently, please. He's older than all of us."

---

They set the last block on a Monday in late November. Maren laid the mortar bed herself, a smooth grey ribbon along the top of the course below. Tobias stood ready with the lewis and the block and tackle, and between them they swung the stone into place with the face turned inward. It settled onto the bed with a soft, final sound. Maren tapped it home with her father's mallet. She pointed the joints and struck them off with the edge of a trowel, and then there was nothing to see. The spandrel was a flat field of honey-coloured stone, like every other spandrel in the triforium.

Tobias was quiet again.

"You all right?" Maren asked.

"Yeah." He rubbed the back of his neck. "It's weird, though. Knowing he's in there."

"He's always been in there. Now you know."

"Do you think there are more?"

Maren looked down the long passage of the triforium, bay after bay, arch after arch. She thought of the piers of the crossing and the capitals of the north aisle where that arrow-with-too-many-feathers had been cut again and again. She thought of the miles of stone in this building and the thousands of hands that had shaped it, almost none of whose names anyone remembered.

"I think," she said, "it's very likely."

---

That evening, after Tobias had gone home and the last verger had locked the south door, Maren climbed the scaffold one more time. She didn't turn on the work lights. The nave below was dark except for the red sanctuary lamp burning at the far end, a single coal in all that shadow.

There was a small offcut of new stone in her bag, a scrap of the same oolitic limestone they'd used for the repairs, no bigger than her fist. She had been carrying it around for a week. She sat down on the boards with her back to the pier, set the stone on her knee and took out a fine point.

She worked slowly by the light of her head torch. When she had finished there was a mark on one face of the offcut: two short horizontal lines with a vertical between them, like a letter H with its crossbar lifted too high. Her father had used it on every stone he'd ever cut. She had never used it herself. Until tonight she hadn't been sure she had the right.

Beside it, after a moment's thought, she carved a very small swan.

There was a gap in the rubble core behind the bay-ten spandrel. It was scheduled for repointing in the spring, and the cavity would be packed and sealed. Nobody would open it again for a hundred years, perhaps two hundred. She reached in and set the little stone carefully at the back, face inward, where the dark would keep it.

Then she sat for a while in the quiet. Overhead, unseen, the vault held its shape against the weight of the roof as it had for eight centuries. It was a thrust and counter-thrust of stone that would stand only as long as every part of it kept pushing against every other part. Below, the sanctuary lamp burned steadily.

*So you have to look up*, she thought.

She picked up her father's mallet and her bag and climbed down through the scaffold in the dark. She knew which planks flexed and which held. The cathedral went on standing around her, unfinished, as it always had been.
