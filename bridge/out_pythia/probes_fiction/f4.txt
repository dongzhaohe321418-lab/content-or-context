**The Chart**

My mother kept the hurricane chart on the back of the pantry door, where the light was poor and nobody but her had reason to look. It was a laminated map of the Atlantic, the kind the hardware store in Port Selene gave away every June with a purchase over forty dollars, and she marked it with a red grease pencil she kept tied to a string looped over the doorknob. Every storm got a line. Every line got a name written small at its tail, in the careful upright hand she had learned from the nuns.

At the end of each November she wiped the whole thing clean with a rag and a little cooking oil. The red came away in long pink smears, and for a few days the map would smell faintly of coconut. Then she hung it back on the door, blank, and waited.

She died in March. That June, nobody went to the hardware store, because there was no reason to. I took the chart down from the pantry door and found, underneath it, a rectangle of paint two shades brighter than the rest, the color the kitchen had been when I was a child. I stood looking at that brighter rectangle for a long time. Then I hung the chart back up and found the grease pencil, worn down to a stub, still on its string.

---

"You're doing Gran's thing," Teo said in July, coming in for water. He was fifteen, and he moved through the house the way the tide comes over the flats, all at once and from every direction.

"Somebody has to."

"The phone does it. The phone does it better." He held his up, the screen bright with a swirl of color, a cone of probability fanning out from a smudge of cloud near the Cape Verde islands. "See? It's got the whole spaghetti thing. Twelve models."

"I see it."

"So why draw it?"

I didn't have an answer that would survive his face, so I said, "Because the phone forgets in December, and the door doesn't."

"The door forgets in December. You wipe it."

He was right about that. He was right about a lot that summer, and it made me tired in a way I couldn't explain to him.

---

The first one that year was called Abel, and it went nowhere. It spun itself out north of the Azores like a top losing interest, and I drew it in a short hooked line that ended in open water. The second, Bianca, crossed the Yucatán and fell apart over the mountains. I drew her too. My mother had always drawn every storm, even the ones on the far side of the Gulf that could never touch us. "They're all in the same family," she used to say. "You don't only write to the cousins who visit."

I didn't understand that when I was young. I thought it was one of those things old women said because it sounded wise and nobody could check.

By August the chart had five lines on it, and Teo had stopped asking about it. He had other concerns. His father lived in Tampa now, with a wife named Courtney who sent Christmas cards with glitter on them, and there was an arrangement being discussed in long phone calls I could hear only Teo's half of: a school there with a robotics program, a room of his own, a car when he turned sixteen.

"It's not about you," he told me one night, which is the thing people say when it is partly about you.

"I know."

"It's about, like. There's nothing here, Ma."

I looked out the window at the dark, at the line of lights along the seawall, at the fishing boats knocking together in the harbor like cows in a barn. "There's a lot here."

"There's a lot of the same here."

---

The sixth storm formed on the twenty-ninth of August, off the coast of Africa, a little bruise of thunderstorms with a low spinning underneath. The hurricane center gave it a number first, then a name when it earned one. The name was Delphine.

I stood at the pantry door with the grease pencil and couldn't make my hand write it.

Delphine was my mother's name.

It wasn't a common name, and it had never been on the list before, not in my lifetime. They rotate the lists, six of them, and they retire names that do too much damage, and they fill the holes with new ones. Somebody in an office in Geneva or Miami had put my mother's name on a list years ago, and the list had come back around, and here it was, turning in the warm water two thousand miles away.

Teo found me there. He looked at the chart, at the pencil, at my face.

"Oh," he said. He'd seen it on his phone already, I could tell. "I was going to say something. It's kind of creepy, right?"

"It's just a name."

"Yeah." He was quiet. Then, carefully: "Gran would've thought it was funny."

She would have. She would have laughed her big laugh, the one that showed her gold tooth, and said something like *Finally they name one after somebody who knows how to make an entrance.* And then she would have drawn the line, small and upright, and gone back to the stove.

I wrote it. *Delphine.* My hand shook a little on the *ph.*

---

For six days she did what storms do in the open ocean, which is to grow and wobble and refuse to commit. The spaghetti on Teo's phone spread wide, some strands curving harmlessly north toward Bermuda, some running straight west into the islands, a few, a stubborn few, threading through the passage south of us and bending back up toward the coast.

Toward us.

I drew her once a day, at the same time my mother always had, after the five o'clock advisory. Teo started showing up at five. He didn't say why. He just leaned against the refrigerator and watched me make the little dot and connect it to the last little dot.

"She's going pretty straight," he said on the third day.

"She is."

"The European model has her turning."

"Your grandmother used to say the European model was a snob."

He laughed, surprised into it. "What does that even mean?"

"I have no idea. She said it every year."

On the fourth day Delphine became a hurricane. On the fifth she became a major one, Category 3, an eye clearing out in the satellite pictures like someone had pressed a thumb into the clouds. On the sixth the strands on the phone drew in close together, the way a crowd goes quiet, and most of them ran across the passage and through the little cluster of dots on the map that was us.

---

The island does a thing before a storm that I have never seen anywhere else, though I've only been to a few other places, so maybe everybody does it. It goes very calm and very busy at the same time. The sky turns a clean, polished blue, and the sea lies down flat, and the heat sits on you like a hand. And underneath that stillness everybody is moving: hauling boats up the ramp on rollers, nailing plywood over the windows, lining up at the hardware store and the gas station and the one bank machine.

At the hardware store, Mr. Okafor was handing out the laminated charts again, though it was September and the season was half over.

"For your mother's door," he said, and then caught himself. "Ah. I'm sorry, Inès."

"No," I said. "I'll take it."

I already had one. I took it anyway. Teo watched me roll it up and put it under my arm with the box of nails and the duct tape and the three gallons of water.

"Two charts," he said in the truck.

"One for the pantry. One for you."

"I don't need—"

"Take the chart, Teo."

He took the chart.

---

We boarded the house on Thursday. Teo did the high windows from the ladder, and I held the plywood steady and handed up the screws, and neither of us said anything about Tampa. The radio in the kitchen ran the advisories every hour now, the announcer's voice going flat and careful the way it does when the news gets bad. Landfall projected Friday night. Sustained winds one hundred and twenty. Storm surge eight to eleven feet in low-lying areas.

Our house wasn't low-lying. My grandfather had built it on the ridge above the harbor, out of cinderblock and stubbornness, and it had come through Hugo and Marilyn and the long bad year when there were two in three weeks. My mother had been born in it, and she had died in it, in the front room with the windows open so she could hear the boats.

"We should go to the school," Teo said, looking at the sky. "The shelter."

"This house has been through worse."

"You don't know that. Nobody knows that. That's literally the point of hurricanes."

He was right again. But the shelter was the gymnasium at the primary school, three hundred people on cots under fluorescent lights, and I had been there for Marilyn when I was nine, and I remembered a man crying in the dark all night, and the smell of the toilets by the second day.

"If it's still a three tomorrow morning, we stay," I said. "If it's a four, we go."

He thought about that. "Deal."

---

Friday morning it was still a three. We stayed.

The rain came first, around noon, in bands. A gray sheet would come walking across the harbor and hit the house all at once like a thrown bucket, and then pass, and the sun would almost come out, and then another. By three the bands had closed up into one long roar. By five the power went, with a pop and a sigh from the refrigerator, and the house went dim, lit only by the gray light leaking in around the plywood.

I lit the hurricane lamp. Teo got the battery radio going. At five o'clock the advisory came through crackling: Delphine's center forty miles to the southeast, moving west-northwest at twelve miles an hour.

I got up and went to the pantry door.

"Ma," Teo said. "Seriously?"

"Bring the lamp."

He brought the lamp. He held it up so I could see the map, and I found the coordinates and made the dot and drew the line from yesterday's dot. It was very close to us now. The red line was almost touching the little speck that was our island. In the lamplight the grease pencil looked black.

"Okay," Teo said quietly. "Okay. I get it now."

"Get what?"

"Why you draw it." He was looking at the chart, not at me. "On the phone it's like—it's happening somewhere. It's a picture of a thing. This is like you're keeping track of it. Like you're watching it so it knows somebody's watching."

I didn't say anything, because if I had, I would have cried, and the wind was getting loud enough now that I wasn't sure I'd be able to stop.

---

The worst of it came around eleven.

I want to describe it but I'm not sure I can. The sound was the main thing. It wasn't a howl, the way people say. It was more like being inside a drum while somebody very large and very patient kept hitting it, over and over, with no rhythm you could follow. The house shuddered. Things banged on the roof: branches, maybe, or pieces of other people's houses. Water came in under the kitchen door in a thin bright sheet and ran across the tiles, and Teo and I pushed towels against it, and the towels soaked through, and we pushed more.

At one point something hit the plywood over the front window hard enough to crack it. I heard it split. Teo grabbed my arm, and we went into the pantry, the two of us, because it was the only room in the house with no windows. My grandfather had built it that way on purpose. We sat on the floor with our backs against the shelves of canned beans and rice, the lamp between our feet, and the chart hanging on the door in front of us.

"Tell me something," Teo said. He had to shout it.

"What?"

"Anything! Tell me something about her!"

So I told him about my mother. I told him about the time she'd chased a goat down the length of the main road in her church clothes because it had eaten her hat. I told him she'd learned to read the newspaper in English before she could speak it, so for years she pronounced everything like it was spelled, and the whole island still said *Wed-nes-day* because of her. I told him she'd wanted to be a nurse, and had been one, for eleven months in Miami when she was nineteen, and had hated the cold of the air conditioning so much she came home.

"She left?" Teo said. "She went to the States?"

"She did."

"And came back."

"She came back."

He was quiet for a minute, or as quiet as you can be inside a drum. Then he said, "Did she say why?"

I thought about it. The house groaned around us. "She said Miami was fine. She said it was a nice place. She said the only problem was that nobody there knew which way the wind was going to come from." I looked at him. "I think she meant that you can live anywhere. But some places, you know how they move. You know where they're going to hit you. And there's something in that."

"That's a weird reason."

"She was a weird woman."

He laughed, and then he put his head on my shoulder the way he hadn't done since he was nine, and we sat there while Delphine tore at the roof.

---

The eye came over us at about one in the morning.

I'd heard about it all my life but never been in it. Hugo's had passed to the south, and Marilyn's to the north. The noise stopped. Not faded, stopped, like a door closing. The pressure did something strange in my ears. Teo lifted his head and looked at me with enormous eyes.

"Is it over?"

"No. It's the middle."

"Can we look?"

Against every rule my mother had taught me, I said yes.

We went to the back door and opened it a crack. The yard was a wreck, the mango tree down across the fence, the neighbor's tin roof lying in our garden like a crumpled letter. But above all that, straight up, the sky was clear. Stars. A whole column of stars, and around them, on every side, a wall of cloud rising so high it seemed to lean in at the top, lit faintly silver from somewhere I couldn't see. It was like standing at the bottom of a well.

"Holy," Teo whispered. "Holy—"

"Don't swear."

"I wasn't going to swear. I was going to say holy." He was staring up. "She's huge."

*She.* He'd said it without thinking.

"Yeah," I said. "She is."

We stood there maybe two minutes. Then the wind started picking up from the other direction, the back wall of the eye coming on, and I pulled him in and shut the door and we went back to the pantry and waited out the second half, which was worse than the first, but shorter.

---

In the morning the island looked like it had been picked up and shaken. Boats in the street. The seawall broken in two places. The church steeple lying across the square. But the house on the ridge had held, mostly. We'd lost the plywood over the front window, and the window behind it, and a third of the roof over the back bedroom. That was all.

Nobody on our side of the island died. On the windward side two old men had refused to leave their houses near the beach, and one of them was found two days later, and one never was. I knew them both a little. Everyone did.

The hurricane center retired the name that spring, the way they do when a storm does enough damage. *Delphine* would never be used again. Somebody would choose a new D name to fill the hole in the list, and in six years it would come back around, and some other woman in some other kitchen would draw it on some other door.

---

Teo went to Tampa in August of the following year. I helped him pack. At the bottom of his duffel, under his good shoes, I saw the second chart, the one Mr. Okafor had given me, still rolled up and rubber-banded.

"You're taking it?"

He shrugged, turning red. "Florida's in the box. Might as well."

"You'll need a grease pencil."

"I bought one already." He reached into the pocket of his jeans and showed it to me, red, brand new, the paper wrapper still on. "Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird."

"You're making it a little weird."

The night before his flight, at the end of a quiet season with only three lines on the chart, I took my mother's pencil down off its string and gave it to him too. Then I went to the hardware store and bought a new one for myself.

In November I wiped the chart clean with a rag and a little coconut oil. The red came off in long pink smears. And I left the line from the year before, the one that ran right through our island, alone. I had traced it in black marker months earlier, so it wouldn't come off. It runs across the map now, permanent, through the middle of everything. A little upright name at its tail, in my handwriting, which looks more like hers every year.

Some lines you don't wipe off. Some cousins you keep writing to.
