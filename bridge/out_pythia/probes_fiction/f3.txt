**The Third Volume**

The proofs came on Tuesdays, wrapped in brown paper and smelling of the printer's ink, and on Tuesdays Mrs. Constance Mallory did not come down to breakfast.

Ruth Peale had been in the house on Ellery Street for eleven months before she understood that this was not illness. In the first weeks she had carried up toast and weak tea and found her mistress at the writing table by the window in the same grey wrapper she had slept in, her hair still in its night plait, a pen in one hand and a red pencil in the other, as though she could not decide which weapon the morning required.

"Put it there," Mrs. Mallory would say, without looking up, and Ruth would put it there, on the corner of the table where the toast went cold and the tea grew a skin.

By the spring Ruth had learned to bring the tray later, near eleven, and to bring a second cup in case Mr. Otley from the publisher's had already arrived. By the summer she had learned to read upside down.

She had not meant to. She had been taught her letters at the parish school in Bramford, well enough to read a Bible and a bill of sale, and her mother had said that was as much reading as a girl in service needed. But the pages lay on the table in long galley strips, and the words were large and black and very clean, and one morning while Mrs. Mallory was searching for her spectacles under a heap of correspondence, Ruth's eyes fell on a sentence and would not get up again.

*Agnes stood at the gate with her hands in her apron, and she did not wave, because she knew that if she waved he would turn, and if he turned she would not be able to bear his face.*

Ruth read it twice. Then Mrs. Mallory found her spectacles and said, "That will be all, Peale," and Ruth went down to the scullery and stood among the copper pans for a long moment with her hand pressed flat against her breastbone.

---

The novel was called *The Orchard at Haverleigh*, and it was appearing in monthly parts in *The Cornhill Companion*, which Ruth had never seen and could not have afforded. It concerned a family of declining gentry in Herefordshire, a disputed will, a clergyman with a secret, and, at the edge of all this, a dairymaid named Agnes Thorne, who was in love with the clergyman's younger brother.

Ruth learned all of this in pieces, the way a person learns the shape of a room in the dark. She read what lay faceup on the table. She read the discarded sheets that went into the grate on Wednesday mornings, smoothing them on her knee before she lit the fire. Once, God forgive her, she read an entire chapter that Mrs. Mallory had left on the stairs, sitting on the third step from the top with her duster in her lap and her heart going so hard she thought it would be heard in the drawing room.

She told no one. Who would she have told? Mrs. Briggs the cook could not read at all and thought novels were the reason the Mallorys' last girl had run off with a soldier. The boy who came for the ashes was eleven. There was only Ruth, and the long black lines of type, and Agnes Thorne standing at her gate.

---

It was in October that the trouble began.

Mr. Otley arrived at half past ten on a Tuesday, which was early for him. He was a narrow man with a moustache that seemed to be trying to escape his face, and he took the second cup of tea from Ruth's tray before she had set it down.

"Constance," he said. "We must speak about the third volume."

"We are always speaking about the third volume," said Mrs. Mallory. "It is the only volume anyone ever wishes to speak about. Nobody writes to me about the first."

"Nobody has reason to. The first is splendid. The second is splendid. The third—" He made a gesture with his teacup that slopped a little into the saucer. "The subscribers are restless. Mudie's has written."

Ruth, who had been dismissed in all but word, found that the coal scuttle by the door needed rearranging.

"Mudie's," said Mrs. Mallory, "may write to me directly, if they have opinions."

"They have one opinion, and it is mine. The girl, Constance. The dairymaid."

Ruth's hands stopped on the coal.

"What of her?"

"You cannot let her marry Edmund Ashby. A dairymaid and a gentleman's son. The libraries will not stand it, and the libraries are the market. You know this. You knew it when you began."

"I did not know it when I began," said Mrs. Mallory. "When I began, I did not know she would be so much better than everyone else in the book."

There was a silence.

"Then she must die," said Mr. Otley, quite gently, as a doctor says it. "That is the usual remedy. A fever, a fall, a good death in the last number. The readers will weep, and they will forgive you everything, and you will have the highest sales of your life. I have seen it done a dozen times."

"I have done it myself," said Mrs. Mallory. "Twice."

"Then a third time will come easily."

Ruth must have made a sound, because both of them turned.

"Peale," said Mrs. Mallory. "Is something the matter with the coal?"

"No, ma'am."

"Then perhaps you might leave it."

---

She did not sleep that night. She lay in the narrow bed under the eaves and listened to the house settle, and she thought about Agnes Thorne, who milked six cows before dawn and had a sister in the workhouse and had once, in the fourth number, walked eleven miles in the rain to bring the doctor to a child who was not hers. Agnes, who did not wave.

It was ridiculous. Ruth knew it was ridiculous. Agnes was ink and paper and the inside of a lady's head. She was no more real than the shepherdesses on the mantel clock. And yet Ruth found that she was crying, quietly, into her pillow, the way she had not cried since she left Bramford.

In the morning she carried the tray up at eleven, and found Mrs. Mallory at the window with the red pencil, and before she could think better of it she said, "Please, ma'am. Don't kill her."

The red pencil paused.

Mrs. Mallory turned in her chair, very slowly. She was a woman of perhaps fifty, with a long, clever, tired face and grey eyes that were rather too large for it. Ruth had never before been looked at by them directly, not for so long.

"Kill whom, Peale?"

There was no going back now. Ruth set the tray down because her hands had begun to shake.

"Agnes, ma'am. Agnes Thorne."

"I see." Mrs. Mallory took off her spectacles and folded them. "And how is it that you know Agnes Thorne?"

"I read her, ma'am. Off the table. And the grate. I'm sorry. I know I shouldn't have. You can give me my notice, only please don't kill her because of what Mr. Otley said. She doesn't deserve it."

"Deserve," said Mrs. Mallory. She said it as if she were tasting it. "Sit down, Peale."

"Ma'am?"

"There is a chair. Sit in it. I am not going to give you notice; I am going to ask you questions, and I cannot do that with you standing over me like a constable."

Ruth sat, on the very edge of the chair.

"What is your Christian name?"

"Ruth, ma'am."

"Ruth. How much of the book have you read?"

"Most of it, I think, ma'am. Some bits are missing. I never found out what was in the letter Mr. Ashby burned."

"Nobody has found that out. I have not decided." Mrs. Mallory leaned back. "Tell me why she does not deserve it."

Ruth opened her mouth and closed it. She had never been asked her opinion of anything more important than whether the fish was fresh.

"Because she's good, ma'am," she said at last. "Not good like the curate's wife, who's good where people can see. Agnes is good when there's nobody about. And she never asks for anything. She walked all that way in the rain for the Pellew baby and she never told anyone, not even Edmund. And if she dies, it's like saying—" She stopped.

"Go on."

"It's like saying the only thing a girl like her can do in a book is die nicely so the ladies can cry." Ruth felt her face go hot. "Begging your pardon."

Mrs. Mallory said nothing for so long that Ruth began to rise.

"Sit," said Mrs. Mallory. "I am thinking."

---

The next Tuesday, Mrs. Mallory came down to breakfast.

Mrs. Briggs nearly dropped the kidneys. Ruth, who was laying the table, had to set the butter dish down very carefully.

"Peale," said Mrs. Mallory, taking her chair, "when you have finished with the dining room, come up to the study. Bring a pencil. Mrs. Briggs, you will have to manage the upstairs dusting without her this morning, I'm afraid."

"Without her, madam?"

"I require her for literary purposes," said Mrs. Mallory, and buttered her toast, and did not explain.

In the study, there was a second chair drawn up to the writing table. Mrs. Mallory pushed a stack of foolscap toward it.

"This is the fourteenth number," she said. "Edmund has learned that his brother forged the codicil. Agnes has learned that she is to be turned out of her place at Michaelmas, because Mrs. Ashby suspects. I have written it three ways. In one, she takes a fever from nursing her sister and dies on the first of October with Edmund holding her hand. Mr. Otley will like that one very much."

"And the others, ma'am?"

"In the second, she marries Edmund, and they are cut off, and go to live in Birmingham, and are poor and happy. Mr. Otley says that one will be laughed at. He is probably right. It reads like a pantomime. Happy poverty always does, when one writes it from a house with three servants."

Ruth, who had grown up in two rooms with five siblings, privately agreed.

"And the third?"

Mrs. Mallory looked at the window, where the London fog was pressing its grey face to the glass.

"In the third, she does not marry him at all."

"She doesn't?"

"She refuses him. She understands precisely what it would cost him, and she is not willing to be the cost. And she goes away. Not to die. To work. And the book ends without her, and the reader does not know where she went." Mrs. Mallory turned back. "I cannot decide whether it is the truest thing I have ever written or the cruellest."

"Can I read it, ma'am?"

"That," said Mrs. Mallory, "is why you are here."

---

Ruth read all three. It took her the better part of the morning, because she read slowly, moving her finger under the lines, and because Mrs. Mallory's hand was worse than the printer's type, all loops and slashes. Mrs. Mallory did not hurry her. She sat at the other end of the table and answered letters, and every so often she glanced over, and once she said, "That word is 'obdurate,' not 'obdurant,'" without being asked.

When Ruth had finished, she put the pages down in three neat piles and folded her hands.

"Well?" said Mrs. Mallory.

"The first one's very beautiful, ma'am. I cried."

"That is rather the point of it."

"But it's not her. It's—" Ruth searched. "It's like a picture of her somebody painted after. She'd never just lie there and let them all be sad over her. She'd be getting up to see if the cows were milked."

Mrs. Mallory's mouth twitched.

"And the second?"

"Mr. Otley's right about that one, ma'am. Begging your pardon. There's nobody in Birmingham that happy."

"And the third."

Ruth looked at the third pile for a long time.

"It's true," she said finally. "It's what she'd do. Only—"

"Only?"

"Only you've made it so she's sad and noble about it. Like she's giving him up for his sake and it's breaking her heart. And maybe it is. But you never let her want anything else."

Mrs. Mallory set down her pen.

"Explain."

Ruth struggled. "In chapter nine. When she's at the fair. And she sees the lady in the stall selling ribbons, the one that's got her own shop in Hereford. And Agnes stands there ever so long looking at her. You never come back to that. I always thought you would. I thought that was what Agnes wanted, really, underneath. Not Edmund. Not to be a lady. Just to have a thing that was hers that nobody could turn her out of at Michaelmas."

The fog shifted against the window.

"Good God," said Mrs. Mallory, very quietly. "The ribbon woman."

"I'm sorry, ma'am, I didn't mean—"

"Be quiet a moment, Ruth."

Mrs. Mallory pulled a clean sheet toward her and began to write, very fast, the pen scratching like a mouse in a wainscot. Ruth sat as still as she could. After ten minutes Mrs. Mallory stopped, read what she had written, crossed out a line, and read it again.

"I wrote that scene," she said, not looking up, "in the summer of last year, in Malvern, in a hired room with a bad chimney. I put the ribbon woman in because I needed a reason for Agnes to be standing still when Edmund came round the corner. That is all she was. A reason to stand still." She looked up. "And you saw that she was the whole book."

"I didn't, ma'am. I only thought—"

"You thought what Agnes would think. Which I, apparently, did not." Mrs. Mallory gave a short laugh, without much humour in it. "Thirty years I have been doing this, and I have never once asked a dairymaid what she wanted."

"I'm not a dairymaid, ma'am."

"No. But you are nearer one than Mr. Otley."

---

Mr. Otley came on the following Tuesday and was given the fourteenth number to read in the drawing room, with a fresh pot of tea and a plate of Mrs. Briggs's seed cake. Ruth served it. She was not sent away, and so she stood by the sideboard with her hands folded and tried not to watch his face.

He read for twenty minutes. Once he said, "Hm." Once he turned back a page. When he had finished, he set the sheets down on his knee and looked at Mrs. Mallory over his spectacles for a long moment.

"She refuses him."

"She does."

"And goes to Hereford. And takes a position with this ribbon person. Mrs.—"

"Mrs. Gedge."

"Mrs. Gedge. With a view to purchasing a share in the business when she has saved enough." He took off his spectacles. "Constance, this is not an ending. Nobody dies. Nobody marries. A girl gets a job in a shop."

"Yes."

"Mudie's will not know what to do with it."

"Then Mudie's," said Mrs. Mallory, "may learn."

Mr. Otley looked down at the pages again. His moustache did something complicated.

"The last paragraph," he said. "Where Edmund rides past the shop, a year later, and sees her through the window, and she is measuring out ribbon for a farmer's wife, and she looks up, and she—"

"Waves," said Mrs. Mallory.

"Waves. And he rides on." Mr. Otley cleared his throat. "That is rather good, actually."

"It is the best thing I have ever written," said Mrs. Mallory, "and I did not write it alone."

Mr. Otley glanced around the room, as though expecting a collaborator to emerge from behind the curtains. His eyes passed over Ruth at the sideboard without stopping.

Mrs. Mallory's did not.

---

*The Orchard at Haverleigh* was published in three volumes in the spring of the following year. The reviewers were divided. The *Athenaeum* called the ending "perverse and unsatisfying." A clergyman in Leeds preached a sermon against it. Mudie's took four hundred copies, then another six hundred, then wrote to ask whether Mrs. Mallory might consider a sequel concerning the later fortunes of Miss Thorne.

Mrs. Mallory did not write a sequel. But on the first Tuesday of every month thereafter, a second chair was drawn up to the writing table by the window, and Mrs. Briggs managed the upstairs dusting alone, and complained about it, and was quietly given a rise in wages to stop.

On the flyleaf of the first copy of the first edition, which Ruth Peale kept for the rest of her long life, first in a tin trunk under the eaves on Ellery Street and later on the shelf above the counter of a small draper's shop in Hereford, there was an inscription in a hand all loops and slashes:

*For Ruth, who would not let her die nicely.*
