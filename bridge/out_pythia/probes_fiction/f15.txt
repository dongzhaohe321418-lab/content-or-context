**The Name on the Card**

The first time Walter Pruitt saw his name forty feet wide, he was sitting in the eleventh row of the Pantages with a box of Jujubes melting in his fist, and he thought he might be sick.

SCREENPLAY BY WALTER PRUITT.

The letters were white and serifed and floated over a matte painting of a desert canyon, the kind of red that only existed in Technicolor and in the dreams of men who had never been to Arizona. Then the card dissolved and the canyon went on without him, and a stagecoach came rattling around the bend, and the picture began.

Beside him, Ruth leaned over and whispered, "You're squeezing them to death."

He opened his hand. The Jujubes had fused into a single amber lump.

"Sorry," he said.

"Don't be sorry. Be happy. That's you up there."

It wasn't him, of course. That was the whole point. But he nodded, and she squeezed his arm, and for an hour and forty minutes he watched a picture called *The Long Quiet* and tried to look like a man who knew what was coming next.

---

It had started fourteen months earlier, in the back booth of a delicatessen on Fairfax, where Walter had been eating a pastrami sandwich and reading the classifieds for jobs he was not qualified to hold. He was twenty-nine. He had sold insurance, then encyclopedias, then nothing at all. He had a degree in English from a college in Ohio that nobody in California had heard of, and a drawer full of short stories that had come back from *The Saturday Evening Post* with notes so polite they hurt.

A man slid into the booth across from him without asking. He was perhaps fifty, with a long face and heavy eyebrows and a cardigan buttoned wrong.

"You're Pruitt," the man said. "Ellie Sobel's cousin."

"Second cousin."

"Close enough. She says you can keep your mouth shut."

Walter set down his sandwich. "Ellie says a lot of things."

"She also says you need money." The man took a cigarette from a flat silver case and didn't light it, only turned it in his fingers. "My name is Abe Kessler. You might have seen it somewhere."

Walter had. Everyone had. Abe Kessler had written *Harbor Lights* and *A Song for Ginny* and the picture with the boxer that won the Oscar in '47, the one where Walter's mother had cried so hard in the Dayton Strand that an usher brought her a glass of water. And then, two years ago, Abe Kessler had sat in front of a committee in Washington and declined to name the people he'd gone to meetings with in 1938, and after that his name hadn't been anywhere at all.

"I'm not in trouble," Walter said, which was a strange thing to say, and he knew it as soon as it was out.

"No," Kessler agreed. "You're not. That's what makes you useful."

---

The arrangement was simple, the way the most dangerous things often are.

Kessler wrote. Walter typed, so the pages would be in Walter's hand, on Walter's machine—a secondhand Royal he bought with the first envelope of cash. Walter took the meetings. Walter sat in the outer office at Meridian Pictures under a framed poster of Rita Hayworth and waited for a producer named Lou Feldman to call him in, and then he sat across a desk the size of a rowboat and nodded while Feldman said things like *the second act sags* and *can we get a dog in this*.

"A dog," Kessler said that night, in the kitchen of the little house in Silver Lake where he lived with his wife and his books and the curtains always drawn. "He wants a dog."

"I told him I'd think about it."

"Good. That's good. Always tell them you'll think about it." Kessler rubbed his eyes. "All right. We'll give him a dog. The dog dies in the third reel."

"Can it do that?"

"Walter, in this business a dog can do anything except win an argument with Lou Feldman."

That was the first time Walter laughed in that house. Kessler's wife, Miriam, looked up from the sink, surprised, and then she laughed too, and after that it was a little easier to come by.

---

He learned to lie in small, specific ways.

When Feldman asked where he'd studied, he said Ohio, which was true. When Feldman asked what he'd written before, he said short stories, which was also true, and when Feldman asked where they'd been published, Walter said, "Mostly in drawers," and Feldman laughed so hard he had to take off his glasses, and that became the story around the lot: *Pruitt, the kid from Ohio, funny guy, came out of nowhere.*

The hard part wasn't the lying. The hard part was the talking.

"They'll want you to explain things," Kessler told him. "Why the sheriff doesn't draw first. Why the girl leaves in the end. You have to know. You can't look at the page like it's a stranger's."

So they sat up nights, the two of them, with the Royal between them on the kitchen table, and Kessler walked him through every scene. Not just what happened, but why. Why the sheriff—his name was Cole Harlan, and he was going to be played by a big, sad-eyed actor named Dane Rutledge—kept his gun on the table during the long dinner scene. Why the town never said the name of the man who was coming on the noon train.

"Because they all know it," Kessler said. "They all know, and none of them will say it. That's the picture, Walter. That's the whole picture. A town full of decent people who know exactly what's coming and decide it's somebody else's business."

Walter looked at him across the table.

"Feldman will think it's about a gunfighter," he said.

"Feldman," said Kessler, "is welcome to think so."

---

He met Ruth during the second week of shooting. She was a script girl, which meant she sat in a canvas chair beside the camera with a stopwatch and a binder and wrote down everything—which hand Rutledge held his hat in, how much coffee was left in the cup, whether the clock on the saloon wall said ten past or quarter past. She had black hair pinned up under a scarf and a pencil behind each ear, and the first thing she ever said to him was, "You're standing in the eyeline."

"I'm sorry—the what?"

"The eyeline. Dane's supposed to be looking at the door. He's looking at you." She pointed with one of the pencils. "Move left about four feet. Or be a door."

He moved left.

Later, at lunch, she found him on the steps of the saloon set eating a sandwich from the craft table.

"You wrote the dinner scene," she said.

There it was. He'd known it would come, the first time someone he liked asked him about the work. He'd practiced. He'd practiced in the bathroom mirror.

"I did," he said.

"It's the best thing I've read in three years." She sat down two steps below him, sideways, so she could look up at him. "The part where Harlan says, *A man can be polite to the devil if he's only staying for supper.* I read that twice. I don't read anything twice. I don't have time."

Walter had typed that line at two in the morning while Kessler paced the kitchen in his socks, muttering it over and over, trying different words in the middle. *Civil to the devil. Kind to the devil. Polite.*

"Thank you," Walter said, and it was the worst thing he had ever said, and it was only two words.

---

They went to dinner. They went to the beach at Santa Monica and walked until the fog came in. She told him about growing up in Bakersfield with four brothers who all worked oil; she told him she wanted to direct someday, and then laughed before he could, so he wouldn't have to.

"Women don't direct," she said. "Ida Lupino, and look how they talk about her."

"I wasn't going to laugh."

"Everybody laughs."

"I wasn't."

She looked at him then, in the gray light, with the water coming in behind her, and he understood that she believed him, and that she believed everything else too. That every time she looked at him she was seeing the man who had written *a man can be polite to the devil*. That she was, in some way he couldn't get his hands around, a little in love with Abe Kessler and didn't know it.

He went to Silver Lake that night and sat in the car outside the house for twenty minutes before he went in.

Kessler was at the table, reading galley pages of something under the lamp. He didn't look up.

"You've met a girl," he said.

"How do you—"

"You've got sand on your shoes and you look like a man who swallowed a lightbulb." Now he looked up. "Is she on the picture?"

"Script supervisor."

Kessler took off his reading glasses and folded them very carefully.

"Walter," he said. "I like you. Miriam likes you. So I am going to say this one time, as a friend. You cannot tell her."

"I know."

"You don't know. You think you know. You think, she's a good girl, she'd never tell anybody. And maybe she wouldn't. But someday somebody's going to sit her down in a room with a flag in it and ask her what she knows, and the only way she's safe is if the answer is nothing." He set the glasses on the galleys. "It's not my neck I'm worried about. My neck's already been wrung. It's yours. And now it's hers."

---

The picture wrapped in October. Feldman threw a party on Stage Nine with a cake shaped like a stagecoach, and Rutledge got drunk and sang "Red River Valley" with his arm around the boom operator, and Ruth danced with Walter to a record of Patti Page while the grips struck the saloon behind them, wall by wall.

"What's next for the kid from Ohio?" Feldman asked him at the end of the night, his tie loose, his face red and happy. "I've got a thing at Fox wants a writer. War picture. Submarines."

"I'll think about it," Walter said.

"That's what I like about you, Pruitt. You think."

---

And now the premiere, and the Pantages, and his name forty feet wide.

He watched the dinner scene. He watched Rutledge set the gun on the table beside the potatoes. When Harlan said, *A man can be polite to the devil if he's only staying for supper,* a ripple went through the theater—not a laugh exactly, more like a held breath let out—and Ruth's hand found his in the dark and held on.

He thought about Kessler, who was not here. Who could not be here. Who was at this moment, Walter knew, sitting in the kitchen in Silver Lake with the curtains drawn and the radio on, not listening to it.

He watched the town refuse to say the name. He watched the noon train come in. He watched Cole Harlan walk down the middle of an empty street while behind every window a decent person closed the shutters. The dog had died in the third reel, as promised. The woman in the gingham dress came out at the very end, after the shooting, and stood beside Harlan and said nothing, and that was the only time in the picture that saying nothing was the brave thing.

When the lights came up, people stood and applauded. It went on a long time. Feldman, in the front row, turned around and found Walter and pointed at him with both hands, grinning, and people turned to look, and Walter stood because he didn't know what else to do, and the applause got louder.

He could feel it on his skin like heat from a stove. It was not meant for him. It went through him and kept going, out the doors and into the street and up into the hills, and he hoped some of it reached the kitchen in Silver Lake, and he knew none of it would.

---

Afterward, on the sidewalk under the marquee, with the searchlights sweeping the clouds, Ruth pulled him into the shadow of a doorway.

"You're shaking," she said.

"It's cold."

"It's seventy degrees." She put her hands flat on his chest. "Walter. What's wrong?"

He looked at her. He thought of the kitchen. He thought of a room with a flag in it.

"Nothing's wrong," he said.

She didn't move her hands. She was a script girl; it was her job to notice when the clock on the wall had changed between takes, when a hat had moved from the left hand to the right. She looked at him for a long moment, and he watched her see something, though he never knew exactly what.

"Okay," she said finally. "Okay. You don't have to tell me."

"There's nothing to tell."

"I said okay." She took his arm. "Let's go get a drink before Lou finds you and makes you talk about submarines."

---

He went to Silver Lake the next morning with the reviews. *Variety* called it "a taut, intelligent oater with more on its mind than most." The *Times* said the screenplay was "spare, wise, and quietly furious," and singled out "newcomer Walter Pruitt" as "a writer to watch."

Kessler read them at the kitchen table in his bathrobe. Miriam stood behind him with her hand on his shoulder. When he got to *quietly furious* he made a small sound, and Walter couldn't tell if it was a laugh.

"Newcomer," Kessler said. "That's nice. I always wanted to be a newcomer."

"Abe, I—"

"Don't." He folded the paper. "Don't apologize. This is what we wanted. It worked." He pushed the paper across the table. "Keep these. Your mother will want them."

"They're yours."

"They have your name on them, Walter. They're yours. That's how it works." He stood, and pulled his robe tighter, and went to the window, and for the first time since Walter had known him, he opened the curtains. Light came into the kitchen, thin and yellow. "Feldman said submarines?"

"Fox. A war picture."

Kessler stood looking out at the street for a long time.

"I've never been on a submarine," he said. "Have you?"

"No."

"Well." He turned around. There was something in his face Walter hadn't seen before—not happiness, but something next door to it. "I suppose we'll have to think about it."

---

Years later—after the blacklist broke, after Kessler's name went back on the pictures and then on a little brass plaque at the Writers Guild, after Walter had written six films of his own, real ones, typed on the same old Royal—a young reporter asked him about *The Long Quiet*. Everybody knew by then. It had been in all the books.

"Did it bother you?" the reporter asked. "Taking the credit?"

Walter was sixty. Ruth was in the next room, on the phone with a cameraman about a picture she was directing in the spring. He could hear her laughing.

"Taking it, no," he said. "Keeping it. That bothered me."

"What's the difference?"

He thought about the Pantages. The applause going through him like heat. A woman's hands flat on his chest in a doorway, deciding not to ask.

"The difference," Walter said, "is about twenty years. And one dog."

The reporter wrote that down, though he didn't understand it, and Walter didn't explain. Some things a town knows, and doesn't say. He'd learned that from the best.
