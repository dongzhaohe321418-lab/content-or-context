**The Overdue Book**

The letter from the Development Office called it a Gaudy, which Margaret Ilse had forgotten was a word. *Members who matriculated between 1984 and 1986 are warmly invited to return to Halloran College for dinner in Hall, followed by drinks in the Senior Common Room.* Below that was a smaller line about a giving campaign for the new library extension, and below that, in what looked like real ink, someone had written: *We do hope you'll come. —R.*

She did not know an R. She suspected the R did not know her either, and had signed two hundred of these at a long table with a cup of tea going cold. Still, the ink had done something the printed words had not. She put the letter on the kitchen windowsill, where it stayed for three weeks. Then she went upstairs, opened the bottom drawer of the chest in the spare room, and took out the book.

It was a library copy of Hopkins's poems, the 1967 Gardner and MacKenzie edition, bound in the dull green buckram the college library had used for everything in those years. On the spine was a white label with a shelfmark inked by hand: HAL 821.8 HOP. On the inside of the front cover was a pocket with a card in it, and on the card was a column of stamped dates. The last was 14 JUN 1986.

She had meant to return it. For about a decade she had meant to return it actively. After that she had kept meaning to in a more theoretical way, the way you mean to learn Italian.

---

The porters' lodge had been rebuilt. In her day it was a dim wooden hutch smelling of paraffin heaters and wet coats, with pigeonholes floor to ceiling and a hatch you leaned through to ask for your key. Now it was glass, with a touchscreen for visitors and a small bowl of wrapped mints. Behind the counter, a young man in a waistcoat was explaining the Wi-Fi to two men in their late fifties who seemed to resent having to learn it.

"Name?" he said when it was her turn, kindly enough.

"Ilse. Margaret. I was here eighty-four to — well. Eighty-four."

He scrolled. "Ilse, Ilse. Here you are. English, matriculated 1984." He paused and frowned slightly at the screen, the way people do when a database has said something they'd rather not repeat aloud. "Dinner's at seven-thirty. Drinks on the lawn from six. You're in staircase four tonight, if you're staying. Room eleven."

"Staircase four," she said. "Is that the one by the chapel?"

"The one by the chapel is three. Four's the one with the wisteria."

She knew which one had the wisteria. She had lived on it, in room eleven as it happened. She almost said so, then didn't. Somebody had either been thoughtful or hadn't been, and she couldn't decide which would be worse to find out.

---

The room was smaller than she remembered and a great deal cleaner. There was a kettle, a hairdryer in a drawstring bag, and a laminated card about fire procedures. The window still stuck at the same point, two-thirds of the way up, and you still had to lift and pull towards yourself to get it past. Her hands did it before she thought about it.

She stood with the window open and the book in her handbag and looked down at the quad. The lawn was full of people in summer suits and linen dresses, holding glasses at the angle people hold glasses when they are mainly holding them for something to do. She tried to find faces she knew and found that she could do it the way you find constellations: a set of shoulders, a laugh carrying across the grass, a way of standing with one hand in a pocket. Fat Tom Esterházy, who wasn't fat now and possibly never had been, only at nineteen they had all been cruel and imprecise. The Coulter twins, still dressed alike, which must be on purpose by now. A tall woman with cropped white hair whom she could not place at all, until the woman turned and Margaret saw the gap between her front teeth and thought: *Priya. God. Priya Ramesh.*

She did not see Ann, because Ann would not be there. She had known that when she put the letter on the windowsill and known it when she took the book out of the drawer. Still, she looked for her anyway, for the length of a breath, the way you reach for a stair that isn't there.

---

The library extension was not built yet, but the old library was still where it had always been, up a stone staircase off the second quad, and it was open. A sign said *Alumni welcome 2–6 p.m. Please do not remove books from the Reading Room.* It was twenty past five.

Inside, the smell was the same: dust, floor polish, and the sweetish rot of old paper. That smell had not been rebuilt. At the issue desk sat a librarian who could not have been more than thirty, with an enamel pin of a cat on her cardigan and a laptop open in front of her. She looked up and smiled the smile of someone who had been smiling at alumni since two o'clock.

"Hello. Are you looking for anything in particular? We've got a little display on the Gaudy years in the alcove, college magazines and so on —"

"I've come to return something," Margaret said.

She put the book on the desk.

The librarian looked at it. Then she picked it up, turned it over, opened the cover, and found the card in its pocket. Margaret watched her read the last date. She watched her do the arithmetic, and then do it again, as if the first answer had been a mistake.

"Nineteen eighty-six," the librarian said.

"Yes. I'm sorry."

"No, it's —" The librarian laughed, a real one, surprised out of her. "Sorry. It's just that we did a stock check in 2009 and this would have been written off. You're not in trouble. I don't think the system even knows it exists any more." She tapped at the laptop. "No. It doesn't. As far as Halloran College is concerned, this book is a ghost."

"What would the fine have been?"

"Oh, God. Back then? Two pence a day, I think. Maybe five." She frowned at the ceiling. "Forty years, three hundred and sixty-five days … call it fourteen and a half thousand days. At two pence that's —"

"Two hundred and ninety-two pounds," Margaret said. "I worked it out on the train."

The librarian looked at her with new interest. "You worked it out on the train?"

"I worked it out a lot of times. Over the years. The figure used to frighten me. Then it went past the point where it could frighten me, and it got sort of funny. And then at some point it stopped being about the money at all." She found she had put her hand flat on the cover, as though the book might leave without her. "Is there a way to pay it? I'd like to pay it."

"There isn't, really. There's nothing to pay it *into*." The librarian hesitated. "There's the library campaign. The extension. If you wanted."

"Then I'll do that."

"You really don't have to."

"I know," said Margaret.

The librarian had been leafing through the pages as they spoke, idly, the way librarians handle books, and now she stopped. "Oh," she said. "Someone's written in it."

"Yes."

"Quite a lot."

"Yes," Margaret said. "That's why it's late."

---

Ann Delacourt had been the first person Margaret met at Halloran, at the foot of staircase four on the first Sunday of October 1984, both of them carrying cardboard boxes, both of them lost. Ann had a Glaswegian accent she would spend a year trying to lose and then two years deliberately getting back. She smoked roll-ups out of the stuck window of room eleven because her own window on the floor above faced the Dean's garden. She read poetry the way other people ate when they'd been ill, with a kind of grateful fury, and she wrote in the margins of everything. Library books, her own books, Margaret's books. Once, a menu.

"You're not supposed to do that," Margaret had told her, the first time she saw the pencil come out over a library copy of Donne.

"It's pencil," Ann said.

"It's still not allowed."

"Somebody's going to take this out in thirty years," Ann said, "and they're going to be eighteen and miserable and they won't understand a word of it, and they'll find me here going *this bit's about sex, by the way,* and they'll feel less alone. That's a public service. That's practically a scholarship."

In the summer term of their second year, Ann had borrowed the Hopkins for her finals reading, which was a year early, which was very Ann. She carried it everywhere. She annotated it until it was nearly two books, his and hers. By June it lived on the floor of Margaret's room, because Ann lived there more or less too. They had plans, the two of them, which they made out loud at two in the morning: a flat in Edinburgh, a little press, something with a shop. They would publish people nobody else would publish, and they would be poor and it would be glorious.

On the eleventh of June, Ann went home to Glasgow for her grandmother's eightieth, meaning to be back by Tuesday. On the motorway outside Carlisle a lorry crossed the central reservation. That was all. It was the kind of thing that was in the paper for one day, and then not.

The book had been in Margaret's room. It had been stamped out on the fourteenth, three days after, because Margaret had gone to the issue desk without thinking and renewed it in Ann's name. The librarian at the time, an elderly man called Mr. Pell, had stamped it without a word. She had always wondered whether he knew.

She did not sit her second-year exams. She went home in the third week of June and didn't come back in October, and the college wrote her a series of increasingly gentle letters and then stopped. She worked in a bookshop in Leeds, then in a bigger bookshop in Manchester, then for a publisher, then, for twenty-two years, ran a small press in a converted chapel outside Hebden Bridge that published people nobody else would publish. They were poor. It was, much of the time, glorious.

She never had the book rebound or erased. She never lent it. Some years she didn't open it at all. Other years she read it in a single sitting, Ann's pencil and Hopkins's ink going back and forth across the pages like two people talking at once.

---

"Can I read some?" the librarian asked. "Sorry. Is that weird?"

"No. That's what it's for."

The librarian turned to "The Windhover" and read the margin aloud, quietly, in the empty library.

"*'Buckle' — does he mean collapse or fasten? BOTH. He means both. Show this to M., she'll say I'm overreading. I'm not overreading.*" She looked up. "Is that you? M.?"

"That's me."

"Did you say she was overreading?"

"I said it constantly. She was right about 'buckle', though. I came round to it in about 1994."

The librarian turned more pages. Every few she stopped, read, and smiled, or didn't. Near the back, in "The Leaden Echo and the Golden Echo," she stopped for a long time.

"There's one in pen here," she said. "Different handwriting."

"Yes," said Margaret. "That one's mine."

It was dated 1999. Margaret did not need to look at it. It said: *You'd have hated the press's first catalogue. The font's all wrong. I kept your name off the colophon because you'd have argued about the font for a year and I had to get it to the printer. It's called Delacourt Books anyway. I hope that's all right.*

The librarian closed the book carefully, the way you'd close a door on someone sleeping.

"I don't think I can put this back on the shelf," she said.

"No. I didn't think so."

"I mean — I could. Technically. I'd have to re-accession it. But someone would take it out and they'd have no idea, and they'd rub it all out to be helpful, or they'd lose it." She looked down at the green cover. "We have a special collections room. It's mostly letters and photographs. College history. I could ask the archivist."

"It isn't history," Margaret said, and then heard herself and thought: *Well. It is now, rather.* "Sorry. Yes. Ask the archivist."

"Was she — would she be in the register? For the display?" The librarian nodded towards the alcove. "We put out the matriculation photographs."

---

The photographs were in a glass case, three long panoramic prints, one for each year. 1984 was in the middle. Two hundred and twelve students in rows on the steps of Hall, in gowns, squinting into an October sun. The names were typed underneath on a strip of card, faded to the colour of tea.

Margaret found herself almost at once, back row, fourth from the left, ridiculously young, with a fringe she had cut herself. She had expected that to hurt and it didn't. It was like looking at a niece.

Next to her, half a head shorter, mouth open because she'd been talking when the shutter went, was Ann.

Margaret looked at the strip of names. DELACOURT, A. ILSE, M. There they were in alphabetical order, side by side, which they had never been on any list in life. Someone had put a small pencilled cross beside Ann's name, very faint. She wondered how long it had been there, and who had done it, and whether it had been Mr. Pell.

Beside her own name there was nothing. No cross, no note, no degree. Just ILSE, M., present on the steps on a bright day and then, as far as the paperwork was concerned, gone somewhere.

"You could graduate, you know," said a voice behind her.

It was the tall woman with the white hair. Priya Ramesh, holding two glasses of wine and offering her one.

"I saw you go in," Priya said. "I thought it was you on the lawn and I couldn't think of your surname, and then I could, and then you vanished up the library stairs like a woman going to confession. I was going to give you twenty minutes." She nodded at the photograph. "They do it, you know. Retrospective degrees. My husband's on the governing body, God help us all. If you did the first two years, there's a thing. You'd get an ordinary BA. You'd get to wear the hood."

"I don't want the hood."

"Nobody wants the hood. The hood is hideous. You'd get to sit at High Table, though, once. They give you pudding wine."

Margaret laughed for the first time that day, and it came out of her strangely, like something she'd put down a long time ago and had just found was lighter than she remembered.

"Do you remember Ann?" she asked.

Priya looked at the photograph. "Delacourt. Of course. She told me Larkin was a coward and I wasn't to read him, so obviously I read nothing else for a year." She was quiet a moment. "She wrote in my Larkin, actually. I'd forgotten. Every time he said something particularly miserable she'd written *BUCK UP PHILIP* in the margin. I've still got it. I think it's in the loft."

"You should give it to the library," said Margaret. "They're starting a collection."

---

At dinner, the Master said the grace in Latin, very fast, and the speech in English, rather slowly. He mentioned the extension. He mentioned the many distinguished careers represented in the room tonight. He asked everyone to stand for a moment in memory of members of their years who were no longer with them, and read the names, and when he came to *Ann Delacourt* he pronounced it wrong, with the stress on the second syllable, the way an Englishman would.

Margaret stood with everyone else and did not correct him. Ann would have. Ann would have done it from her seat, loudly, in the accent. Margaret thought about that, and it was enough to keep her face where it needed to be until they all sat down.

Afterwards, in the Senior Common Room, the young librarian found her by the fireplace, still wearing the cat pin, now with a lanyard over the cardigan.

"The archivist said yes," she said. "Special collections. She wants to know what to call it in the catalogue."

Margaret thought about it. Outside the tall windows the quad was going blue, and somebody's laugh carried across the lawn, and up on staircase four the window of room eleven was still open two-thirds of the way, because she'd forgotten to shut it, the way you could forget anything for forty years if you tried.

"Put Hopkins," she said. "Annotated. Two hands." She paused. "And put both our names. Alphabetical. She'd want to go first."
