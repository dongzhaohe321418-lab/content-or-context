**The Count of Arrows**

Theodoros had been hired to count things, and so he counted.

He counted the mules on the road east from Theodosiopolis: four hundred and twelve on the morning they crossed the river, four hundred and nine by evening. One had gone lame, one had been stolen by Pecheneg cavalry who swore it had wandered into their lines of its own will, and one had simply vanished. He counted the bundles of arrows, each of fifty shafts, bound with gut and stamped on the binding with the seal of the imperial arsenal. He counted sacks of barley, jars of oil, horseshoes, nails for horseshoes, and the coils of rope that the engineers swore they needed and never used. At night, by a lamp he had to beg oil for, he wrote the numbers on wax tablets. When the tablets filled he copied them onto parchment in a small, careful hand, and the parchment went into a cedar box that had belonged to his father.

His father had also counted things, though in Constantinople, where the things stayed put. That had been his father's great joy. He was a man of the warehouses along the Golden Horn, and he said a sack of grain was the most honest creature God had made. Theodoros had believed this until the army.

"You write too much," said Gagik, on the eleventh day.

Gagik was Armenian, a spearman from somewhere around Ani, with a beard gone the color of ash and a habit of eating raw onions as though they were apples. He had been assigned to guard the baggage, which he took as an insult and carried out with enormous diligence. He sat on an overturned crate beside Theodoros's lamp, chewing.

"I write what I'm paid to write," Theodoros said.

"Who reads it?"

"The logothete's office."

"In the City."

"Yes."

Gagik considered this. "So you count arrows here, and a eunuch counts your numbers there, and between the two of you nobody has shot anything."

"Somebody has to know how many there are."

"I know how many there are." Gagik spat a fragment of onion skin into the dark. "Not enough. There are never enough. That's the only number a soldier needs."

---

The emperor rode past the baggage train twice in those weeks, and both times Theodoros forgot to count anything at all.

Romanos Diogenes was not a beautiful man. He was thick through the shoulders, broad in the face, and plainly a soldier before he was anything else. When he passed, men straightened without being told. He wore the purple boots, but they were muddy like anyone's. On the second occasion he reined in beside the wagons and asked a question about the horseshoes, a real question, not one made to sound interested. Theodoros answered it with his voice cracking, and the emperor nodded and said, "Good," and rode on.

"You see," Theodoros said afterward. "Somebody does read it."

Gagik shrugged. "He asked about horseshoes. He didn't ask about you."

But Theodoros noticed that the old man watched the emperor go for a long time, and that he did not eat his onion until the dust had settled.

---

The army was the largest Theodoros had ever imagined, and also, he slowly understood, not quite one thing. It was a city that had packed itself up and walked. Like a city it had neighborhoods that did not like one another.

There were the tagmata, the professional regiments, with their lamellar armor polished until it shone like fish scales. There were the Armenian levies, Gagik's people, who spoke their own tongue among themselves and fell quiet when Greeks came near. There were Frankish mercenaries under a Norman named Roussel, tall men with kite-shaped shields who laughed at jokes no one else understood. There were the Pechenegs and the Uzes, horse-archers from the steppe, who rode as though they had been born in the saddle and who, Theodoros heard, were cousins of a kind to the very Turks they had come to fight.

And there was the rearguard, under Andronikos Doukas.

"Remember that name," Gagik said, one evening near the end, when they could see the mountains around Lake Van rising blue to the south.

"Why?"

"Because his uncle was emperor before this one. Because his family wanted this one to fail before he ever put on the boots. Because a man doesn't stop being a Doukas when you hand him a command." Gagik lowered his voice, though no one was near. "If I were the emperor I would keep that man where I could see him. Not behind me."

"You're not the emperor."

"No," Gagik said. "God is merciful to the empire in that, at least."

---

They came to Manzikert in the middle of August. The fortress fell back into Roman hands almost before Theodoros had finished unloading the siege ropes, which he noted, with some private satisfaction, had still not been used. The garrison surrendered. There was wine that night, and singing in four languages. Theodoros counted the casks the quartermaster opened and wrote the figure down. Then, because Gagik was laughing at him, he drank from one of them.

The emperor had divided his forces before they arrived. A great part of the army had gone off toward Khliat under other commanders, Roussel's Franks among them. Theodoros recorded the supplies that went with them, page after page. He did not think about what it meant. A clerk does not think about strategy. He thinks about whether the barley will last.

Two days later, foragers began to come back wounded.

At first there were a few, and then more. They had arrows in them of a kind Theodoros had never seen, short and thick, with heads shaped like leaves. The surgeons cut them out and threw them in a pile, and Theodoros, not knowing what else to do, counted the pile. Thirty-one. Fifty-six. Ninety.

"Turks," said Gagik. He picked one up and turned it in his fingers. "The sultan himself, maybe. Alp Arslan. They said he was in Syria."

"They said wrong."

"They usually do."

The Pechenegs did not come back from a scouting ride. Or rather some of them did, but they were riding the wrong way, on the far side of the plain, toward the Turkish fires. Theodoros heard the news from a cook, who heard it from a groom, who had seen it himself. He did not write it down. There was no column for it.

---

On the twenty-fifth there were envoys. Theodoros saw them only from a distance: men in silk on small fine horses, and a great deal of bowing. The rumor ran through the baggage like water through sand. The sultan offered peace. The sultan offered a truce, or an exchange of territory, or something in between.

"He'll refuse," Gagik said.

"How do you know?"

"Because he brought all this way." Gagik swept a hand across the camp, the wagons and tents and horse lines and smoke. "An emperor doesn't march the whole empire into Armenia to go home with a treaty. In the City they'd call him a coward. They'd say a Doukas would have done better." He shook his head slowly. "He's not fighting the sultan. He's fighting Constantinople. The sultan just happens to be standing in the way."

The emperor refused.

---

The morning of the battle was hot even before the sun had cleared the hills. Theodoros woke to trumpets and the sound of thousands of men all making noise at once, a sound he had no word for. He went to his wagons out of habit. There was nothing to count. Everything that could be carried into battle had already been taken, and everything else would wait.

Gagik was armed. He had his long spear, his round shield painted with a faded red cross, and a coat of mail that looked older than Theodoros. He was standing beside the wagons as though he had been waiting.

"You're going," Theodoros said.

"They're taking everyone who can hold a spear. The baggage will be guarded by the baggage." Gagik smiled with half his mouth. "That's you."

"I can't fight."

"No. So don't. Stay with the wagons. If it goes badly, the Turks will want the wagons more than they want you. Lie down under one and be very boring." He paused. "You're good at that."

Theodoros laughed, though nothing was funny. "And if it goes well?"

Gagik looked out toward the plain, where the army was forming up in long lines like stitches in cloth. The emperor was at the center. The banners were bright.

"Then I'll come back," he said, "and you can count how many onions I've eaten in celebration."

---

Theodoros did not see the battle. Afterward he would hear it told in a hundred ways, in taverns and churches and once, years later, by a Frank who swore he had been there and plainly had not. Nobody would ever believe that Theodoros had stood within a few miles of it and seen almost nothing.

What he saw was dust.

The army moved forward across the plain, and the dust rose behind it in a wall that blotted out the hills. He could hear it, a long rolling noise that came and went with the wind. He heard horns. He heard what might have been screaming or might have been the wind in the tent ropes. The hours passed and the sun climbed and the dust kept moving away from him, east and south. The army was advancing. The army was winning, surely. Why else would it go forward?

A boy who tended the horses climbed onto a wagon to see better and reported, with mounting excitement, that the Turks were falling back. "They won't stand," he said. "They're running. They're running all day."

Theodoros sat in the shade of his wagon and opened his father's box and looked at the numbers. Four hundred and twelve mules. Arrows, so many bundles. Barley, oil, nails. He thought that this was what it looked like when an empire went to war: an empire was a thing made out of counted objects. It moved forward like a tide because someone, somewhere, had known how many horseshoes there were.

In the late afternoon the noise changed.

He couldn't have said how. It was the same noise, and yet it wasn't. The horns sounded a call he didn't know. The boy on the wagon went quiet.

"What is it?" Theodoros said.

"They're turning," said the boy. "The emperor's turning back. The standard—they're coming back toward camp."

"That's all right," Theodoros said. "It's getting dark. They'll come back and eat and go out again tomorrow."

"The rear's moving," said the boy. "The rear's moving the wrong way."

Theodoros stood up and climbed onto the wagon beside him. Through the haze, in the reddening light, he could see what the boy meant. The great body of the army was wheeling back toward the camp. The tight line of banners at the center was the emperor, and beyond it the Turks were no longer running. They were following, swarming at the flanks like gnats around an ox. Behind the emperor, where the rearguard should have been standing firm to cover the turn, there was a second dark mass of men and horses. It was not standing. It was riding away.

"Doukas," Theodoros whispered, and he did not know how he knew.

The word ran through the camp ahead of the fact. The emperor was dead. The emperor was taken. The rearguard had gone. The rearguard had told everyone the emperor was dead, which was why it had gone. The Armenians had broken. The Armenians had held. The Franks had never come back from Khliat. Nobody knew anything, and everybody shouted it.

Then the first riders reached the camp, and they weren't stopping. They rode through the wagons, men with blood on their faces and no helmets, and they didn't look down. Behind them came men on foot. Behind those came the noise, close now, and under it a sound Theodoros recognized from the surgeons' tents. It was the hiss of the short thick arrows.

He did what Gagik had told him. He lay down under the wagon and was very boring.

---

He lay there all night.

He heard horses, many horses. He heard a language he did not understand, laughing. He heard someone weeping in Greek, quite close, for a long time, and then not. Twice the wagon above him rocked as men climbed onto it and threw down sacks and jars, and twice he held his breath until he thought his chest would split. Once, a hand reached under and felt along the ground and touched his boot. It stayed there, considering. Then it moved on.

He held his father's cedar box against his stomach the whole time. He didn't know why. There was nothing in it anyone wanted.

---

When the sun came up the camp was very quiet.

Theodoros crawled out. The wagons had been stripped. The tents were down or burning. The mules were gone. All four hundred and nine, or however many had been left. He found himself trying to count the absence, and he stopped.

There were men on the ground, more than he could look at. A few Turkish riders moved slowly among them in the distance, taking things. They didn't seem interested in a single dirty clerk standing among the wagons. Perhaps they thought he was dead and hadn't yet fallen over.

He began to walk.

He walked west, because that was where the City was. He met others doing the same: a priest with no shoes, two Cappadocian archers with no bows, a cook he recognized who walked with his arms wrapped around himself and wouldn't speak. By midday there were thirty of them. Theodoros found that he was counting them without meaning to. Thirty-one. Thirty-four. Each time a man came limping up out of a ravine or out from behind a rock, the number in his head went up by one. It was the first good number he had known in a day.

On the second evening, beside a dry streambed, a voice said, "You're alive. That's a waste."

Gagik was sitting against a boulder. His shield was gone and his mail was torn at the shoulder, and there was a bandage around his thigh, dark through. He had no spear. He had, impossibly, an onion, and he was eating it.

Theodoros sat down beside him because his legs gave way.

"The emperor," he said, when he could.

"Taken," said Gagik. "I saw it. His horse went down. He fought on foot—he had a wound in his hand, and still he fought. They took him alive. They'll take him to the sultan." He chewed. "And Doukas rode home and told everyone the emperor was dead. So they'll have to make a new one. And the new one will be whatever the Doukai want."

"He'll be ransomed. Romanos."

"Maybe. And then he'll go home and find somebody sitting in his chair." Gagik looked at the onion. "It wasn't the Turks, you know. They were good. They were patient. But it wasn't them. They didn't beat us. We let go of each other."

They were quiet a while. Around them, in the dusk, the other walkers were settling down in clusters, too tired to make fires.

"How many?" Gagik said at last.

Theodoros looked up.

"You've been counting," Gagik said. "I know you. How many?"

Theodoros looked across the streambed at the shapes of men in the twilight, sitting, lying, holding their wounds, sharing a waterskin around in silence.

"Fifty-two," he said. "With you."

Gagik nodded slowly, as though this were an important figure, the most important figure there was.

"Write it down," he said.

Theodoros opened his father's box. The parchments were still there, all the mules and arrows and barley, numbers belonging to an army that no longer existed. At the bottom, unused, was a single clean sheet. He had no ink. But there was charcoal in the streambed from some old shepherd's fire, and he took up a piece and wrote, in his small careful hand:

*Fifty-two.*

He sat looking at it until it was too dark to see.

"Somebody has to know," he said.

"Yes," said Gagik, and passed him the rest of the onion.
