**The Ice Reader**

Every winter since she was nineteen, Marguerite Dufresne had walked out onto Lac Rémi with a steel bar and a notebook, and every winter the town waited for her to tell them whether they could drive.

She did it the way her father had done it. She went at first light, when the cold had pulled the lake tight and the ice spoke loudest. She drove the bar down in a line from the government dock toward the far point. She stopped every fifty paces, chipped a hole, measured with a hooked tape, and wrote the number in pencil. Pencil, because pens froze. Her father had told her that the first morning, when she was nineteen and furious at being asked to do it at all.

"The lake doesn't care what you think," he'd said. "Write what it tells you."

Now she was sixty-one, and the lake had told her a great many things. This January it was telling her something she didn't like.

---

The thermometer outside the hardware store read minus thirty-four when she came back off the ice. Gilles Pelletier was already standing in the doorway with two coffees, which meant he wanted something.

"Well?" he said.

"Twenty-two at the dock. Twenty-four out to the reef. Then it drops." She took the coffee. "Eleven, by the point. Nine in one spot."

Gilles winced. "Nine centimetres."

"Nine."

"That's the current from the creek mouth."

"It's always the current from the creek mouth. It's worse this year. It was warm in December."

He looked past her at the lake, which lay white and blank and innocent under a sky the colour of skim milk. On the far shore, eight kilometres off, the smoke from the Anishinaabe community at Kitchi Point rose in thin grey threads. In a good winter the ice road cut the drive between the two towns from two hours to fifteen minutes. In a good winter the school bus ran on it. The fuel truck ran on it. People went across for hockey on Friday nights and came back singing.

"The council meets tonight," Gilles said. "They'll want to know when."

"Tell them when it's ready."

"Marguerite."

"Tell them the lake will let me know."

He sighed the sigh of a man who had been mayor too long. "There's something else. Somebody was asking for you at the motel. Came in on the evening bus."

"Who?"

Gilles hesitated, which was unlike him. "He said his name was Thomas."

---

She did not go to the motel. She went home, fed the woodstove, and sat in her father's chair looking at the wall where the old ice charts hung in frames, forty-two winters of them, the early ones in her father's square handwriting and then, abruptly, in hers.

Thomas. She had not heard the name aloud in twenty-six years.

He found her anyway, which she should have expected. He knocked at four in the afternoon, when the light was already going amber and blue. She opened the door and there he was: her younger brother, gone heavy around the middle, his beard grey, wearing a city coat that had no business north of Sudbury.

"You'll freeze in that," she said.

"Hello to you too, Margo."

Nobody had called her Margo since he'd left.

She let him in because it was minus thirty-four and because she was not a monster. He stood dripping melted frost on the mat, looking at the wall of charts.

"You kept them all," he said.

"Somebody had to."

He took that like a slap, which was how she'd meant it, and then he did something she didn't expect. He laughed. Softly, tiredly.

"I deserved that. I rehearsed this on the bus for nine hours and I didn't come up with anything better than you just did."

"Why are you here, Thomas?"

He took off his gloves. His hands were shaking a little, and not only from cold. "I'm sick," he said. "It's not the dramatic kind, not yet. But the doctor used the word 'progressive,' and I found I wanted to see the lake. And you. Not necessarily in that order."

---

She made tea because her mother would have made tea. They sat at the kitchen table, the one with the burn mark where Thomas, at eleven, had set down a hot frying pan and their father had said nothing, which was worse than shouting.

"You still do the ice," Thomas said.

"Every year."

"Papa would be proud."

"Papa would say I'm too slow." She turned her mug in her hands. "He was always out there by the second week of December. He'd have the road flagged by New Year's."

"He was reckless," Thomas said quietly.

The kitchen went very still.

"Don't," Marguerite said.

"I'm not here to fight about it."

"Then don't."

But it was there between them on the table, as solid as the burn mark: the winter of 1999, the third week of December, their father driving the old Ford out past the reef to check the far stretch himself because he'd been impatient, because the town was impatient, because he had always been right before. Thomas had been in the passenger seat. Thomas had gotten out. Their father had not.

Afterward, Thomas had left. Not right away—he'd stayed for the funeral, stayed through a terrible January of people bringing casseroles and not quite meeting his eyes—and then one morning he was simply gone, a note on this same table that said *I can't be here*, and she had been nineteen and alone with a steel bar and a notebook and a whole town waiting.

"I've thought about it every day," Thomas said. "For twenty-six years. I want you to know that."

"I've thought about it every winter," she said. "That's different. I've had to."

---

She didn't sleep well. At five she was up, dressed in her layers, the bar over her shoulder. When she stepped out into the dark, Thomas was standing at the end of the driveway in a borrowed parka—Gilles's, she could tell by the hockey crest on the sleeve.

"No," she said.

"I'm not asking to drive. I'm asking to walk with you."

"You don't know what you're doing out there anymore."

"Then show me."

She looked at him for a long moment, at his breath freezing in his beard, at the stubborn set of his jaw that was their father's jaw exactly. Then she handed him the notebook and the pencil.

"You write," she said. "I'll measure. If I say stop, you stop. If I say go back, you go back and you don't argue."

"Understood."

They walked down to the government dock. The lake was black under the stars, and when they stepped onto it the ice let out a long resonant groan that travelled away from them into the distance, a sound like a whale singing underneath the floor of the world.

Thomas flinched.

"That's good," Marguerite said. "That's the cold making it. Thick ice talks. It's the quiet stuff you watch out for."

"I'd forgotten that sound."

"You'd forgotten a lot of things."

But she said it more gently than she meant to.

---

They worked their way out. Fifty paces, chip, measure. She called out the numbers and he wrote them, and his handwriting, she saw when she glanced at the page under her headlamp, was still the same slanted scrawl it had been when he was a boy doing his homework at the kitchen table.

Twenty-three at the dock. Twenty-four. Twenty-five at the reef, where the ice was clear as window glass and you could see, in the beam of the lamp, bubbles of trapped air suspended like frozen pearls.

"It's beautiful," Thomas said. "I didn't remember it was beautiful."

"It's the reef. Shallow water freezes clean."

They went on. The sky was greying in the east now, the smoke from Kitchi Point beginning to show against it. Past the reef the ice changed—went white and cloudy, layered with snow that had soaked and refrozen. She slowed. She tested each step with the bar before she trusted it.

"Fifteen," she said.

He wrote it.

"Thirteen."

He wrote it. His breath was coming harder.

"You all right?"

"Fine. Keep going."

"Eleven." She stopped. Ahead, perhaps a hundred metres, she could see the faint dark discolouration that marked the creek current, a long grey bruise under the snow. "This is where it was yesterday. We'll go along the edge of it, not across."

She turned north to skirt it. Thomas didn't move.

"Margo."

"What?"

"This is where it happened. Isn't it."

She looked around. The far point. The line of the creek. The shape of the hills against the lightening sky. She had walked past this spot every January for twenty-six years and she had never once let herself stop.

"Yes," she said. "About here."

---

He sat down on the ice. Just sat, in Gilles's parka, his legs out in front of him like a child.

"Thomas, get up. You'll freeze to it."

"Give me a minute."

She stood over him with the bar, feeling foolish and frightened and something else she didn't have a name for.

"He told me to get out," Thomas said. "Did you know that? I never told anybody. I never told the police. He heard it crack—you know the sound, the bad one, the sharp one—and he looked at me and said, 'Out, Tommy. Now. Walk back to the reef and don't run.' And I did. I got out and I walked. And I heard it go behind me and I didn't turn around until I got to the reef because he'd said don't run and I thought if I turned around I'd run."

Marguerite's throat had closed.

"I thought," Thomas said, "for twenty-six years, I thought: if I'd stayed in the truck. If I'd argued. If I'd grabbed the wheel. If I'd turned around sooner. And I couldn't look at you, because you'd lost him and I'd been right there, I'd been *right there*, and I'd just walked away like he told me."

"You were seventeen," she said.

"I was seventeen and I did what he said."

"That's what you were supposed to do." She knelt beside him, the cold coming up through her knees. "Thomas. That's what you were supposed to do. That's the rule. He taught us the rule. You don't go in after someone. You go for help. You walk to safe ice and you don't run."

"I know the rule."

"Then why—"

"Because knowing the rule doesn't make you feel any better about it!" His voice cracked across the ice and came back to them off the hills. "You of all people. You came out here every year. Every year, Margo, you walked out here alone where he went in, and you did it right, you did it slow and careful, and I was in Toronto doing nothing, working in an office, being nobody, and every January I'd think, she's out there now. She's out there doing the thing I couldn't do."

She hadn't known that. She hadn't known that he counted the winters, too.

"I was so angry at you," she said finally. "For leaving. For leaving me with it."

"I know."

"I'm still angry."

"I know."

"But I didn't know he told you to go." She wiped her face with the back of her mitten; the tears were freezing on her lashes. "I thought you just—I thought you got out because you were scared. And I hated you for it, and I hated myself for hating you, because who wouldn't be scared?"

"I *was* scared."

"Of course you were." She laughed, and it came out half a sob. "I'm scared every single morning I come out here. Do you know that? Forty-two years. I still listen for it every step."

---

The sun came up while they sat there. It came up all at once over the eastern hills the way it does in deep cold, flat and gold and enormous, and the whole lake caught fire with it, every crystal of snow lit, the long shadows of the pressure ridges stretching blue toward the west.

Thomas looked at it with his mouth open.

"I'd forgotten that, too," he said.

"You've been gone a long time."

"Too long."

She stood, and held out a hand, and after a moment he took it and she hauled him up. His knees cracked. He swore in French, which made her laugh properly for the first time.

"Your accent's gone terrible," she said.

"Twenty-six years of Toronto will do that."

"Well. You can fix it."

He looked at her. "Can I?"

She didn't answer right away. She looked at the grey bruise of the current, at the far point, at the smoke rising straight up from Kitchi Point into the windless gold air. Somebody over there was making breakfast. Somebody's kid was waiting for a school bus that couldn't come yet.

"The road won't be ready for two weeks," she said. "Maybe three, with that current. I'll need to measure every morning until then. My knees aren't what they were, and it's hard to write with mitts on."

Thomas was quiet for a moment.

"I could write," he said.

"You could."

"I'd need a better coat."

"Gilles will lend you his until you get one. He's a soft touch."

"Margo." He swallowed. "I don't know how long I've got. Before it gets bad, I mean. The doctor said years, probably. But I don't know."

"Nobody knows," she said. "That's what the ice teaches you. You go fifty paces at a time. You measure. You write down what it tells you. You don't pretend it's thicker than it is, and you don't pretend it's thinner, either."

He took the notebook back out of his pocket. Under *eleven* he had written nothing, and now he wrote, in that slanted boy's hand: *Jan. 14. Creek current. Walked around it.*

"Is that right?" he asked.

She looked at the page.

"That's right," she said. "Come on. We've still got the far stretch to do, and I want to be home before the coffee's cold."

They went north along the edge of the bad ice, slowly, testing every step. Somewhere beneath them, the lake groaned its long, deep, living song, and this time neither of them flinched.

---

The ice road opened on the second of February. The first vehicle across was the school bus, which was tradition, and the second was Gilles Pelletier's truck, which was also tradition, because the mayor had to be seen to trust it.

The third was an old blue pickup with two people in the cab, a grey-haired woman driving and a grey-bearded man in the passenger seat with a notebook open on his knee. They drove slowly—slower than anyone needed to—and about two-thirds of the way across, past the reef, near the place where the creek current ran, the truck stopped for a moment.

Nobody in town knew why. Nobody asked. After a minute or so the truck started again and went on toward Kitchi Point, where the hockey game was starting at seven, and where, people said afterward, the two of them sat in the stands together and shouted at the referee in French, both of them, with exactly the same accent.
