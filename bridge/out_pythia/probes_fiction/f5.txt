**The Request Stop**

The halt at Carrow Bend had no building. It had a platform long enough for one carriage, a lamp on a post, a bench with a brass plate too weathered to read, and a sign that said, in white letters on a green board, TRAINS STOP ON REQUEST. HAND SIGNAL TO DRIVER.

Nell Akinyemi had been driving the Thornmouth branch for eleven weeks when she noticed the man.

It was a Tuesday in October, the light already going thin by four. She came round the long curve above the river, where the line bent like an elbow and the rails squealed in a way she had stopped hearing after the first fortnight, and there he was on the platform. Old, in a dark coat buttoned to the throat, a flat cap, one arm raised. Not waving, exactly. Holding his hand up, palm out, the way a child does in a classroom when he isn't sure he wants to be called on.

She began to brake. The rulebook was clear: a hand signal at a request stop meant you stopped. The two-car unit groaned and slowed, and the single door that would line up with the short platform came to rest almost exactly in front of him. She was pleased with that. She opened the doors.

He didn't get on.

She leaned out of the cab window. "Are you coming?"

He lowered his arm. He seemed surprised, as if the train had appeared out of nowhere. "No," he said. "No, thank you."

"You signalled."

"I did," he agreed. "I'm sorry. Go on, you'll be late."

She was, by three minutes, by the time she reached Thornmouth. Nobody said anything. On the Thornmouth branch, nobody ever said anything. It was a line that ran because closing it would have required a consultation, and nobody had the energy for a consultation.

---

The next day he was there again. Same coat, same cap, same raised hand. She slowed, not as much this time, watching him. He didn't step toward the edge. He didn't pick up a bag, because he didn't have one. She let the train roll through at a walking pace, and as the cab passed him he nodded at her, a small, courteous nod, the kind you'd give a neighbour across a garden fence.

"There's a bloke at Carrow Bend," she said that evening, in the mess room at Thornmouth, to Dev, who had driven the branch for nineteen years and was eating crisps out of the bag with the concentration of a surgeon. "Puts his hand out every afternoon. Never gets on."

"Oh, aye," said Dev. "That's Mr. Pryce."

"You know him?"

"Everyone knows him. He's been doing it since before I started."

"Doing what, though? Why?"

Dev shook the last crumbs into his palm and considered them. "Never asked," he said. "Seemed rude."

"It's rude to make a train stop for nothing."

"You don't have to stop. Most of us just slow down a bit. Give him a toot." He licked salt from his thumb. "He's harmless."

"I didn't say he wasn't harmless. I said why."

Dev looked at her the way older drivers looked at her quite often, with a sort of tired fondness, as if she were a puppy that had just discovered its own tail. "Not everything on this line has a why, Nell. Half the signals don't have a why. There's a level crossing at Penhallow that goes down for no train at all, twice a day, half past nine and twenty past two. Nobody knows why. Network Rail sent a man. He couldn't find out either."

---

She thought about it over the weekend, which irritated her. She had come to the branch from freight, where everything had a why, usually measured in tonnes. She had asked for the transfer because her mother was in a home in Thornmouth and the freight rosters had her sleeping in cab rest rooms in Doncaster three nights out of seven. The branch was slow and quiet and paid less, and the passengers were mostly schoolchildren and pensioners going to the hospital, and she had expected to be bored, and she was, a little. A man who raised his hand for no reason was the most interesting thing that had happened to her since August.

On Monday she stopped properly. Brought the train to a halt, opened the doors, and when he didn't move she put on the handbrake, told the conductor — a lad called Josh who mainly played games on his phone between stations — that she'd be a minute, and climbed down from the cab onto the platform.

Mr. Pryce looked alarmed. "Oh," he said. "Oh, you shouldn't — is something the matter?"

"That's what I'm asking you."

"With me? No. Nothing's the matter with me."

Up close he was older than she'd thought. Eighty, maybe more. His coat was good wool, old but brushed. His shoes had been polished that morning. He had very pale blue eyes, the kind that look watery even when they aren't.

"You put your hand up every day," she said. "You know it means you want the train to stop."

"I do know that."

"So why do you do it?"

He looked past her at the train, at the two carriages humming patiently, at the handful of faces in the windows peering out at this unusual delay. A woman with a pushchair. Two girls in school uniform. A man asleep with his forehead against the glass.

"You'd better go," he said gently. "Those people have places to be."

"They're fine. Ninety seconds."

He sighed. It wasn't an annoyed sigh. It was the sigh of someone setting down a thing he'd been carrying for a long way.

"My wife," he said, "got off here. Once."

Nell waited.

"That's all, really," said Mr. Pryce. "It's a very small thing. Please. Go on."

She went. She was four minutes late into Thornmouth and Josh asked her what that had been about and she said, "Nothing," and was surprised at how fierce it came out.

---

She didn't stop the next day, or the day after. She slowed, and she raised her own hand from the cab, and he raised his back, and that seemed to be an arrangement. But on the Friday the weather turned, and the rain came sideways off the river so hard the wipers couldn't keep up, and when she came round the elbow at Carrow Bend there he was, soaked, cap dark with water, hand up.

She stopped. She opened the doors.

"Get in," she called. "For God's sake. I'll take you to Thornmouth and you can get the bus back, or I'll drop you on the return."

He hesitated. Then — and she would remember this, the way he did it, as if stepping onto ice — he walked forward and got on.

She let him ride in the front of the leading carriage, just behind the cab, which she wasn't supposed to do but there was nobody else in that car. She could see him in her mirror, sitting very upright, hands on his knees, dripping onto the moquette.

At Penhallow the level crossing was down, for no train at all, and she had to stop and wait for it to lift. She slid the cab door open a few inches.

"Do you want to tell me?" she said. "You don't have to."

He was quiet for long enough that she thought he wasn't going to. The rain drummed on the roof. Out on the road, a single car waited at the barrier, its headlights making two long yellow smears.

"It was 1961," he said. "September. I was twenty-four. She was twenty-two. Her name was Ruth. We'd been married four months."

The barrier didn't lift. Nell didn't mind.

"We were living in Thornmouth, in two rooms above a bakery. I worked at the boatyard. She worked in the post office. And we'd had — I won't say a row. We'd had a disagreement. About whether we'd go to her mother's for Christmas, if you can believe it. September, and we're arguing about Christmas. That's how young we were. It got very — well. It got very big, the way those things do, where it isn't about Christmas anymore. It's about whether you're the sort of person who'll always put your own family first. It's about whether you've made a mistake."

"And she left," said Nell.

"She got on this train. The branch, the old steam service then. She said she was going to her mother's in Lostbridge, which was the far end of the line, past where it goes now. They cut it back in sixty-six." He looked out of the window at the rain. "I went after her. I ran to the station and the train had gone, so I borrowed a bicycle from a man I didn't know, and I rode out along the river road. Seven miles. In my work boots."

The barrier lifted. The car drove across. Nell released the brake and the train eased forward.

"And she'd got off at Carrow Bend," Nell said.

"She'd got off at Carrow Bend. There was a little hut there then. A shelter, with a window. She'd put her hand out and the driver had stopped and she'd got off, and she was sitting in the hut when I came by on the road. I'd never have seen her except she'd lit a match. To look at the timetable on the wall. I saw the match."

"Why'd she get off?"

He smiled. It changed his whole face; it took about sixty years off it.

"I asked her that. She said she'd got halfway and realised she didn't actually want to go to her mother's. She said she wanted to go home, but she was too proud to get the train back. So she got off in the middle, where she wasn't anywhere, and she thought she'd sit there until she knew what she wanted. She said, 'I thought you might come, and if you came that would decide it, and if you didn't come that would decide it too.'"

"And you came."

"I came. On a stolen bicycle. Well, borrowed. I took it back." He wiped rain from his eyebrow with one knuckle. "She sat on the handlebars all the way home. Seven miles. In the dark. I've no idea how we didn't die."

The train came into the cutting before Thornmouth, the walls rising up on either side, slick and black.

"We were married fifty-eight years," said Mr. Pryce. "She died in the spring of 2019. And the summer after, I found I'd walked out to Carrow Bend without quite meaning to. And a train came. And I — " He lifted his hand, a little, from his knee, and let it fall. "It's a ridiculous thing for a grown man to do."

"It's not," said Nell.

"It is a bit."

"All right, it is a bit." She brought the train into Thornmouth, under the canopy, into the yellow light. "Doesn't mean you shouldn't."

---

She drove him back on the return working. He sat in the same seat. When she stopped at Carrow Bend to let him off, he stood on the platform in the rain, which had eased to a drizzle, and he looked very small next to the lamp post.

"What are you waiting for?" she said. "When you put your hand up. Do you think — "

"Oh, I don't think she's coming back. I'm not soft in the head." He considered. "I think it's the asking. The train comes, and I ask it to stop, and it's like saying — I'm still here, I'm still the one who came after her. Does that make any sense?"

"Bit."

"It used to be, every driver stopped. The first year. Then they got to know me and they'd slow down and wave, and that's fine, that's lovely, actually. But it's been a long time since one of them stopped." He looked up at her. "So thank you."

"I'm going to stop every day now," Nell said. "Just so you know."

"You'll get in trouble."

"Ninety seconds. Nobody on this line has noticed anything since 1966."

He laughed, a real laugh, and turned and walked up the little path toward the road, and she watched him go in her mirror until the curve took him away.

---

She did stop, every day, through October and into November. Sometimes he got on and rode to Thornmouth and had a cup of tea at the café by the station and came back. Mostly he didn't. Mostly she stopped and opened the doors and he said, "Not today, thank you," and she said, "Right you are," and closed them again, and the whole thing took perhaps forty seconds, and Josh, who had at first been baffled, began to look up from his phone at Carrow Bend and lift a hand to Mr. Pryce through the window.

Dev found out, inevitably. Dev found out everything eventually, the way water finds out about a crack.

"You've been stopping for Pryce," he said in the mess room.

"I have."

"Every day?"

"Every day."

He opened a fresh bag of crisps. Salt and vinegar. He held it out to her, which in nineteen years, she later learned, he had done for perhaps three people.

"Good," he said. "I used to. Got out of the habit." He crunched. "Got out of a lot of habits."

---

In the first week of December, Mr. Pryce wasn't on the platform.

She slowed anyway, came almost to a stop, peered into the dusk. The lamp was on, the bench was empty, the sign said TRAINS STOP ON REQUEST. She waited a full minute with the doors open, the cold air coming into the carriage, until Josh rapped on the cab door and said, "Nell, we've got to go."

He wasn't there the next day either.

On the third day she rang the number for the Thornmouth surgery, which was the only thing she could think of, and the receptionist wouldn't tell her anything, of course, because she wasn't family. She asked at the café by the station. The woman behind the counter knew him — "Arthur, yes, lovely man, has a tea cake" — but hadn't seen him.

It was Dev, in the end, who knew someone who knew someone. Arthur Pryce had had a fall at home. A broken hip. He was in the hospital at Penhallow and they were saying he'd go into a care home after, because he couldn't manage the stairs on his own anymore, and there was nobody to help him with them.

"Which home?" said Nell.

"Dunno. Does it matter?"

It did, as it turned out, although she didn't know that yet.

---

She visited him in the hospital on her day off. He was propped up on pillows, much smaller in a hospital gown than in his good wool coat, and when he saw her his face did the thing again, the sixty years falling off it.

"The driver," he said. "You came."

"I came."

"On a stolen bicycle?"

"Bus."

"Less romantic."

"Much less."

She sat with him for an hour. They talked about nothing much — the hospital food, the weather, Josh's phone, Dev's crisps. Before she left he took her hand in his, which was dry and light as paper.

"I shan't get out to Carrow Bend again," he said. "I know that. Don't say I will."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good." He squeezed her fingers. "Will you do something for me?"

"Depends."

"Slow down there. Now and then. That's all. Just slow down."

---

The home he went to, in January, was the one where her mother lived.

She didn't arrange it. She couldn't have. It was simply the one with a bed. But on her first visit after he moved in, she found him in the day room, in a chair by the window, and her mother in the chair next to him, the two of them watching the rain on the car park with the absorbed attention of people watching a film.

"Nell," said her mother, who on good days knew her and on bad days didn't, and today was a good day. "This is Arthur. He used to stop trains."

"I know," said Nell. "I used to stop for him."

Arthur looked up at her and lifted his hand, palm out, the way a child does in a classroom.

She sat down between them. Outside, faintly, from beyond the roofs, came the two-tone horn of the four o'clock to Thornmouth, coming round the elbow above the river — Dev at the controls, today. She knew without looking that he'd be slowing at Carrow Bend. She knew without looking that he'd be raising his hand to an empty platform, to a lamp and a bench and a green sign in the dusk.

Arthur heard it too. He turned his head toward the sound.

"There," he said softly, to nobody in particular. "Still here."
