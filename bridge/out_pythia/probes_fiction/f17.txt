**The Last Siren at Wattle Creek**

The siren at Wattle Creek Recreation Reserve had been bought second-hand from a fire station in 1971. It made a sound like a cow giving birth through a trombone. Every spring, when the committee met to plan the following year, someone proposed replacing it. Every spring, Les Harcourt said over his dead body, and since Les was eighty-four, the committee took that as a reasonable timeline.

Les was the timekeeper. He had been the timekeeper since before the siren, back when the job meant a whistle and a wristwatch. He sat in a fibro box at the edge of the wing with a stopwatch on a lanyard and a thermos of tea so stewed it could strip paint. Nobody else was allowed in the box except, this season, Danny Mercer's boy.

The boy's name was Callum. He was fourteen, too young for the seniors and too sulky for the under-sixteens, which he'd quit in March after an argument with the coach about whether he was "putting his body on the line." Callum held that his body was his own business. His father, who had been putting his body on the line for Wattle Creek for twenty-one seasons, did not agree but had run out of ways to say so.

So Danny dropped him at the timekeeper's box on the first Saturday in April. Callum sat on an upturned milk crate with his phone, and Les ignored him for the whole first quarter. At quarter-time Les held out the stopwatch.

"Hold that."

"Why?"

"Because my thumb's gone funny and I need to pour me tea."

Callum held it. Les poured his tea and drank half of it before he spoke again.

"Ball goes dead, you stop it. Umpire bounces or throws it up, you start it. Goal's kicked, you stop it. Centre bounce, you start it. That's the whole job."

"That's the whole job?"

"That's the whole job. Doing it right for eighty minutes a week for sixty years is the hard bit."

---

Everyone at the club knew it was probably the last season. They didn't say it at the bar, or at least not before the third beer, but they knew. The league had sent the letter in January. Wattle Creek and Tarrawong were to "explore a pathway to amalgamation" for the following year, and everyone understood that the pathway led one way, to Tarrawong's ground, which had lights and a sealed carpark and a clubroom built since the Whitlam government.

Wattle Creek had forty-one registered senior players across two grades, which sounded like plenty until you remembered that eleven of them were over thirty-five and four were the Pattison brothers, who were good for about one game in three, depending on the harvest and their wives. The reserves had forfeited twice the year before. The netball side was healthier, but the netballers were already talking to Tarrawong.

Danny was thirty-nine. He played in the ruck, where you could hide your age by being large and stubborn. His knees sounded like a fire in dry grass. He'd told his wife Jodie this would be his last year, and she'd said, "You said that the last four years," and he'd said, "Yeah, but this time the club's dying, so it'll stick."

He meant it as a joke. Neither of them laughed.

---

By round four Callum had stopped bringing his phone into the box.

He hadn't decided to stop. He'd just found he couldn't watch the ball and the screen at once, and if he missed a stoppage Les would say nothing, which was worse than being yelled at. Les's silences were very specific. They seemed to say: I have been doing this since Menzies and you can't manage forty seconds.

There was more to the job than Les had let on. You had to watch the umpire's arm, not the ball, because the ball might roll on a few metres after the whistle. You had to know the difference between a ball-up after a stalled pack and a ball that had only nearly gone dead. At the end of each quarter there was the siren, and that was a different thing altogether.

"You don't touch the siren," Les said. "Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because when the clock says it's time, you press it and you hold it till the umpire turns round. If you're late, some poor bugger kicks a goal that shouldn't count. If you're early, some poor bugger misses out on one that should. Either way a fella remembers it for the rest of his life." Les drank his tea. "I've seen fights over a second and a half. Real ones. Car park, after."

"Over a second and a half?"

"Grand final, 1979. Tarrawong. I was early." Les screwed the cup back onto the thermos. "That's all I'm saying about it."

---

Wattle Creek lost the first five games. That surprised no one. Then they beat Lismore Flat by four points in a game played in sideways rain, and the clubrooms that night felt like a wake where the corpse sat up and ordered a round.

Danny had kicked the winning goal, his first in two seasons, from a free kick at the top of the square. It wobbled through like a drunk finding his front door. He told the story about nine times that night and the kick got longer and straighter every time.

"Was it after the siren?" Callum asked in the car on the way home. He'd been in the box. He knew it wasn't. He wanted to hear what his father would say.

"No," said Danny. "About forty seconds before. Why?"

"Les said if it's after the siren, you get to kick it anyway, and that's the best kind."

"Les would know." Danny drove in silence past the silos, then said, "You like it in there with him?"

Callum shrugged, which Danny had learned over the past two years could mean anything from *yes* to *I'm planning to run away to Queensland*.

"He's all right," Callum said. "He's a bit weird."

"He's the most normal bloke at that club."

"That's what I mean."

---

In June, the coach, a schoolteacher from Tarrawong named Graham Fitch who'd taken the job because nobody else applied, called a meeting after training. He stood under the one working floodlight with his hands in his jacket pockets.

"I'm not going to lie to you blokes," he said. "We're not making finals."

This got a laugh, because they were eighth of nine.

"But I reckon we can win four more. Maybe five. And I reckon there's people in this town who'd like to see that. People who've been coming here for fifty years. So I want us to play every game like it's the last one at this ground, because the way things are going, one of them will be."

Nobody laughed at that.

Danny drove home and sat in the ute in the driveway for twenty minutes with the engine off. Jodie came out in her dressing gown and got into the passenger side.

"You right?"

"My old man played there," he said. "His old man played there. There's a photo in the rooms of the 1923 side, and one of the blokes in the back row is a Mercer. I don't know which one. There's no names on it."

"I know, love."

"Callum won't play. That's fine. I'm not saying it's not fine. It's just—" He spread his big hands on the steering wheel. "It just stops. With me. In a merger."

Jodie put her hand on the back of his neck. "He's in the box every week," she said. "He hasn't missed one."

"That's not playing."

"No," she said. "It's not."

---

In July, Les's thumb got worse.

He didn't say so. He just started handing Callum the stopwatch at the start of the second quarter instead of at the start of the third. By the end of the month, Callum ran the clock the whole game, and Les sat beside him on a folding chair with a blanket over his knees, watching the umpires and correcting him under his breath.

"Ball-up. Start it. Start it."

"I've started it."

"You were late."

"I was not."

"You were late by half a second. I can hear it."

"You cannot *hear* half a second."

Les turned his head and looked at him with watery, very pale blue eyes. "Sixty years, son," he said. "You'd be amazed what you can hear."

Callum checked the scoresheet against the clock at three-quarter time. He'd been late by about half a second at the ball-up Les had flagged. He didn't tell Les. He suspected Les knew.

---

The third-to-last home game was against Tarrawong.

The crowd was the biggest anyone could remember, maybe four hundred. People had driven in from properties out past the lake. There were old men in club scarves that had been knitted by wives now in the Wattle Creek cemetery. There were kids running round the boundary with plastic footies, and dogs, and a sausage sizzle staffed by the netball girls, who'd decided out of solidarity, or nostalgia, or just for the gate money, to stick around one more week.

Les didn't come.

His daughter rang the club on Saturday morning. He'd had a fall in the night. Nothing broken, they thought, but he was in Horsham Hospital for observation and he was furious about it. He'd asked her to pass on a message, which the club president, a stock agent named Rob Kinnane, read out loud to Callum in the timekeeper's box half an hour before the bounce.

"He says: 'Tell the boy he does the siren.'"

Callum stared at him.

"There's a second timekeeper from Tarrawong," Rob said. "Fella named Doug. He'll run the official clock with you, keep you honest. But the siren's ours. Always has been. Home ground, home button." Rob looked at the big red button on the wall, which was mounted on a piece of varnished wood and labelled, in Les's capitals, SIREN, as if anyone could mistake it. "You right with that?"

"Yeah," said Callum. His voice did something strange. "Yeah, I'm right."

---

Doug from Tarrawong was about sixty and wore a hearing aid and said almost nothing for three quarters, which Callum appreciated. They started and stopped their watches in near-unison and wrote down the times at each break and compared them, and they were never more than a second apart.

The first quarter went to Tarrawong by two goals. The second went to Wattle Creek by three, mostly because the elder Pattison brother, who'd turned up for once, kicked two from the pocket and then had to go off with a hamstring. The third was a grind, rain coming across the paddocks in grey sheets, the ball like a bar of soap. At the last change it was Tarrawong by one point.

Callum pressed the siren at three-quarter time. He held it until the field umpire turned and raised her arms, and he let go, and his hand was shaking.

"Steady," said Doug, and Callum nearly jumped, because it was the first thing Doug had said since "G'day."

---

Danny had been in the ruck all day. He'd had a shocker in the first quarter and been beaten in every contest by a twenty-two-year-old from Tarrawong who was six foot six and bounced like a kangaroo on a trampoline. In the second quarter Danny had stopped trying to jump with him and started just standing on the spot where the kid wanted to land, which was technically legal and worked a treat, and which the crowd loved.

At three-quarter time Graham Fitch knelt in the middle of the huddle in the rain and said, "Twenty minutes. That's all. Twenty minutes and you never have to play Tarrawong again, because next year you'll *be* Tarrawong."

It was supposed to be funny. Somebody laughed. Danny didn't. He was looking across the ground at the timekeeper's box, where he could see the pale shape of his son's face behind the glass.

---

The last quarter was the best football Wattle Creek had played in ten years. Everyone said so afterwards, even the Tarrawong supporters. They led by eight. They trailed by four. They led by two. With six minutes left, Tarrawong kicked a goal from a fifty-metre penalty, and the bounce-around kid took the next centre clearance, and Tarrawong kicked another, and suddenly it was Tarrawong by ten with three minutes to go.

In the box Callum watched the clock and the umpires and nothing else. He didn't let himself look at the scoreboard. He could hear the crowd, though, rising and falling, and he could feel the box shaking slightly every time the old men along the fence started banging on the corrugated iron with their fists.

Wattle Creek kicked a goal. Four points.

Centre bounce. Callum started the clock.

Danny won the hit-out. It was a terrible tap that dribbled forward off the side of his hand, but it was forward, and Wattle Creek's rover, a seventeen-year-old named Tyson Huang who worked at the IGA, gathered it on the half-volley and ran. He ran fifty metres with three Tarrawong players chasing him and bounced it twice and kicked it long and high into the forward pocket, where a pack formed like iron filings around a magnet.

The ball spilled. Nobody could hold it. It bounced towards the boundary.

Callum looked at the clock. Thirty-one seconds.

Danny had followed the ball. Nobody knew why. Ruckmen don't follow the ball to the forward pocket, especially thirty-nine-year-old ruckmen with knees like dry grass, but there he was, lumbering into the pack, and the ball came off someone's shin and he dropped on it like a man falling onto a grenade.

The umpire blew her whistle. Holding the ball. Free kick, Wattle Creek, to Danny Mercer.

Callum stopped the clock. Nineteen seconds.

He looked at Doug. Doug looked at his own watch and nodded once.

Danny got up slowly. He was about thirty metres out on a slight angle, near the boundary, with the wind blowing across the face of goal and the ball as slick as a fish. He hadn't kicked a goal from thirty metres in four years. He stood there for a long moment, looking at the posts.

Then he looked at the timekeeper's box.

Callum couldn't see his father's expression through the rain. He saw him lift one hand, briefly, the way you'd wave to someone across a paddock.

The umpire called time on. Callum started the clock.

Danny went back on his mark. He had thirty seconds by the rules to take his kick, but the game clock was running, and there were nineteen seconds left on it, then eighteen, then seventeen. The crowd had gone completely silent. Callum could hear the rain on the tin roof of the box and Doug's breathing and his own heart.

Fourteen. Thirteen.

He had his hand on the siren. He wasn't pressing it, just resting there, ready.

Danny ran in. Eleven. Ten. He kicked.

It was an ugly kick. It came off the outside of his boot and started off right, towards the point post, and it was going to miss. Callum could see it was going to miss. Then the wind got under it, the same crosswind that had been ruining kicks all day, and pushed it back, slowly, slowly, and it went through. It barely cleared the goal post, a metre inside, maybe less.

The goal umpire paused. Then he brought both index fingers up in front of his chest.

Callum stopped the clock. Seven seconds.

Two points to Wattle Creek.

Behind him the crowd went off like a bushfire jumping a road. People came over the fence. The goal umpire waved his flags and somebody's dog ran onto the ground and Danny Mercer was on his back in the forward pocket with a pile of teammates on top of him, and Callum was laughing, and his eyes were stinging, and he had his hand on the stopwatch.

"Centre bounce," said Doug quietly. "Seven seconds."

The umpires cleared the ground. It took four minutes. Callum didn't take his eyes off them. The field umpire walked to the centre circle and held the ball up and looked at the box, and Callum, for the first time all day, looked her in the eye.

She bounced it. He started the clock.

Tarrawong won the clearance and kicked it long. It was in the air when the clock reached zero.

Callum pressed the siren.

It made its terrible sound, the cow and the trombone, and he held it, and held it, and held it, until the field umpire turned around and raised her arms. Only then did he let go.

---

They lost the next two. They finished eighth of nine. In February, the merger went through, and in March the Wattle Creek Football Club officially ceased to exist, and the Tarrawong–Wattle Creek Football Netball Club came into being with new jumpers that nobody liked.

Les Harcourt came home from hospital in time for the presentation night. He was in a wheelchair, which he loudly said was temporary. He was presented with a life membership, his third, and a framed photograph of the siren.

Then he asked for the microphone, which nobody had planned for. He said that the Tarrawong timekeeper had approached him about the coming season, and that he was too old for it, and that he had recommended a replacement.

"Young fella," Les said. "Bit late on his ball-ups. But he's good on the siren." He paused. "Best I've seen."

At the back of the room, Danny Mercer had to go outside for a while.

---

The siren went to Tarrawong in the end. The new committee proposed replacing it at the first meeting. A fourteen-year-old in the second row stood up and said, "Over my dead body," and the committee, after some discussion, decided that was a fairly long timeline, and moved on to the next item.
