**The Blue Window**

My grandfather kept the crystal on the kitchen windowsill, where the morning light came through it and threw a small blue shape onto the counter. He said he'd grown it in 1971, in a jam jar, the summer before he married my grandmother. It was the size of a walnut and had flat sides and sharp edges. The blue looked almost too bright to be real, like a gas station slushie or the sky in a child's drawing.

"Copper sulfate," he told me the first time I asked. I was six. "Don't lick it."

"Why would I lick it?"

"Because it looks like candy. Everybody wants to lick it. Your mother did."

"Did she die?"

"She threw up in the sink and cried for an hour. Then she was fine. Don't lick it."

I didn't lick it. I was the kind of child who obeyed warnings, which my grandfather thought was a shame. When I was fourteen my parents split up and my mother took a job in Calgary that she said would last a year. I moved into the spare room at my grandfather's house in Port Elvin, and by then the crystal had turned white.

It hadn't turned all at once. I noticed it in October, a chalky bloom along one edge, like frost on a car window. By November half of it had gone pale and dull, and the blue shape on the counter had shrunk to a smudge.

"It's dying," I said.

My grandfather was at the table with the crossword, which he no longer finished. He looked up at the windowsill for a long moment, and I wondered whether he knew what I meant.

"It's not dying," he said finally. "It's drying out. Furnace has been running. Dry air pulls the water out of it."

"There's water in it?"

"Five waters for every copper. That's what makes it blue." He set down his pencil. "Without the water it's just a white powder. Same copper. Different company."

He used to say things like that all the time, little sideways sentences that sounded like jokes until you thought about them later. He had worked for thirty-one years at Harmon Plating on the edge of town, first on the line and then as the man who mixed the baths, the long tanks of acid and dissolved metal where they hung car bumpers and faucet handles and hospital fittings and ran current through them until the metal crept out of the liquid and coated the parts. He came home smelling of something sharp and sour, my mother said. He had burn scars on the backs of his hands that looked like tiny pale maps.

Harmon closed in 2009. Since then he'd fixed lamps for the neighbors, and polished things, and, for the last two years, forgotten things.

---

The doctor called it *mild cognitive impairment*, which my grandfather said was a very polite way of saying that he'd put his wallet in the freezer. He didn't seem frightened by it, or if he was, he didn't let me see. He wrote notes and stuck them to the cupboards. *TURN OFF THE BURNER.* *WREN HAS PIANO TUESDAYS.* *THE CAT IS DEAD, DON'T CALL FOR HER.* That last one made me cry the first time I read it, and then, over the weeks, it started to make me laugh, and I couldn't tell if that made me a bad person.

My mother called on Sundays. "How is he really?" she'd ask, and I'd go out onto the back porch with the phone so he wouldn't hear.

"He's okay. He forgets stuff. He's still him."

"Is he eating?"

"He eats eggs. Lots of eggs."

"Wren, if it gets to be too much—"

"It's not too much."

It wasn't, mostly. Mostly it was quiet. We watched curling on TV, which he loved and I pretended to hate. He taught me to solder. He let me drive the truck in the church parking lot on Sunday afternoons, when there was nobody there except the gulls.

The church was St. Bartholomew's, a stone building at the top of the hill above the harbor, with a tall steeple clad in copper. The copper was green, the soft blue-green of old statues and bathtub stains and the inside of some seashells. You could see it from everywhere in town, from the ferry, from the highway coming in. People gave directions by it. *Go past the green steeple and turn left.*

In November, a sign went up on the church lawn: STEEPLE RESTORATION FUND. There was a drawing of a thermometer, the kind that fills up red as the money comes in. Below it was an architect's sketch of the steeple as it would look afterward, and the steeple in the sketch was a bright, shining orange-brown, like a new penny.

"They're going to make it brown," I said, the first time we drove past.

My grandfather slowed the truck. He looked at the sign for a long time. A car behind us honked.

"They're going to make it *ugly*," he said, and drove on.

---

That night he couldn't sleep. I heard him moving around downstairs, the creak of the third step, the clink of the kettle. When I came down he was at the kitchen table with a pad of graph paper, writing.

"What are you doing?"

"Arithmetic." He didn't look up. "They're replacing the whole skin. New sheets. I want to know what it'll cost them and how long until it turns green again on its own."

"How long?"

"Depends on the air. Out here, with the salt, maybe fifteen years. Twenty. In a city with a lot of sulfur in the air from the factories, faster, and it's a different green. Bluer." He tapped the pencil against the pad. "The old roof's been up there since 1928. That patina is almost a hundred years of rain."

"Is it rust?"

"Sort of. Not exactly. Rust eats iron. Flakes off, and the next layer rusts, and the next, until there's nothing left. Copper's smarter." He drew a little line on the paper, then a little bumpy layer above it. "The air gets to it, turns the outside to oxide first. Brown, then dark, almost black. Then the rain and the salt and the carbon dioxide work on that, and it turns into this green crust. Copper carbonates, chlorides, sulfates, depends where you are. And the crust is tight. It holds on. It keeps the rain off the metal underneath. The green is how the copper protects itself." He set down the pencil. "They want to tear off the armor because they think it looks old."

"Maybe the roof's actually broken, though?" I said. "Like, maybe it leaks?"

He looked at me. For a second I thought I'd made him angry. Then he laughed, a real laugh, the first in a while.

"Maybe it leaks," he said. "Good. That's a good question. You'd make a good engineer. Your mother would have made a good engineer if she'd stopped arguing with me long enough." He tore the page off the pad, folded it, and put it in the pocket of his robe. "We'll go look."

---

On Saturday we went to look.

The priest was a young man named Father Okafor, who had only been at St. Bartholomew's for a year and still seemed surprised every time somebody stopped him in the grocery store. He met us at the side door in a fleece jacket and running shoes.

"Mr. Halloran," he said. "Your granddaughter said on the phone you used to work in metals?"

"Plating. Thirty-one years."

"Then you'll understand this better than I do." He led us up a narrow staircase into the bell tower, and then up a ladder, and then up another ladder, which my grandfather climbed slowly, his breath loud in the small space. I stayed right behind him with my hand hovering near his back, not touching. At the top was a cramped wooden room under the point of the steeple, and the light coming in between the boards was green.

Father Okafor shone a flashlight onto the inside of the roof. "There. And there. You see?"

The inside of the copper sheeting was dark brown, almost purple. In two places there were holes, small and ragged, the size of a coin, and around them the wood beneath was black and soft.

My grandfather put his face very close to one of the holes. He touched the edge with his thumb.

"It's not the copper," he said.

"Sorry?"

"The copper didn't fail. Look." He pointed with his scarred hand. "See these? Nails. Somebody fixed this. Fifties, maybe sixties. They used steel nails on a copper roof." He shook his head. "You put two different metals together and add salt water, you've made a battery. The steel gives itself up. Corrodes away to nothing, and the hole spreads, and the water gets in. That's galvanic corrosion. Copper's fine. The copper's beautiful. Somebody just did a lousy repair."

Father Okafor was quiet. "The contractor said the whole roof was past its life."

"The contractor wants to sell you a roof."

"Grandpa."

"Well, he does." He straightened up, and winced, and put a hand on a beam. "You don't need a new steeple. You need someone to pull every steel nail out of this thing and patch it properly. Copper nails, copper flashing. Lead-coated if you like. It's a week of work. Maybe two."

"And the patches will be bright," Father Okafor said slowly. "They'll be brown. They'll stand out."

"For a few years." My grandfather was looking at the green light coming through the gaps. "Then they'll catch up."

---

He was quiet in the truck on the way home. I thought he was tired from the ladders. When we turned onto our street he said, "What was the priest's name again?"

"Father Okafor."

"Okafor." He said it twice more under his breath, like he was trying to fasten it down.

At home he went to lie down, and I stood in the kitchen looking at the crystal on the windowsill. It was almost entirely white now. Just one corner still held the blue, a small stubborn chip of color.

I got out my phone and looked it up. Copper sulfate pentahydrate. The five waters he'd talked about. When you heat it, the water leaves and it turns into a white powder. When you add the water back, it turns blue again. It said that right there, in a chemistry teacher's video with eleven thousand views. She sprinkled white powder on a watch glass and dripped water onto it from a pipette, and the powder bloomed blue like ink in a puddle, and got a little warm.

I thought about dripping water on the crystal. But I was pretty sure it wouldn't work on something that size. It would just make the surface mushy and blue and ruin the edges. It wouldn't be the same crystal anymore.

---

The parish council met on a Tuesday night in the church basement, under fluorescent lights, with cookies on a paper plate. There were about thirty people. The contractor was there, a heavy man in a quarter-zip with a laptop. He had a slideshow.

My grandfather had insisted on coming. He'd put on his good jacket and a tie, and I'd had to help him with the tie, standing in front of him in the hall while he lifted his chin and looked at the ceiling. His hands had been shaking. When I finished he patted my shoulder, twice, the way he used to when I was small.

"You have to talk if I lose it," he'd said in the truck.

"Lose what?"

"The thread." He'd tapped his temple. "I wrote it down. It's in my pocket. If I stop, you read it."

The contractor talked for twenty minutes. He showed photos of the holes. He showed a graph. He said the words *structural integrity* four times; I counted. Then Father Okafor said that a parishioner with some expertise had asked to speak, and my grandfather stood up.

He was good, at first. He explained the steel nails, the salt, the battery. He made the battery sound simple, which it was. A woman near the front nodded. A man in the back said, "Huh."

Then he said, "And the patina is—the patina is—"

He stopped.

Thirty people waited. The fluorescent light buzzed. My grandfather stood there with his mouth slightly open and his hand half raised, like he'd been reaching for something on a high shelf and found the shelf gone.

I stood up. I took the folded page out of his jacket pocket. My face was so hot I could feel my pulse in my ears. His handwriting was spiky and small, and some of the lines went downhill at the end.

"The patina is not damage," I read. "It is the copper taking care of itself. It took ninety-five years of weather to make that color. You cannot buy it. You can only wait for it." I swallowed. "If you strip it off, none of us in this room will live to see it come back. Except maybe the kids."

The basement was very quiet.

"That's me," I said. "I'm the kids."

Somebody laughed, kindly. My grandfather sat down heavily in his folding chair. I sat next to him. He found my hand under the table and held it so hard it hurt.

---

They voted on it in January. Nineteen to eleven for the repair. The contractor was not pleased; I heard he told someone at the hardware store that it was a false economy and the whole thing would come down in a storm in ten years. My grandfather said, "Let him wait ten years and see."

The repairs happened in April. A crew from Halifax, three men and a woman who specialized in old churches, put up scaffolding and spent eleven days pulling out the steel nails one at a time. They cut away the damaged sections and soldered in new copper, and when they were done the steeple was green with a handful of bright orange patches on the seaward side. It looked like a quilt, or like someone had stuck Band-Aids on it.

People complained. Mrs. Dunphy from the bakery said it looked like a disease.

"It looks like a repair," my grandfather said. "That's what a repair looks like."

He was worse by spring. He forgot my name twice, once for almost an entire afternoon, calling me Ellen, which was my mother's name. I didn't correct him. I didn't know if I should. My mother flew in for a week in May and they talked at the kitchen table late at night in low voices, and when she left she said she was going to try to transfer back east by the fall, and that we were going to look at places, *nice* places, with gardens, where he'd have people around.

"He has people around," I said. "He has me."

"I know, honey," she said. "I know he does."

---

The night before she flew back to Calgary, I took the crystal down from the windowsill.

It was white all over now, even the last corner. When I touched it a little powder came off on my fingers. I found a jam jar in the cupboard, an old one with a label from a farm stand that didn't exist anymore, and I filled it with hot water from the kettle. Then I dropped the crystal in.

It didn't dissolve right away. The water clouded around it, and then, slowly, the cloud turned blue. Faint at first, like a vein under skin. Then deeper.

My mother came in while I was stirring it with a chopstick. She stopped in the doorway.

"Is that Dad's crystal?"

"It was." I kept stirring. "It turned white. It's not coming back by itself. So I'm going to grow a new one out of it."

She came and stood next to me. We watched the water go blue. "He taught me how to do that," she said. "In this kitchen. You hang a seed crystal on a thread and let the water evaporate slow."

"I know. I looked it up."

"Mine never came out right. They were always all lumpy." She laughed a little. "I licked one."

"I know. He told me."

"Of course he did." She put her hand on the back of my neck, the way he did sometimes. "It won't be the same crystal, Wren."

"It's the same copper," I said. "Different company."

She looked at me sideways, like she'd heard the voice behind it, and didn't say anything.

---

It took five weeks. I hung a little seed crystal on a fishing line tied to a pencil laid across the jar's mouth, and I put the jar on the windowsill where the old one had been, and every morning I checked it. My grandfather checked it too. Some mornings he knew what it was. Some mornings he'd stand there with his coffee and say, "What's that, then?" and I'd say, "It's copper sulfate, Grandpa," and he'd say, "Don't lick it," and I'd say, "I know."

By July it was the size of a grape. By August, the size of a walnut. It had flat sides and sharp edges, and in the morning it threw a small blue shape onto the counter, bright as the sky in a child's drawing.

We moved out in September, to a townhouse in Dartmouth near my mother's new office, ten minutes from a place called Harbourview Residence where my grandfather would go in the winter. On the last day in Port Elvin, I drove the truck one more time, with him in the passenger seat and the jar wrapped in a towel on my lap, past the church at the top of the hill.

The patches on the steeple had already started to darken. They weren't orange anymore. They were going brown, the deep brown of old pennies, on the way to something else.

"Look," I said. "It's catching up."

My grandfather looked up at the steeple for a long time. I didn't know what he was seeing, or whether he remembered the ladder, or the nails, or the basement with the cookies and the fluorescent lights. Maybe he didn't. Maybe it didn't matter.

"That's a good roof," he said finally. "Somebody took care of that."

"Yeah," I said, and turned left at the green steeple, the way everybody did.
