**The Mortar Woman**

The English had been building on the rock for two summers before Gwenllian ferch Iorwerth agreed to work for them, and she would have gone on refusing if the cow had not died.

It died in March, on its side in the wet bracken below the farm, its belly swollen like a bladder. Her son Dafydd, who was nine, sat beside it all afternoon. He seemed to think that if he kept it company it might change its mind. Gwenllian let him sit. At dusk she took him by the wrist and walked him home. She did not say what they both knew: without the cow there would be no butter to trade, and without butter there would be no grain by midsummer, and her husband had been in the ground at Llanfair since the winter before last.

So in April she walked down to the shore, where the masons' lodges stood in rows of wattle and thatch like a second village. She asked a man with a tally-stick whether they still paid women to carry.

"Carry what?" He spoke French, then English, then a little Welsh that he had plainly learned for exactly this purpose.

"Whatever you want carried."

He looked at her arms. Then he wrote something on his stick with a knife and said, "Lime. Tomorrow. Before the bell."

---

The lime came by boat from somewhere down the coast. It was burned in the kilns near the beach, and it went up to the rock in baskets on women's backs, because women were cheaper than mules and complained less, or so the tally-man believed. Gwenllian learned in the first hour that he was wrong about the second part. The women complained without stopping, in Welsh, and so cheerfully that the English foremen took it for singing.

"He's got a face like a boiled sheep's head," said Marared, who walked in front of her on the path. She meant the foreman at the second gate. "Look at him. Look at him. If you put him in a pot with leeks he'd be an improvement."

"Hush," said someone behind.

"He doesn't understand a word. Do you, *cariad*?" She smiled at the foreman, and the foreman, who did not understand a word, nodded back at her with dignity.

The baskets were lined with old sacking. Even so, the lime found its way through to the skin. By the end of the first week Gwenllian's shoulders had raw patches that stung like nettles when she washed them in the stream. Marared showed her how to rub them with goose fat and how to fold the sacking double where the straps went. Marared also told her, unasked, who among the women had lost husbands to the English, who had lost sons, and who had lost nothing but was working anyway out of plain greed.

"And you," Marared said, "you've lost a cow."

"How do you know that?"

"Everyone knows that. Your boy told the miller's girl, and the miller's girl tells everybody everything." She shifted her basket. "It's no shame. My Hywel died at the bridge, and I still carry their lime. God doesn't feed you for being proud."

Gwenllian said nothing. Her own husband had not died at any bridge. He had died of a cough, in his own bed, which seemed to her both a mercy and a sort of insult. There was no one to hate for it. She had to carry that around inside her the way she carried the lime, a weight with nowhere to set it down.

---

The castle was not yet a castle. It was a set of intentions in stone. The outer curtain wall stood to the height of three men in some places and was still a trench in others. The great gatehouse rose on the landward side, two drum towers with a dark throat between them. Gwenllian could not look at it without thinking of a mouth. Everything was bound in scaffolding of alder poles, lashed with rope and climbed by men who seemed made of string. At the top of everything, wherever she looked, she would find the master mason.

His name was Maître Jacques, or James, or Iago, depending on who was saying it. He came from Savoy, which Gwenllian understood to be a country somewhere past England and past France, full of mountains. He was small and grey-bearded. He wore a hood of good blue cloth, which was always white with dust, so that he looked like a frosted plum. He walked the wall-walks as if he were walking a road in his own village. Sometimes he stopped and laid his hand flat on a stone, the way a man touches a horse to see if it is calm.

She did not expect to speak to him. Women who carried lime did not speak to master masons. But on a day in June, the mortar-mixer at the north-west tower was taken sick. He had a griping in the bowels, which went through the lodges every summer like gossip. The foreman pointed at Gwenllian and said, in his terrible Welsh, "You. Stir."

So she stood at the mortar trough with a long wooden hoe. She folded the wet lime into the sand and the sand into the lime. The mixture went from lumpy to smooth, from grey to the colour of old cream. A shadow fell across the trough.

"Too wet," said Maître Jacques.

He spoke Welsh. It was not good Welsh; the vowels were in the wrong places, like furniture in a strange house. But it was Welsh.

She looked up at him. "The foreman said this much water."

"The foreman is building a wall today. I am building a wall for three hundred years." He crouched, took a lump of mortar on two fingers and pressed it with his thumb. "You see? It slumps. Like a tired man. It should stand up a little, and be proud." He took the hoe from her without asking and added a scoop of sand, then another. He worked it through with short, hard strokes. "There. Now try."

She pressed it. It held its shape under her thumb, with a little shine at the edges.

"Proud," she said, in Welsh, and he grinned. One of his front teeth was missing.

"Exactly. Proud. Who taught you to mix?"

"Nobody. I mixed it ten minutes before you came."

"Then you are quicker than the man who is sick. Keep the trough. I will tell Richard."

Richard was the foreman with the face like a boiled sheep's head. Gwenllian could imagine what Marared would say about a woman being given a man's place at the trough, and she found, a little to her surprise, that she was looking forward to hearing it.

---

The trough paid a penny more a week than the baskets did. It also gave her an hour each afternoon in the shade of the half-built tower, when the masons ate. Maître Jacques sometimes sat on a block of uncut stone and ate an onion like an apple. He talked, to her or to nobody.

"In my country," he said once, "the mountains are higher, but the rain is not so stupid. Here the rain does not fall. It *lives* here. It pays rent."

"You could go home," Gwenllian said.

"When the king stops wanting castles." He bit into the onion. "So, never."

She was quiet for a while. Then, because the shade was cool and her shoulders were not hurting, she asked the question she had been carrying since April. "Do you know what it's for?"

"The castle?" He looked at her sideways. "It is for keeping a garrison. For storing grain and arms. For being seen from the sea."

"It's for keeping us down," she said. "That's what it's for. My father's father hunted on this rock. Now there's a gate on it with holes in the roof, for pouring things on people's heads."

He did not argue, which she had not expected. He turned the onion in his hand and looked at the gatehouse, where two men were swinging a block of stone up on a wooden crane, a treadwheel turning beneath them with a boy walking inside it like a squirrel.

"Yes," he said at last. "That also is what it is for."

"And you build it anyway."

"I build it anyway." He threw the end of the onion into the grass. "Listen. When I was a boy, my master took me to a church in Lyon. Very old. Romans built some of it, then Burgundians, then Franks, then monks. Everyone who built it hated the people who built it before. Burned it, knocked it down, built on top. And my master puts my hand on one stone in the crypt and says, *Feel. That one, the Romans cut. Still true.* He meant the face of the stone was still flat. A thousand years. Everybody who paid for it, dead. Every reason for it, gone. Stone still true." He shrugged. "I do not decide what the king wants. I decide whether the stone is true."

"That's a comfortable thing to tell yourself."

"Yes," he agreed. "Very comfortable. I sleep like a baby." He stood up and brushed his knees. "Your mortar is too dry today. Too proud. A little water."

---

All summer the tower rose. Gwenllian learned the parts of it the way she had once learned the parts of a plough. There was the batter at the base, where the wall flared outward so that stones dropped from above would bounce into the attackers. There were putlog holes, square sockets left in the wall to hold the scaffolding, which would be stopped up with stone when the work was done. There were the arrow-loops, narrow on the outside and splayed wide on the inside, so that the archer could see a great deal and be seen very little. She hated the arrow-loops most of all. She said so to Marared, who was still carrying baskets and still not speaking to her entirely without spite.

"You hate them," Marared said, "but you mix the mortar that holds them up."

"I know."

"So what's the use of hating?"

Gwenllian did not have an answer. She went home that night to Dafydd. He had been minding the neighbours' goats for a ha'penny a week, and he wanted to know whether the English had pots of boiling oil yet. Was it true that the gate had a portcullis with iron teeth? Could he come and see it?

"No," she said.

"Why not?"

"Because it isn't for you to see. It's for you to be afraid of."

He considered this with the seriousness of nine years. "I'm not afraid of it."

"Good," she said. "Don't be." She kissed the top of his head, which smelled of goat, and lay awake a long time listening to the rain paying its rent on the roof.

---

In September, with the light already thinning, they began to stop up the putlog holes on the lower stages of the north-west tower. The scaffolding came down in sections, the poles stacked on the grass like bones. Masons went along the face of the wall on ladders, setting small squared stones into the sockets. The mortar for this was a finer mix, and Gwenllian made it in a smaller trough. She carried it up herself in a wooden hod, because the boy who usually did it had fallen and cracked his wrist.

The mason on the ladder was a young Englishman called Walter, who sang to himself without knowing the words. He took the hod from her at the top, set it on a hook and said, "Hold this," handing her the next stone. Then he swore and climbed down to fetch his trowel, which he had left at the bottom.

She stood alone on the ladder, three men high, with the stone in her hand and the open socket in front of her face.

It was dark inside the putlog hole. It went back into the wall the length of her forearm, and the wall around it was cold and damp and enormous. She thought of the castle finished, the scaffolding gone, the holes all stopped, the stone faces smooth and grey and true. It would sit on the rock looking at the sea for three hundred years, or a thousand, after everyone who had paid for it was dead and everyone who had carried for it was dead. After Dafydd was dead. After his children's children were dead.

She took the little knife from her belt. It was the knife she used for everything, for cheese and rushes and the splinters in Dafydd's feet. On the back face of the stone, the face that would be pressed into the wall and never seen, she cut letters. She did not know many. Her husband had known more and had taught her a few, one winter by the fire, scratching them in the ashes. She knew enough. The knife was blunt and the stone was hard, and the marks were shallow and crooked. But they were there.

**GWENLLIAN**, and beneath it, because there was room, **DAFYDD**.

She heard Walter on the ladder below her. She put the knife away and held out the stone, letter-side towards her own palm, and he took it without looking and buttered it with mortar and pushed it into the dark. He tapped it twice with the heel of the trowel, and then it was simply part of the wall, flush and grey, a small square among ten thousand others.

"Next," said Walter.

---

That evening, at the trough, Maître Jacques came by. He was looking along the north-west face with his head on one side, the way he did. She thought for a moment that he had seen, that someone had told him. Her heart beat in her throat. But he only said, "Walter's courses are drifting. Look. He steps down to the left. Every mason in England steps down to the left. I don't know why. Perhaps it is the beer." Then he looked at her. "You are quiet."

"I'm tired."

"Hm." He did not believe her, but he was too courteous, or too indifferent, to press. He picked up a little of the fine mortar on his fingers and pressed it with his thumb. It held. "Good. Exactly right."

"Proud?" she said.

"No. Today it is only right. Proud is for the big walls. For the small stones, you want it humble." He wiped his hand on his blue hood, which was so dusty by now that it made no difference. "The small stones do the hard work. Nobody looks at them. You stop the hole and you forget it. But if you take them out, the whole face weathers. Rain gets in. In two hundred years—" He made a crumbling gesture with his fingers. "So. Humble, and very good. The most important stones in the wall."

She looked at him for a long time. He looked back at her with the patience of a man who has spent his life waiting for mortar to set.

"Maître Jacques," she said, "do you ever put things in the walls? Things no one will see?"

He smiled his gap-toothed smile, and for a moment he looked very young.

"In Saint-Georges," he said, "in the bell-tower, there is a stone with my mother's name on the back. I was nineteen. My master would have beaten me blue." He shrugged. "Every mason does it, I think. Every one. These walls are full of names. Thousands of names, all facing inwards. The king thinks he is building a castle. He is building a—" He searched for the Welsh word and could not find it. "A book. A book that is closed."

"A book nobody can read."

"Nobody yet," said Maître Jacques. He picked up his hood and went off along the foot of the wall, frosted and small, stopping every twenty paces to lay his hand on a stone.

---

The castle was finished, more or less, three years later. By then Gwenllian had another cow, red with one white ear, and had gone back to the farm. She did not see the garrison march in. She heard about it from Marared, who heard it from the miller's girl. She did not go down to the shore again for a long time.

But Dafydd went. At twelve he was brave and foolish, as she had hoped he would be. He stood at the edge of the moat and looked up at the north-west tower. It was grey and smooth and enormous, and the evening sun lay flat across its face. He could not have said which of the ten thousand small square stones was the one his mother had told him about, that night by the fire when she taught him the letters of his name.

It did not matter. He knew it was there. He knew the castle had been built to make him afraid. And he knew that somewhere inside it, turned in towards the dark and holding the whole wall up, was his own name.

He was not afraid of it. He put his hand flat on the cold stone at the foot of the tower, the way he had once seen a small grey man do.

Then he went home for his supper.
