**The Sleeper**

The shark came up on the longline the morning after Inga's mother stopped speaking.

It wasn't a fight. Greenland sharks don't fight. Bjarni hauled the line hand over hand from the stern of the *Solveig*, cursing the weight and the cold and the hooks set for halibut, and then the water off the starboard side went dark and heavy. A shape rose out of it like a submerged log turning over, and then there was a head the width of a car door. It had a blunt snout, a mouth that drooped in what looked like weary disapproval, and an eye clouded white. A pale worm-like thing hung from the eye and trailed in the water like a ribbon.

"Christ," Bjarni said. "Look at the size of her."

Inga looked. She had been looking at the sea for an hour without seeing it. She'd been thinking about the hospice room in Ísafjörður, the radiator that ticked, her mother's hand on the blanket going still for long stretches and then lifting an inch, as if she were about to make a point and had thought better of it.

"Is she dead?" Inga asked.

"They're never dead. They're just slow." Bjarni leaned over the gunwale and peered down. "Five meters. Maybe more. You know how old something like that is?"

"Old."

"Older than old. There's a woman in Copenhagen who cuts out their eyes and dates the lenses. Some of them were born before Shakespeare." He wiped his nose on his sleeve. "Before the Reformation, maybe."

The shark rolled. Its skin was the grey-brown of a wet stone and scarred all over, pocked with copepods and old wounds that had healed into pale constellations. The hook was set in the corner of its jaw. It didn't thrash. It hung there, and Inga had the uncomfortable sense that it was waiting for them to understand something.

"What do we do?" she said.

"Cut the line. Lose the hook." Bjarni didn't move to do it. "Or we bring her in. The institute in Reykjavík pays for samples. Your mother would have—" He stopped.

"Would have what?"

"Nothing." He took his knife from his belt. "She liked these things. That's all. She used to say they were the only honest animals in the sea."

---

Her mother, Halldóra Sigurðardóttir, had been a marine biologist for forty years before she retired to the house above the harbor in Súðavík. Inga had grown up with her mother mostly absent, on research cruises in the Denmark Strait, at conferences in Bergen and Halifax, or bent over a microscope in the converted fish shed behind the house. When she was present she was distracted. Inga would say something at dinner and her mother would look up a beat too late, as if the words had reached her through a great depth of water.

"You're not listening," Inga would say at fourteen, at sixteen, at twenty.

"I am listening. I'm just listening slowly."

Inga left for university in Copenhagen and stayed away. She studied economics, which was the furthest thing from the sea she could think of. She married a Dane, divorced him, and built a life of spreadsheets and bright, well-heated offices. She called home on birthdays and at Christmas. The calls were short. Her mother asked about her work and Inga asked about the sharks, and both of them answered in full sentences that said almost nothing.

Then in September a doctor in Ísafjörður called, and Inga flew home. She found her mother small in a bed by a window, her hair gone white and loose. Her mother looked at her for a long time and said, "You came faster than I expected."

"It's been eleven years since I was here, Mamma."

"That's what I mean." And her mother smiled.

They had nine days of talk after that, more than in the previous decade. Then on the tenth day her mother stopped. The nurses said that happened, that the body began to economize. She still breathed and her eyes still opened. But the words had gone somewhere Inga couldn't follow.

So Inga had come out on the boat. Bjarni had been her mother's skipper for twenty years and had known Inga since she was in a snowsuit. He'd called the night before and said, "You need to get out of that room for a morning. Come fishing." She hadn't known how to refuse him.

---

Bjarni didn't cut the line. Instead he ran it to the winch and brought the shark alongside slowly, slowly, until its head rested against the hull and its great tail moved lazily in the green water behind. He looped a soft rope around its tail and tied it off, and for a while the three of them stayed like that, the boat and the man and the woman and the animal, rising and falling on the swell.

"I'm not killing her," Bjarni said, as if Inga had suggested it. "I'll take the hook out and let her go. But I want you to see her first."

"I've seen her."

"No you haven't." He crouched by the rail. "Come here."

Inga came. Up close the shark smelled of ammonia and deep cold. Her mother had told her once why: the flesh is full of a compound that keeps the cells from collapsing under pressure, and it makes them poisonous to eat fresh. The old Icelanders buried the meat in gravel for months and hung it to dry until it was *hákarl*, which tourists ate on dares and spat out.

"The parasite," Bjarni said, pointing to the pale ribbon trailing from the eye. "It eats the cornea. Most of the old ones are half blind."

"That's horrible."

"Is it? They live in the dark anyway. Down where they go, a thousand meters, two thousand, there's nothing to see." He shrugged. "She doesn't need eyes. She's got time."

Inga looked at the clouded eye. She thought, absurdly, that it was looking back at her.

"How do they hunt?" she asked. "If they're blind. If they're slow."

"Nobody knows exactly. Your mother had theories." Bjarni laughed softly. "Your mother always had theories. They find seals in their stomachs, you know. Whole seals. And reindeer once, and a polar bear. And how does a thing that swims slower than a man catch a seal?"

"How?"

"Seals sleep in the water. They hang there and doze." Bjarni looked down at the shark. "And she comes up out of the dark so slowly they never notice. She doesn't have to be fast. She just has to be patient longer than they can be careful."

---

He worked the hook out with pliers. It took a long time. The shark barely moved while he did it, except once, when its jaw shifted and Bjarni snatched his hand back and swore. Then he laughed at himself.

"She wouldn't have bitten," he said. "She's too tired of people to bother."

"How do you know it's a she?"

"No claspers. And the size. The females are bigger." He held up the hook, bent and bloody. "Your mother tagged one once. Off Scoresbysund, this was, '04 or '05. A female, six meters. They put a satellite tag on her and watched where she went for a year. You know what she did?"

"What?"

"Almost nothing. She went down and came up. Down and up, every day, following the light. She swam maybe a few hundred kilometers in a whole year." He tossed the hook into a bucket. "Your mother sat at the computer every night watching that little dot. Down and up. She was furious when the tag fell off."

Inga laughed despite herself. It surprised her, how the laugh came out. "I remember that. I remember her at the computer."

"You were—what, fourteen?"

"Fifteen. I wanted to use it to talk to my friends and she wouldn't get off it. I told her she cared more about a fish than about me."

Bjarni was quiet for a moment. The shark's tail moved slowly behind them.

"What did she say?"

"She said—" Inga stopped. She had forgotten this, or buried it. "She said the shark had been alive for two hundred years and I'd been alive for fifteen, and she was trying to learn something from the one that had more practice."

"That sounds like her."

"I thought it was the cruelest thing anyone had ever said to me."

"And now?"

Inga didn't answer. She looked at the shark. The parasite trailed from its eye like a pennant.

---

The scientist in Copenhagen had found the method by accident, Bjarni explained as they waited. He seemed reluctant to let the shark go, and Inga was reluctant too, though she couldn't have said why. The lens of a Greenland shark's eye keeps growing in layers all its life, like a tree or a pearl. The innermost core is laid down before birth and never changes. If you cut it out and measure the carbon in it, you can tell when the animal was born.

"They found one," Bjarni said, "four hundred years old. Give or take. Maybe as much as five hundred. Swimming around off Greenland when the Danes were still burning witches."

"That can't be right."

"That's what everyone said. But it's right." He looked at the animal alongside the boat. "They don't even breed till they're a hundred and fifty. Think of that. A hundred and fifty years of being a child."

"That's a long time to wait to grow up."

"Your mother said the same thing." He grinned. "She said it was the most optimistic thing she'd ever heard. That something could take that long and still get there."

Inga looked at him. "When did she say that?"

"Oh, I don't know. Years ago. On this boat." Bjarni hesitated. "She talked about you a great deal, you know. Out here. More than she ever did on land, I think."

"What did she say?"

"That you were the clever one. That you'd figured out how to live in the world, which she never had." He rubbed his jaw. "That she hadn't been a good mother and knew it, and didn't know how to be a better one without becoming someone else."

The wind had come up a little. The *Solveig* tugged at the rope and the shark swung with it, heavy as a sunken boat.

"She never said that to me," Inga said.

"No. Well." Bjarni looked at the water. "She was slow about things."

---

They let the shark go a little before noon. Bjarni untied the tail rope and pushed at the animal's flank with a boathook. For a long moment nothing happened, and Inga thought, with a sudden lurch of grief that surprised her, *it's dead, we've killed it.*

Then the tail moved, once, a long slow sweep. The great head turned from the hull. The shark slid away from the boat without hurry. It sank as it went, and the water closed green over its back, then darker green, then black. The last thing Inga saw was the pale ribbon from its eye, flickering for a moment against the dark like a hand lifting from a blanket.

"There she goes," Bjarni said. "Back to sleep."

"She'll be fine?"

"She'll outlive us both. She'll outlive our grandchildren." He started coiling the longline. "Some day a hundred years from now, somebody will pull her up and look at that scar in her jaw and wonder who put it there."

Inga stayed at the rail and watched the place where the shark had gone. The surface was already smoothing over. In a minute there would be no sign it had ever been there at all.

"Bjarni," she said. "Take me back now. Please."

---

The radiator in the hospice room was still ticking when she came in. The light had gone the thin gold of an October afternoon. Her mother lay as Inga had left her, eyes half open, breathing slowly. The nurse looked up from the chair in the corner and said, "No change," and then, seeing Inga's face, quietly left the room.

Inga sat on the edge of the bed. She still smelled of the boat, of diesel and ammonia and cold salt. She took her mother's hand.

"We caught a shark today," she said. "Bjarni and me. A Greenland. A big female, five meters, with that parasite in her eye. He took the hook out and we let her go."

Her mother's breath went in, went out.

"He told me about the satellite tag. The one at Scoresbysund. Do you remember? I was so angry with you. I was so angry with you for so long." Inga's voice wavered and she let it. "He said you told him she was the most optimistic thing you'd ever heard of. A hundred and fifty years to grow up and still getting there."

The radiator ticked.

"I think I understand now," Inga said. "I think I've been the slow one. I think it took me this long."

She sat with her mother's hand in hers and watched the light move across the blanket. She didn't know how long. She wasn't counting. Outside, the fjord had gone from gold to grey, and the lights of the fishing boats were coming on one by one along the harbor.

At some point her mother's hand moved.

It wasn't much, an inch, maybe less. The fingers tightened and then loosened around Inga's. Her mother's eyes, which had been fixed on the window, turned slowly toward her. They were clouded and very pale, and they seemed to be looking up from a great distance, as if from a great depth of water.

"Inga," her mother said. Her voice was barely there. "You came back."

"I came back, Mamma."

Her mother's lips moved again. Inga leaned close.

"Was she—" a breath "—old?"

Inga laughed. It came out wet and broken, and she put her forehead down on the blanket beside her mother's hand and laughed until it turned into something else.

"Yes," she said, when she could. "Yes, Mamma. She was very old. Bjarni thinks two hundred years. Maybe more. She had scars all over her. She barely moved. She just hung there in the water next to the boat as if she had all the time in the world."

Her mother's fingers moved against her hair, very slowly.

"Good," her mother whispered. "Good. That's how you do it."

"How you do what?"

But her mother had closed her eyes. Her breathing stayed slow and even. After a while, Inga understood she wasn't going to say anything more. Not tonight, and perhaps not ever.

Inga stayed. The nurse came back and went away again. Night settled over the fjord, black and deep, and somewhere far out beyond the harbor mouth, at a depth where no light had ever reached, a blind shark the size of a small boat was rising toward the surface. It rose slowly, with patience, toward something sleeping that did not yet know it was there.

Inga held her mother's hand and listened. She listened slowly, and for once she was in no hurry at all.
