**The Inner Voices**

The first thing Magdalena learned about Herr Kapellmeister Anselm Vogt was that he hummed in the wrong key when he was lying.

She learned it in October of 1738, three weeks after her father died and left her nothing but a debt to the chandler, a box of quills, and a letter of recommendation addressed to the court at Weissenau. The letter said her father had been a copyist of the first rank, and that his daughter had assisted him since the age of nine, and that her hand was clean, her ink was black, and she did not make errors. All of this was true. What the letter did not say, because her father had not known how to say it, was that for the last four years his eyes had gone soft as wet paper, and that it was Magdalena, not he, who had copied every part that left their house.

Vogt read the letter standing at the window of his music room, holding it very close to his face and then very far away, as if he could not decide where the words were hiding.

"Your father wrote a fine hand," he said.

"Yes, Herr Kapellmeister."

"Hm." He hummed something, a little run of notes, and set the letter down. "We need someone for the Advent cantatas. Twelve parts each, four cantatas, and the Duke wants the new Magnificat by Christmas Eve. Can you do it?"

"Yes."

"Hm." The humming again, tuneless, a half-step flat of where it ought to sit. "Then you'll start tomorrow. There's a room above the stables. It's warm because of the horses. It smells because of the horses."

She would understand only later that he had not been asking whether she could copy.

---

The music room at Weissenau was long and narrow, with a harpsichord at one end and a writing desk at the other, and between them a sea of paper. Vogt worked at the desk in the mornings and at the harpsichord in the afternoons, and in the evenings he walked the gallery of the east wing with his hands clasped behind him, and the servants said he was composing in his head, and stayed out of his way.

Magdalena worked at a small table near the stove. He would bring her a score—a full score, all the voices stacked one above another like the floors of a house—and she would draw out each part: the first violins on their own sheet, the seconds on theirs, the oboes, the bassoon, the continuo with its little figures beneath, the four singers. It was work she had done since childhood. She could do it half asleep. She could do it, she sometimes thought, with her eyes closed.

That was why she noticed, in the second week, that he was not.

It was a small thing. In the alto line of the third cantata, in the seventeenth measure, the note sat on the space where it should have sat on the line—an F where any sense said G. A slip of the pen. She marked it with a tiny cross in pencil and brought the score to him.

"Herr Kapellmeister, measure seventeen, the alto—"

He took the page and held it close to his face, then far away.

"It's an F," he said.

"Yes. But the tenor has the F, and the harmony—"

"It's an F," he said again, and hummed, and the humming was wrong.

She went back to her table and copied the F. The next day, rehearsing in the chapel, the alto sang it, and the whole chord curdled like milk, and Vogt, standing at the front with his roll of paper beating the time, did not stop. He did not even seem to hear. When the cantata was finished he said, "Good. Again on Thursday," and walked out.

That night, in the warm room above the horses, Magdalena lay awake and listened to the animals shift and breathe, and thought about her father holding a page against his nose and saying *Lena, my love, read me this line, the light is poor tonight.*

---

She did not say anything. That was the second thing she learned at Weissenau: how not to say anything.

The next score he brought her had three errors. The one after that had five. They were not errors of the mind—she could see that the music itself was good, better than good, music that turned corners you did not expect and then made you feel you had expected them all along. They were errors of the hand. A note one step off. A stem pointing nowhere. A whole measure of the second violins left empty, as if the pen had simply passed over it.

She began to fix them.

She did it carefully at first, only the obvious ones. The alto who should have sung G sang G. The second violins got their missing measure—she wrote it in herself, looking at the firsts and the viola and finding the line that fit between them like a key in a lock. Nobody noticed. The music went into the chapel and came out sounding the way it ought to sound, and Vogt beat the time and said "Good. Again on Thursday," and walked out.

By the middle of November she was fixing perhaps a dozen things a day, and the dozen things were becoming harder to call errors. There was a passage in the Magnificat, the *Deposuit potentes*—*he hath put down the mighty from their seat*—where the score as he had written it was simply a smear, ink dragged across the staves by a sleeve or a hand that did not know where the page was. She could make out the bass. She could make out most of the soprano. The middle was gone.

She sat with it for two hours. Then she wrote the middle.

She wrote the inner voices the way she imagined he would have written them—or the way she imagined a man who wrote the bass and soprano he had written would have written them. She gave the tenors a long falling line, a pulling-down, because the words were about the mighty being pulled down, and she gave the altos a little stubborn figure that would not fall, that kept climbing back up, because she thought the mighty would not go quietly. When it was done she played it through in her head and it made the back of her neck go cold.

She copied the parts, and gave them to the singers, and on Thursday she stood at the back of the chapel and listened.

It sounded the way it had sounded in her head. The tenors fell. The altos fought. And at the front of the chapel Anselm Vogt lowered his roll of paper and stood very still, and did not say "Good," and did not say "Again on Thursday." He said nothing at all. He stood with his back to the singers for a long moment, and then he said, "Thank you," in a voice nobody had heard from him before, and walked out.

---

He came to the stable room that night.

She heard him on the ladder first—a slow climb, a pause on each rung—and then his head appeared through the hatch, gray hair standing up like a dandelion gone to seed. He did not come all the way in. He rested his arms on the floorboards and looked toward her, though she was not sure, in the light of the one candle, how much of her he could see.

"The *Deposuit*," he said.

She sat up on her straw mattress and pulled the blanket around her shoulders. "Yes, Herr Kapellmeister."

"That's not what I wrote."

"No."

"What did I write?"

She considered lying. She considered it for perhaps one breath, and then she thought of her father, and of the chandler, and of the long road back to nothing, and she found that she was too tired to lie.

"I don't know," she said. "It was smeared. I couldn't read it. I wrote what I thought should go there."

A horse below them blew out its breath. Vogt rested his chin on his folded arms.

"How long?" he said.

"How long what?"

"How long have you been writing my music for me?"

"I haven't been writing it. Only fixing it. Only—" She stopped. "Since the third cantata. The alto F."

"The alto F." He laughed, short and dry. "I wondered about that. I heard it in the chapel. I heard it and I thought, *that's wrong*, and then I thought, *but I wrote it, so it must be right*, and then I thought, *Anselm, you old fool, you can't see your own hand anymore, how would you know what you wrote?*" He was quiet a moment. "Then the next week it was a G. Somebody had made it a G. I thought it was the alto, being clever. Was it you?"

"Yes."

"Hm." He hummed. It was in tune. "Do you know how long I've been like this?"

"No."

"Two years. Getting worse every winter. The light goes and it doesn't come all the way back in spring. I can see shapes. I can see you're there. I can see the candle, and I can see that the candle is making a halo the size of a cartwheel, which I suspect it is not." He shifted on the ladder. "I can still hear it all. That's the cruelty of it. I lie awake and I hear the whole Magnificat, every voice, plain as I hear that horse. And then I sit down to write it and I put the pen on the paper and the paper is—a field of fog. I put the notes where I think they go. Sometimes they go there."

"You could have told someone."

"Told whom? The Duke?" He made a noise. "The Duke keeps a Kapellmeister the way he keeps a clock. If the clock stops telling the time, you don't nurse it. You buy another clock. I have a wife buried in the churchyard here and a daughter married in Leipzig who writes to me twice a year, and I have this—" a small wave of the hand, meaning the whole palace, the chapel, the music room, the gallery of the east wing where he walked in the evenings, composing in his head— "and I am sixty-one years old, and no one is going to give a blind man a chapel."

The candle hissed. Magdalena looked at her hands.

"My father was like you," she said. "At the end."

"I know. I read the letter."

"The letter didn't say—"

"The letter said his hand was clean and his ink was black and he did not make errors." Vogt's voice was gentle. "I copied parts myself for twenty years, girl. Nobody's hand is clean in their sixtieth year. I knew someone else had been holding the pen. I thought—" He stopped. "I suppose I thought it might be useful. To have someone who knew how to keep a secret like that."

---

They made an arrangement. Neither of them called it that.

In the mornings he sat at the harpsichord and played, and sang the lines he could not write, his old cracked voice going up into the soprano and down into the bass and through everything in between, and Magdalena sat at the desk and took it down. He played the oboe line with his right hand and hummed the first violin and sang the tenor words, *et exaltavit humiles*, and she caught it all and pinned it to the staves like a lepidopterist with a tray of moths.

Sometimes he could not hold all the voices at once. He would play the outer lines—the top and the bottom, the soprano and the bass, the bones of the thing—and then stop, and frown, and say, "There's something in the middle. I can hear that there's something. I can't—" and press his fingertips to his eyes as if he could push the sight back in.

"Like this?" she would say, and sing a line.

"No."

"Like this?"

"No—closer. Closer. Lower in the second half."

"Like this?"

And sometimes he would say *yes*, and sometimes he would say *no, but leave it, it's better than what I had*, and once, only once, in the *Gloria Patri*, he listened to what she sang and was quiet for so long that she thought he had fallen asleep, and then he said, "Where did you learn that?"

"Learn what?"

"That suspension. The way you hold the seventh over the bar line and make them wait for it."

"I didn't learn it anywhere," she said. "It just seemed like the place to make them wait."

He turned his face toward her. In the morning light from the window she could see the cloudiness in his eyes, the milky veil over the brown.

"Lena," he said—the first time he had used the name—"how long have you been writing music?"

She thought about lying again. She was getting worse at thinking about it.

"Since I was eleven," she said. "Not—properly. Not anything anyone has heard. Just at night. On the backs of my father's spoiled sheets." She swallowed. "I used to copy out other men's music all day and then at night I'd write down what I thought they should have done instead."

Vogt laughed so hard he had to hold the edge of the harpsichord.

"*Should have done instead*," he said. "God in heaven. Every composer in the Empire would hang you from the nearest chapel bell." He wiped his eyes. "Did you ever correct me? Before the—before?"

"Twice," she admitted. "In the first cantata. The viola. But I put it back."

"Why?"

"Because it was yours."

He was quiet again. Then he reached over, fumbling a little, and found her wrist, and gave it a single firm squeeze, the way a man might seal a contract.

"Don't put it back anymore," he said.

---

The Magnificat was performed on Christmas Eve, in the chapel at Weissenau, by candlelight, with the Duke and his family in the gallery and the snow coming down outside the tall windows in long gray curtains.

Magdalena stood at the back, as she always did, behind the last row of the household staff. She had copied every part herself. She had written, by her own count, perhaps a third of the inner voices, and some of the outer ones, and a good deal of the *Gloria Patri*, and the long held seventh that made them wait. She knew where every one of her notes was. She could have pointed to each of them on the page like stars.

And still, when the choir came to the *Deposuit*, and the tenors began to fall and the altos began to climb back up, she found that she could not tell anymore which notes were hers and which were his. They had grown together. They had braided into a single line, the way two voices in a duet stop being two voices and become a third thing that neither could have made alone.

At the front of the chapel Anselm Vogt stood with his roll of paper and beat the time. He did not look at the score in front of him. He did not need to. He knew it the way you know a room in the dark—where the chairs are, where the step down is, where the window is that lets the cold in. When the last chord came he held it a long time, longer than was proper, until the sound had filled the chapel up to the painted ceiling and hung there among the gilt angels, and then he closed his hand and the sound stopped.

The Duke applauded from the gallery. Then everyone applauded, because the Duke had.

Afterward, in the cold corridor outside the music room, while the household went to its supper and the snow went on falling, Vogt found her by the sound of her shoes on the stone.

"Well," he said. "Did they hear it?"

"Hear what?"

"You. Did they hear you in it?"

She thought about the Duke, applauding. She thought about the alto who had sung the stubborn climbing figure, a baker's wife from the village, who had come off the riser with tears on her face and not known why.

"No," Magdalena said. "I don't think so. I think they only heard the music."

Vogt nodded slowly. He hummed something—a little run of notes, the opening of the *Deposuit*, the tenors' long fall.

It was perfectly in tune.

"That's the best anyone can hope for," he said. "Come. There's a cantata for Epiphany, and I've already got the bass line in my head, and I can hear something in the middle that I can't quite reach."

"Like this?" she said, and began to sing.
