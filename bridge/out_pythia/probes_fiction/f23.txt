**The Water-Keeper**

The water had to be drawn before dawn, because water drawn in daylight was warm and lazy, and lazy water ran slow. Nikias had told the magistrates this many times, and they had laughed every time, so he no longer told them. He simply went to the fountain house at the edge of the Agora while the stars were still out, filled his two jars from the lion's mouth, and carried them back across the empty square with the yoke cutting into his shoulders.

He had kept the clocks of the law courts for twenty-two years. Before that he had been a boy in Thrace with another name, but he did not think about that boy often. The boy had not known that time could be poured.

The clock was a simple thing. It was a clay vessel the size of a child, painted with a band of black around the neck, with a bronze spout near its base plugged by a peg of wax-sealed wood. When a speaker rose to plead, Nikias pulled the peg. The water ran out into a lower vessel, and when the upper one was empty the speaker was finished, whether he had finished or not. Six measures for the accuser. Six for the accused. When a witness's testimony was read, Nikias pushed the peg back in with his thumb and the city's time stopped, and the only sound in the court was the clerk's flat voice and the shuffling of five hundred jurors on their benches.

He liked that moment. He would never have said so, but he liked it.

---

On the morning of the trial of Philokles son of Demeas, a man was waiting for him at the fountain house.

Nikias knew him only slightly. Kallias was a freedman who did things for people with money. He wore a good cloak badly, as if it were borrowed, and he was leaning against the cold stone with his arms folded.

"You're early," Kallias said.

"The water is early," said Nikias. "I follow it."

Kallias laughed as if this were a fine joke. He watched Nikias set down the jars and position the first beneath the lion's mouth. The water came out in a thick, unhurried rope, silver in the starlight.

"Philokles is a generous man," Kallias said.

"I have heard he is rich. That isn't the same."

"In his case it is." Kallias unfolded his arms. In one palm sat a small leather purse. It clinked when he moved it, a soft, fat sound. "He is accused of things he did not do. Everyone knows the accuser, Lysandros, is a liar with a sharp tongue. Lysandros will speak first. If he speaks for a little less time than he expects, he will lose the thread. He always does. He memorizes his speeches like a schoolboy and if he is cut short he has nothing."

Nikias said nothing. The first jar was full. He moved the second into place.

"It is a small thing," Kallias went on. "A pinch of wax in the spout before he speaks. A little less in it before Philokles speaks. No one measures the water after you. No one would know."

"I would know."

"You would know," Kallias agreed, pleasantly, "and you would be richer. Forty drachmas, Nikias. You could buy back your own body with forty drachmas and some saving. Didn't you tell someone once that you were saving?"

Nikias had told someone once. He had told the clerk Ameinias, years ago, in a wine shop, and Ameinias apparently told everyone everything.

"Put the purse away," Nikias said. "There's a watchman at the corner."

Kallias put it away. He was smiling. "Think about it while you walk. It's a long walk with those jars." He pushed himself off the wall. "I'll be in the court. If you do it, touch your ear before you pull the peg. I'll find you after."

He went off toward the Painted Stoa, whistling, and Nikias stood listening to the second jar fill until it overflowed and soaked his feet.

---

The walk was not long. That was a lie Kallias had told to make it seem as if Nikias would have time to change his mind, and therefore to make him feel he had already half-agreed. Nikias understood this. He had listened to speeches for twenty-two years. He knew how men built little rooms with words and then invited you to sit down inside them.

Still, he thought about it.

Forty drachmas. He had sixty-one, buried in a pot under the floor of the storeroom where he slept, behind the house of the magistrate who owned him. His price, if his master could be persuaded to sell him to himself, would be three hundred, perhaps more. He was old now, so perhaps less. He did not know whether it was better to be worth less.

The court was a long building with open sides, and in the early light the benches were empty and smelled of old wood and olive oil and the sweat of yesterday's jurors. Nikias set down his jars, unhooked the yoke, and rubbed his shoulders. Then he went to the clock.

It stood on its stone base beside the speaker's platform. He ran his hand down its side, the way he always did, feeling for cracks. There were none. He took out the peg, checked the spout for grit, and poured the first measure in. He watched the waterline climb to the first painted mark and stopped exactly there.

"You talk to it, you know," said a voice behind him.

It was Ameinias the clerk, carrying his tablets and a basket of jury tokens, his bald head shining. He was a citizen, poor and cheerful and incapable of silence.

"I don't talk to it."

"You do. You say things under your breath. I've heard you." Ameinias set his basket down. "What do you say?"

Nikias considered lying, and found he was tired of lying already that morning, though he hadn't told any lies yet. "I tell it to be honest."

Ameinias laughed. "And is it?"

"It's water. Water is always honest. It only goes down." Nikias pushed the peg in. "Men are the ones who aren't."

"Hah. You should have been a philosopher."

"Philosophers have to talk," said Nikias. "I'd rather pour."

---

The jurors came in as the sun cleared the roofs. Five hundred and one of them, old men mostly, because old men had the time and needed the three obols the city paid them. They carried cushions and bread and their own opinions. They sat down noisily and argued about the price of fish.

Lysandros arrived first. He was a thin young man with a long neck and quick eyes, and he walked to the front without looking at anyone, his lips moving. Nikias recognized the motion. The man was reciting his speech to himself. Kallias had been right about that, at least.

Philokles came later, surrounded by friends, a big soft man in his fifties with rings on three fingers and a face that had grown used to being agreed with. He did not look at Nikias. Men like Philokles never looked at the clock-keeper. It was one of the reasons Nikias had liked his work.

And Kallias was there, on the end of the third bench, his borrowed cloak arranged around him. He caught Nikias's eye and did nothing. He did not need to.

The presiding magistrate called the court to order. Ameinias read the charge: that Philokles son of Demeas had defrauded the orphaned children of his late brother, taking from their inheritance a workshop of twelve shield-makers and the silver of a mine lease at Laurion, and had concealed this by means of false accounts. Lysandros was the children's guardian, appointed by the archon. The penalty sought was restitution twice over.

Nikias had heard a hundred cases like it. Sometimes the accused was guilty and sometimes the accuser was a vulture. Often both. He had stopped trying to tell which. It was not his work to tell. His work was the water.

"The accuser will speak," said the magistrate.

Lysandros rose and climbed onto the platform. He turned toward the clock, as all speakers did, as if to make sure it was there.

Nikias put his hand on the peg.

He thought about the pot under the floor. Sixty-one drachmas. He thought about the storeroom with its smell of onions and oil, and how he had learned to sleep curled against the amphorae because the floor was warmest there. He thought about the boy in Thrace, who had once run very fast down a hillside after a goat, and fallen, and laughed. He had not thought of that in years. He did not know why it came to him now.

He did not touch his ear.

He pulled the peg.

---

The water ran. Lysandros began.

He was good. Nikias had to admit it within the first few breaths. The young man did not have a fine voice, but he used it carefully, softly at first so the jurors leaned in, and then louder. He told them about the children: two boys and a girl, the eldest nine. He told them how their father had died of fever on campaign at Potidaea, and how he had made his brother Philokles swear to look after them, and how Philokles had sworn.

"He swore by Zeus of Oaths," Lysandros said. "And within a year the shield workshop had a new owner, a man who turned out, when I inquired, to be the cousin of Philokles's wife. And within two years, the children were eating barley like slaves."

A murmur went over the benches. Nikias watched the waterline. It was sinking steadily past the black band.

Lysandros called for testimony. Nikias pushed the peg in. The clerk read a deposition from a shieldmaker who said he had been told to change his marks. The water stood still and Nikias stood with it, his thumb on the wood, while the city waited.

Peg out. The water ran. Lysandros spoke of the accounts. He held up a wax tablet and described the figures. Halfway through, he stopped.

It was a small stop, barely a breath. But Nikias saw his lips move again, silently, searching. The young man had lost his place. The jurors shifted.

Kallias, on the third bench, turned his head very slightly toward Nikias. His face asked a question.

Lysandros found his place. He went on. The water was low now, the last finger's breadth trembling in the bottom of the vessel. Lysandros saw it and spoke faster, and the speed made him clumsy, and he said something about the mine lease that even Nikias could tell was muddled. Then the last of the water gurgled out of the spout, and Nikias said, as he had said thousands of times, "The water is finished."

Lysandros stopped in the middle of a sentence. He stood there a moment with his mouth open. Then he climbed down.

It had been fair, Nikias told himself. Every drop. He had cheated no one. The man had simply lost his thread, as men did.

He refilled the clock to the mark.

---

Philokles did not speak for himself at first. He had paid a man to write his speech and he read it in a smooth, wounded voice. He spoke of his love for his brother, of the expenses of raising three children, of the workshop's debts, which had been hidden from him, which he had nobly paid. He wept at one point. Nikias, who had seen many men weep in court, thought it was quite good weeping.

He called witnesses. Peg in. Peg out. The water ran and stopped and ran.

And somewhere in the middle of it, Nikias noticed a man in the back of the court.

Not a juror. A boy, really, perhaps fifteen, standing behind the last bench among the idlers who came to watch trials the way other people watched cockfights. He was thin and badly dressed, and he was holding the hand of a smaller boy, and the smaller boy was holding the hand of a girl of perhaps five, who was sucking her thumb.

No. Not fifteen. The eldest was nine, Lysandros had said. Nikias looked again. The tall one was older than the others, a slave boy perhaps, or a neighbor's son minding them. The two smaller ones were watching Philokles weep with an expression Nikias could not name.

He had seen that expression before. He had worn it, once, on a ship's deck, watching a coast slide away.

The water ran low. Philokles saw it, and with the ease of a man who had paid for his speech to be exactly the right length, he came to the end of his last sentence just as the last drop fell.

"The water is finished," said Nikias.

---

There was a second round of speeches in cases like this, shorter. Two measures each. Lysandros rose again, and now Nikias could see the anger in him and the fear, and he knew the young man would not do well. He was the kind who did better when calm.

Kallias was looking at him.

Nikias filled the clock to the second mark. His hands were steady. They had always been steady. He placed his hand on the peg.

And then he did a thing he had never done in twenty-two years. He turned and looked out at the court, all of it, the five hundred old men with their cushions and the magistrate and the clerk and Kallias and the children at the back. He looked at them as if he were one of them, as if he had a vote.

He did not have a vote. He had the water.

He pulled the peg. The water ran. Lysandros spoke.

He spoke badly, as Nikias had known he would. He repeated himself. He pointed at Philokles and called him a thief, which made the jurors uncomfortable, because they did not like to be told what to think. He was running out of time and he knew it, and his eyes kept going to the clock.

And Nikias, standing beside it, did something with his body that he would later tell himself was an accident.

He shifted his weight. He leaned, slightly, against the clock's stone base, as an old man leans when his back aches. And the clock, on its base, tilted. The smallest degree. A hair. Not enough that anyone would see.

Enough that the water, which was always honest and only went down, went down a little slower.

Nikias had known for twenty years that it would do this. He had never once tested it. He had simply known, the way he knew that warm water ran lazy.

Lysandros, glancing at the clock, saw that he had more time than he feared. Nikias watched him see it. He watched the young man's shoulders come down from around his ears. And Lysandros took a breath, and stopped pointing, and turned instead toward the back of the court, and said, quite quietly:

"Men of Athens. They are here. Look at them. Then vote however you like."

Five hundred heads turned.

The water ran out. Nikias straightened his back and the clock settled level on its base, and he said, "The water is finished," and his voice did not shake at all.

---

Philokles's second speech was smooth and fine, and Nikias kept the clock perfectly level for every drop of it. He was careful about that. He would not have been able to say why it mattered, but it did.

The jurors voted by dropping bronze tokens into urns. Ameinias counted them aloud. Two hundred and ninety-four to two hundred and seven. Against Philokles.

The big man's face went from pink to white and stayed there. His friends closed around him. Lysandros sat down on the platform step as if his legs had given out and put his face in his hands. At the back of the court, the girl took her thumb out of her mouth and asked something, and the tall boy bent down to answer her.

Kallias was gone from the third bench. Nikias had not seen him go.

---

He found Nikias that evening, at the fountain house, where Nikias had gone to rinse out the jars. The light was purple and the lion's mouth was still pouring its silver rope, as it had for longer than either of them had been alive.

"You didn't touch your ear," Kallias said.

"No."

"I watched you. You did something. At the end." Kallias's voice was not angry. It was curious, which was worse. "What did you do?"

Nikias tipped a jar and let the water slosh out across the stones. "I leaned on the clock. My back hurts. I'm old."

Kallias looked at him for a long time. Then, to Nikias's surprise, he laughed, a short, real laugh, not the pleasant one from the morning.

"You know what Philokles will say. He'll say the clock was crooked."

"Let him. Let them measure it. It's level. It's always been level." Nikias set the jar down. "I'm the one who isn't."

Kallias shook his head. He reached into his cloak, and for one cold moment Nikias thought of knives. But it was only the purse again. Kallias weighed it in his palm.

"He'll want this back, of course. But he doesn't know exactly how much was in it." He took out a coin, then another, and set them on the edge of the fountain, on the wet stone. Two drachmas. "For your back."

"I don't want it."

"I know you don't. That's why it's funny." Kallias turned to go, then stopped. "Those children. Did you know they'd be there?"

"No."

"Lysandros brought them. That was clever. He'll go far, if he learns to remember his speeches." Kallias pulled his borrowed cloak tighter. "So will you, water-keeper. Somewhere."

He walked off into the dusk.

Nikias looked at the two coins for a long while. Then he picked them up, and after a moment, he dropped them one at a time into the basin beneath the lion's mouth, where they sank and lay glinting on the bottom among the coins of a hundred years of wishes.

He filled his jars. They would be warm by morning and he would have to draw fresh ones before dawn, but he filled them anyway, because it was what his hands knew how to do. He hooked them to the yoke and set it on his shoulders and walked home across the empty Agora, under the first stars, the water slopping gently with every step, going down, as it always did, only down.
