**Lake Effect**

The email came on a Tuesday, sandwiched between a dental reminder and a coupon for windshield wipers. Denise almost deleted it. The subject line read *Sample Clearance Inquiry – "Lake Effect" (1995)*, and for a moment she thought it was a scam, the kind that wanted your bank details in exchange for a fortune that didn't exist.

Then she read the first line. *We are reaching out to the rights holders of the recording "Lake Effect" by Northcoast Science, released on Glass Block Records, catalog number GB-001.*

Nobody had said the words *Northcoast Science* to her in twenty-five years. She sat back from the kitchen table and looked out the window at the gray sky over Euclid Avenue. It was March, and the sky had that heavy, undecided look, like it might snow or rain or just keep pressing down on everybody until June.

Glass Block Records had been her brother's idea. GB-001 was the only catalog number it ever had.

---

In the summer of 1995, Marcus was nineteen and Denise was sixteen, and their mother's basement smelled like mildew and solder. Marcus had bought an Ensoniq sampler from a man in Parma who was moving to Arizona and said he didn't need it anymore. He paid four hundred dollars, most of it from two summers of cutting grass and a little of it, Denise suspected, from a coffee can their mother kept behind the flour.

"You can't tell her," he said, the first time Denise came down the stairs and saw the machine glowing on the card table.

"Tell her what?"

"Anything. Just don't tell her anything."

Tavon was there, too, sitting on an overturned milk crate with a spiral notebook on his knee. Tavon lived three houses down and had been in and out of their kitchen since he was seven, eating their cereal and calling their mother Miss Gloria with a politeness that made her soften even when she was mad. He wrote in the notebook in tiny, slanted handwriting, and he wouldn't let anyone read it.

"What's it for?" Denise asked.

Marcus grinned. He had a gap between his front teeth that their mother had always meant to fix and never had the money for. "We're making a record."

"A real one?"

"Vinyl," Tavon said, without looking up. "Twelve-inch. Like grown folks."

---

Denise read the email three times, then called the number at the bottom. A young man answered, cheerful, with the kind of voice that sounded like it was standing in a room full of plants.

"So, yeah, there's an artist—I can't say who yet, NDA stuff—but the producer found the record at a shop in Detroit. He's obsessed with it. The drum break and the little horn stab, the thing that goes *bah-dah*—"

"I know the thing," Denise said.

"Right, of course. So we need to clear the master and the composition. Our records show the label as Glass Block, and the publishing as—honestly, our records don't show much. Are you the right person?"

Denise looked at the wall where, in a cheap black frame, a single twelve-inch sleeve hung. White, with a hand-stamped logo: a square divided into smaller squares, like the glass block windows in every bathroom on the east side. Beneath it, in Marcus's careful marker: *NORTHCOAST SCIENCE – LAKE EFFECT b/w COLD FRONT (INSTRUMENTAL)*.

"My brother passed in 2003," she said. "I'm his executor. So I guess I'm half the right person."

"And the other half?"

"I'll have to find him."

---

They spent the whole summer of '95 on four minutes and twelve seconds.

Marcus found the drums on a record from their grandfather's collection, a gospel album with a choir on the cover in matching purple robes. Between two hymns, the drummer played a little fill, two bars, loose and a little late, like he was smiling while he did it. Marcus looped it until Denise heard it in her sleep.

The horn stab came from somewhere else—Marcus would never tell her where. "Trade secret," he said. "If I tell you, I gotta kill you, and then Mama's gonna kill me, and then who's gonna finish the record?"

Tavon's verses took longer. He'd come down the stairs in the evening after his shift at the car wash, his hands still pruney, and he'd sit on the milk crate and scratch things out.

"Read me some," Denise said one night in August. The basement was the only cool place in the house.

"No."

"Just one line."

He looked at her for a long time. Tavon had a way of looking at people like he was deciding whether they were going to be in a story he wrote later.

"Okay," he said. "One line."

He read it quietly, not rapping, just reading, the way you'd read a grocery list:

"*Snow comes sideways off the water, so you learn to walk crooked / every block's a little church if you know where to look in.*"

Denise didn't say anything.

"It's not done," Tavon said.

"It's good."

"It's not done," he said again, but he was smiling, and he closed the notebook gently, like there was something alive inside.

---

Finding Tavon took two days and an awkward conversation with a woman at the AutoZone on Lee Road who used to date his cousin. He was living in Akron now, she said. He worked for the county. Water department, maybe. Or roads.

Denise drove down on a Saturday. The address was a small brick house with a clean porch and a basketball hoop over the garage, the net new and white. A girl of maybe twelve answered the door, wearing headphones around her neck.

"Is your—is Tavon Wright here?"

"Dad!" the girl yelled over her shoulder, without taking her eyes off Denise. "There's a lady!"

He came to the door wiping his hands on a dish towel. He was heavier than she remembered, and his hair was mostly gone, and he wore reading glasses pushed up on his forehead. But when he saw her, his face did the same thing it used to do when he looked at people—that pause, that deciding.

"Denise Holloway," he said.

"Hey, Tavon."

"Lord." He put the towel over his shoulder. "You look just like your mother."

"I'm older than she was, now, the last time you saw her."

He nodded slowly, like that was a thing that needed some room. "Come in. Kayla, go do something somewhere else."

"Like what?"

"Like *anything*."

---

The record came back from the pressing plant in October 1995, five hundred copies in two cardboard boxes that Marcus carried into the house like they were full of eggs. He'd paid for them with money from the coffee can, all of it this time, and Denise knew because she'd checked.

Their mother found out that night.

Denise remembered sitting on the stairs, out of sight, while their mother stood in the kitchen with the empty can in her hand. She didn't yell. That was the worst part. Gloria Holloway was a yeller, a door-slammer, a woman who could make the plates rattle in the cabinet. But that night she just stood there.

"That was the light bill," she said. "And Denise's school shoes. And something for Christmas."

"I'm gonna pay it back."

"With what?"

"With the record, Mama. It's gonna—people are gonna buy it. I'm gonna take it to the stations, to the stores. Tavon's on it, he's good, he's real good, you'd—you should hear it."

"I don't want to hear it."

"Mama—"

"I said I don't want to *hear* it, Marcus."

He came down to the basement afterward and sat on the floor next to the boxes. Denise came down too. She didn't say anything. After a while, he pulled one record out of the box and slid it out of its sleeve, and he put it on the turntable, and he turned the volume down very low, so low you could barely hear the drums from the purple-robed choir album, the little late fill, the horn stab that went *bah-dah*.

And then Tavon's voice, low and steady:

*Snow comes sideways off the water, so you learn to walk crooked—*

"It's good, Marcus," Denise said.

"I know it's good." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "That's not the point, though, is it."

---

Tavon's kitchen was yellow and spotless. He made coffee in a machine that took little pods, and he apologized for it.

"My wife's idea. I miss the old kind."

"It's fine."

He sat across from her. "So. You didn't drive to Akron to tell me I look old."

She told him about the email. She showed him on her phone. He read it with his glasses pulled down, his lips moving slightly.

"Somebody found it in Detroit," he said.

"Apparently it's a whole thing. Collectors. Somebody told me a copy sold online for three hundred dollars last year."

Tavon laughed—a real laugh, surprised out of him. "Three hundred dollars. We couldn't give those things away. You remember? Marcus drove around in your mom's Buick for a month. Every record store from here to Youngstown. Most of 'em wouldn't even let him in the back."

"He got it on the radio once."

"Once." Tavon held up a finger. "Two in the morning, college station, some white boy with dreadlocks who said it 'had a vibe.' Marcus called me at two-oh-five screaming. I thought somebody had died."

They both went quiet then, because of the word.

"He'd be happy about this," Denise said finally.

Tavon took his glasses off and set them on the table. "Would he?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean—" He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn, then another. "Who's the artist?"

"They won't say yet."

"Somebody big?"

"I think so. The way the guy was talking."

"So somebody big is going to take that drum break from your granddaddy's gospel record, and that horn, and whatever else, and put it on a song that's gonna be in every car in America for three weeks. And then it'll be gone." He shrugged. "Maybe that's fine. Maybe that's what music is. But Marcus didn't make that record to be a—an ingredient."

"What did he make it for?"

Tavon looked at her for a long moment. That deciding look.

"You really don't know?"

---

What Denise remembered about the winter of 1995 was that it snowed constantly, the kind of lake-effect snow that came in bands, so that one street would be buried and the next would be clear. She remembered her mother not speaking to Marcus for most of November. She remembered the boxes of records in the basement, four hundred and something left, and Marcus not touching them anymore.

She remembered Christmas Eve.

There wasn't much under the tree that year. Some socks. A sweater from their aunt. Denise had expected that, and she'd made her peace with it.

But on Christmas morning, there was an envelope on the kitchen table with her mother's name on it, in Marcus's handwriting. Inside was three hundred and forty dollars. And a cassette tape.

Their mother opened the envelope at the table, still in her robe. She counted the money twice. Then she held up the tape.

"What is this."

"Just listen to it," Marcus said. "Please. One time, and I'll never ask you for nothing again."

"Where did this money come from?"

"Tavon and me sold a hundred records to a guy in Pittsburgh. A distributor. And I worked doubles at the Rally's. Please, Mama. Just one time."

Gloria looked at him for a long time. Then she got up, and went into the living room, and put the tape into the old stereo cabinet that had been their grandfather's. Denise and Marcus stood in the doorway.

The drums came in. The little late fill.

Their mother stiffened. Denise saw it—saw her shoulders go up, her hand go flat against the top of the cabinet.

"That's Daddy's record," she said. "That's from Daddy's choir record."

"Yes, ma'am."

"That's Mr. Ellison on the drums. He used to come to the house. He used to—" She stopped. The horn stab came in, *bah-dah*. Then Tavon's voice.

But Denise had never heard this version. This wasn't the one on the record. Tavon's verse ran its course, and then the beat dropped down to just the drums, and there was a different voice—Marcus's voice, which Denise had never once heard rap, not in the whole summer.

He wasn't good at it. His timing was off. He sounded nervous. But the words were clear.

*This one's for Gloria, who worked the second shift / who kept a light on in the window when the snow was coming swift / who never asked for nothing but the bills to all get paid / I took it from the coffee can, Mama, but I'm putting it back, I swear on everything I made.*

It went on for another eight bars. Denise didn't remember most of them later; she was watching her mother, who had both hands on the cabinet now, her head bowed, as if she was praying, or as if she'd been hit.

When the tape ended, there was hiss for a few seconds, and then Marcus's voice again, not rapping, just talking, very close to the microphone:

"Merry Christmas, Mama. That's all."

Their mother didn't turn around for a long time. When she did, her face was wet, and she walked across the room and put her arms around Marcus, who was nineteen and taller than her by a head, and she held him so hard he made a small sound.

"You fool," she said into his shoulder. "You stupid, stupid boy."

"I know."

"Don't you ever take from that can again."

"I won't."

"Play it again," she said.

---

"He made it for her," Tavon said. "The whole thing. The record. All of it. He told me that first night, when he brought the sampler home. He said, 'I'm gonna make something out of Granddaddy's records, and Mama's gonna hear it, and she's gonna remember she used to be happy.'"

Denise couldn't speak for a moment.

"I don't know if he ever told you that," Tavon said gently. "Your mom had a hard couple years, after your dad. Marcus thought—I don't know what he thought. He was nineteen. He thought a song could fix it."

"Did it?"

Tavon shrugged. "She played that tape every Sunday for, what, eight years? Till he passed?"

"Till she passed," Denise said. "She played it after, too. I'd hear it through the wall."

They sat in the yellow kitchen. Somewhere upstairs, Kayla was playing music, the bass coming faintly through the ceiling.

"So what do you want to do," Tavon said. "About the email."

"I don't know. What do you want to do?"

He picked up her phone again and looked at the email for a long time. "Is the vocal in it? The new song? Is it my verse, or just the beat?"

"Just the beat, I think. The drums and the horn."

"Mr. Ellison's drums."

"Yeah."

Tavon nodded slowly. "Mr. Ellison's got grandkids. They're in Cleveland Heights. One of them's a nurse at the Clinic."

"Tavon."

"I'm just saying. If somebody's gonna get paid. That's a good place for some of it to go."

Denise found herself smiling. "You've thought about this before."

"I've thought about that record every week for thirty years," he said. "I just never figured anybody else did."

---

The song came out in September. It was by someone Kayla had a poster of, and it was on the radio constantly for about three weeks, and then it wasn't. The drum break was there, sped up a little, polished, with the late swing mostly quantized out of it. The horn stab was there, *bah-dah*, pitched up, cleaner than it had ever been.

Denise heard it for the first time in a grocery store, in the cereal aisle. She stood very still with a box of oatmeal in her hand until it ended.

The checks came in October. She split them three ways: half for her, half for Tavon, and a third half, as Tavon insisted on calling it, for a woman named Sharon Ellison-Pratt, a nurse at the Cleveland Clinic, who called Denise crying and then laughing and then crying again.

That winter, the first big lake-effect storm came the week before Christmas. Denise stood at her window and watched it come in off the water in a thick white band, burying her street while two blocks over the sky stayed clear.

Then she went into the living room, and found the cassette—the one from the envelope, *MAMA* written on the label in Marcus's hand—and put it in the old stereo cabinet that had been her grandfather's, and then her mother's, and now hers.

The drums came in. The little late fill. Mr. Ellison smiling, somewhere, forty or fifty years ago.

She turned it up loud. Not low. Loud enough to rattle the plates in the cabinet.

*Snow comes sideways off the water, so you learn to walk crooked.*

Outside, it kept coming down. She let it.
