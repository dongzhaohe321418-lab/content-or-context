**What the Beetles Keep**

The chicken had been rotting in a mason jar on the back porch for six days, and my father said it was just about perfect.

"You can't rush it," he said, unscrewing the lid with the tenderness of a man opening a gift. The smell came out of the jar like something with arms. I stepped back off the porch and into the yard, where the grass was brown and stiff from August, and breathed through my mouth.

"Dad."

"Three more days and it would've been too far gone. They like it ripe, not soup." He screwed the lid back on and looked at me over the top of his reading glasses, which he wore all the time now, pushed down on his nose. "You want to come out tonight or not?"

I had driven eleven hours from Minneapolis to the Sandhills of Nebraska because my brother had called and said, *He's not eating right. He's out in the pasture at two in the morning with a headlamp. I think you should come.* My brother lived in Omaha and had three kids and a job that ate him alive, and I lived alone with a cat that my neighbor was feeding, and so it was understood without either of us saying it that I would be the one to go.

"Sure," I said. "I'll come."

---

My mother had been dead for fourteen months. She had been the one who talked. My father was the one who fixed things: fence lines, the pump on the well, the gutter on the north side of the house that pulled loose every winter. When I was a child I thought he was simply quiet the way a stone is quiet, by nature, without effort. It was only later, after I'd moved away and come back a few times as an adult, that I understood his quiet took work. He had decided at some point that the house only had room for one person to fill it with words, and he had let it be her.

Now the house was full of nothing. The clocks seemed louder. He had stopped winding the one in the hall, the one that had been her mother's, and it stood at 4:10, which was not the time she'd died or any time that meant anything. It had just run down.

"So what is it, exactly," I asked at dinner. He had made scrambled eggs and toast, which he said was dinner, and I didn't argue. "The beetle thing."

"American burying beetle." He said it the way you'd say the name of a person you admired. "*Nicrophorus americanus.* Used to be in thirty-five states. Now it's mostly here and a few spots in Oklahoma, Arkansas, some islands off Rhode Island. They put them on the endangered list in '89."

"And you count them."

"The university runs a survey. They need volunteers who know the land. Ron Kessler got me into it. Ron did it for nine years before his knee went." He buttered his toast, all the way to the edges, the way he always had. "I do four transects on our ground and two on the Petersons'. You set the traps at dusk, check them at dawn. Count them, mark them, let them go."

"Paul said you were out at two in the morning."

He was quiet for a moment. "Sometimes I don't sleep so well."

---

We went out at seven-thirty, when the sun had gone orange and fat and was lowering itself onto the hills like an old dog onto a rug. My father drove the pickup across the pasture with the windows down, and grasshoppers clicked off the hood. In the bed of the truck were six five-gallon buckets, a post-hole digger, a bag of flags, a clipboard in a gallon Ziploc, and the jar of chicken, wrapped in two garbage bags, which did not help.

The Sandhills are not what people picture when they picture Nebraska. They picture corn, flatness, the long dull stretch of I-80. But the Sandhills roll. They are dunes, really, ancient ones, grown over with bluestem and sand lovegrass and yucca, and in the evening light they look like the ocean frozen mid-swell. I had grown up out here and couldn't wait to leave, and every time I came back I was ambushed by how beautiful it was.

"Here," my father said, and stopped the truck by a little orange flag fluttering on a wire stake.

He showed me how to dig the hole, how to set the bucket in so its lip was flush with the ground. "If there's a lip, they'll walk around it. They have to just fall in." He put a few inches of damp soil in the bottom of the bucket, and a handful of dry grass. "So they've got somewhere to hide. You don't want them cooking if the sun gets on it before we come back." Then he took a strip of the chicken, using a pair of kitchen tongs that I recognized, with a small cold shock, as my mother's, and placed it in a little cup hung from a stick over the bucket. Last, a square of plywood propped on four nails a few inches above everything, like a roof.

"That's it?"

"That's it. They smell it from a couple miles off. Come in on the wind."

"A couple miles."

"They've got these antennae." He held up his hand and spread his fingers, a little clubbed fan. "Orange at the tips. Pick up a dead mouse from two miles away, maybe further. Nobody's sure."

We set the six traps as the dark came on. By the last one he had his headlamp on and so did I, and we moved inside our two small circles of light. The coyotes started up somewhere to the west. My father didn't talk much while he worked, but when he finished the last trap he stood for a while with his hands on his hips, looking out at nothing I could see.

"Your mother thought this was the most disgusting hobby a man had ever taken up," he said.

"She knew about it?"

"I started the summer before she got sick. She used to make me shower in the barn."

I laughed, and he smiled, and for a second it was almost easy.

---

I didn't sleep well either. The guest room had been my room, and then it had been my mother's sewing room, and now it was a room with a bed and a sewing machine under a dust cover and a bulletin board still pinned with her patterns, tissue-paper ghosts of dresses she'd never made. Around one in the morning I heard the screen door. I got up and looked out the window and saw his headlamp bobbing out toward the pasture, small and alone in all that dark.

I pulled on jeans and followed him.

He was sitting on an upturned bucket near the first trap, the headlamp switched to red so it wouldn't draw the bugs. He didn't seem surprised to see me. He moved over as if there were room on the bucket for two, which there wasn't, so I sat in the grass.

"You can't check them yet," I said. "You said dawn."

"I'm not checking them." He pointed with his chin. "Just listening."

At first there was nothing but the wind moving in the grass and the coyotes, farther off now. Then I heard it: a low droning, coming and going, like a small plane very far away.

"That's them?"

"That's them flying. They're big. Inch and a half, some of them. Sound like a bumblebee with a cold."

Something hit the plywood roof of the trap with a hard little *tock*. Then again. My father's face, in the red light, was very still.

"Tell me about them," I said. "Really. Not the textbook stuff."

He considered this.

"When a male and a female find a dead thing, a mouse, a songbird, whatever, about the size of a chipmunk, that's what they want. They'll fight off other beetles for it. Sometimes the female fights the other females and the male fights the males. Then once they've won it, they bury it."

"Like the name."

"Like the name. They get underneath it and dig out the dirt and it sinks, just sinks down into the ground, like it's going into water. They can bury a mouse in a night. Then they strip the fur or the feathers off, and they roll it up into a ball." He made a ball with his hands. "And they coat it in this stuff from their mouths and their back ends that keeps it from rotting. Keeps the mold off. They make it last."

I made a face, and he caught it and almost smiled.

"Then the female lays her eggs in a little tunnel off to the side. And when the larvae hatch, here's the thing." He leaned forward with his elbows on his knees. "Both of them stay. The mother and the father. They stay down there in the dark, and they chew up the meat and they feed it to the little ones mouth to mouth, like birds. The larvae beg. They reach up and touch the parents' mouths with their legs, and the parents feed them."

"Beetles do that?"

"Almost nothing in the insect world does that. Most bugs lay their eggs and they're gone. These ones stay." He was quiet for a moment. "Ron told me that, the first night. I didn't believe him. I went and read about it."

"How long do they stay?"

"Week or so. Until the young ones can feed themselves. Then the male goes, usually. Leaves first. The female stays a little longer."

The droning came close and went away again.

"I don't think I knew you liked this kind of thing," I said. "Bugs. Science."

"I didn't know either." He turned the headlamp off entirely, and we sat in the true dark, and my eyes slowly opened up to the stars, which out there are not a scattering but a smear, a pour. "Your mother liked to say I never wondered about anything. She said it like a joke. But I think she meant it some."

"Dad."

"She wasn't wrong. I didn't. I had the place to run. I had you kids. I figured wondering was something you did when you had time, and I didn't figure I'd ever have time." He shifted on the bucket. "And then I did."

---

At dawn the grass was soaked with dew and the sky was the color of the inside of a shell. We went from trap to trap and lifted the plywood lids.

The first bucket had nothing but a few carrion beetles of some other kind, small and flat and black, and a sleepy-looking ground beetle. My father tipped them out gently into the grass. The second trap was the same.

The third trap had three of them.

I would not have known, if he hadn't told me, that I was looking at something rare. But I would have known I was looking at something. They were big, as he'd said, as long as the last joint of my thumb, glossy black, and on each of their wing covers were two bands of bright orange-red, scalloped, almost like flames painted onto a hot rod. Behind the head was a shield of that same orange, a little saddle of color. The antennae ended in orange clubs, exactly like he'd shown me with his hand.

"Well, look at you," my father said, softly, to the beetles.

He put on a pair of thin gloves and lifted one out. It walked across his palm with a kind of stately, unbothered deliberation, and then it stopped, raised its abdomen, and made a small rasping sound I could hear from two feet away.

"She's mad at me," he said. "They rub their bellies against their wing covers. It's like a little squeak."

"How do you know it's a she?"

He turned his hand so I could see the face. "Look at the front of the head. Males have a big orange patch, kind of a rectangle. Females it's smaller. More like a triangle." He showed me. Then he took a fine-tipped paint pen from his shirt pocket, white, and with a hand much steadier than I'd seen it at the dinner table, he made a tiny dot on her wing cover. "That's so if we catch her again we'll know." He wrote on the clipboard: date, transect, sex, number. Then he set her down at the edge of the grass, and she stood for a moment, waving her orange antennae, and then she opened the wing covers, unfolded the clear wings from underneath, and flew, droning, low over the bluestem until I lost her in the light.

He handed me a pair of gloves. "You do the next one."

I didn't want to. I did it anyway. The beetle was heavier than I expected, and warm from the soil in the bucket, and its legs were strong and hooked and tickled. It walked to the edge of my hand and looked over, if beetles look, at the long drop to the ground.

"Male," I said, after a moment, checking the head. "Big patch."

"Good. Dot him."

My hand shook. The dot came out a little lopsided. My father looked at it and said, "That'll do."

---

We found nine in all that morning, across the six traps. My father said that was a good count, better than last year, though he warned me not to make too much of it. "You can't read anything into one night. Weather, the wind. You need years." He said it like a man who intended to have years, and I was glad to hear it.

Driving back to the house, the truck bounced over a rut and the empty buckets in the back clanged together. My father had both hands on the wheel. Without looking at me, he said, "I didn't bury her right."

I didn't understand at first. "What?"

"Your mother. I let the funeral home do everything. Your aunt picked the casket. Pastor Lindgren did the service. I just stood there." His jaw worked. "I didn't do anything. I didn't do one thing with my own hands."

"Dad. Nobody does it themselves. That's what the funeral home's for."

"I know it." He pulled up by the barn and turned the engine off, and we sat there with the engine ticking. "I know it. But I keep thinking about those beetles. How they get under the thing and they work all night, both of them, and they don't stop until it's down. And then they stay with it. They stay down there and they keep it good, and they make something live out of it." He took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes with his thumb and forefinger. "And I just stood there in a suit."

I didn't know what to say. My mother would have known. My mother would have said something that made him laugh and cry at the same time, and then she would have gone in and put the coffee on. I wasn't her. I had never been the one who talked either; I had only been the one who left.

So I did what he would have done. I didn't say anything. I reached over and put my hand on the back of his neck, the way you would with a horse, and left it there.

After a while he said, "You've got paint on your thumb."

I looked. A white smear, from the pen.

"So I do," I said.

---

I stayed nine days. On the fourth day I went into town and bought groceries that weren't eggs, and I cooked, badly, and he ate it. On the sixth day he wound the clock in the hall, without comment, and set it to the right time, and the house had a heartbeat again. On the eighth day we took down the bulletin board in the sewing room together and folded my mother's patterns into a shoebox, and he wrote *JEAN — DRESSES* on the lid in black marker, and put it on the closet shelf, not in the trash, not in the barn. Just on the shelf, where it could be found.

Every night we set the traps. Every morning we counted. On the last morning, in the fifth trap, we found a female with a lopsided white dot on her wing cover.

"That's not mine," I said. "I did a male."

My father bent close. "No. That's my dot. That's the first one, from your first night." He lifted her out. She rasped at him, indignant. "She came back."

"What does that mean?"

"Means she's alive. Means she's still around, still looking." He turned her gently in the light. "Season's getting late. She might have a mouse down there somewhere already. Might have young ones."

"Then what's she doing in a bucket?"

He laughed, a real laugh, the first one in nine days that came all the way up from the bottom of him. "Same thing as everybody. Smelled something and came to see." He held his hand out to me. "You want to let her go?"

I put on the gloves. He tipped her into my palm. She was quick this time, and didn't wait at the edge to consider the drop. She opened up the bright red-and-black shields of her back and lifted off, and the morning was so still I could hear her the whole way, that low determined hum, until she went down somewhere in the grass to whatever she was keeping, out of sight, under the ground, in the dark where things are tended.

My father stood beside me watching the spot where she'd gone.

"I'll come back in October," I said. "Before you pull the traps for the year."

He nodded. He didn't look at me. He wrote the number on the clipboard and put the pen back in his pocket.

"Bring a jacket," he said. "Gets cold out here at night."
