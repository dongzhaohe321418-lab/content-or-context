**The Front Left**

Marisol Okafor-Reyes had spent eleven years learning to do one thing in under two seconds, and on the morning of the first race of the season she still threw up behind the hospitality unit.

It was not nerves, exactly. She had explained this to people before, usually men at weddings who asked what she did and then asked whether she got scared. It was more that her body knew something her mind refused to acknowledge: that for the next nine months, roughly twice every Sunday, she would kneel on hot concrete while a car weighing eight hundred kilos came at her at the pit-lane limit and stopped, theoretically, a hand's width from her helmet. The body has opinions about that, and twice a season it expressed them.

She rinsed her mouth with a bottle of water and went back inside the garage, where Danny Holtz, the chief mechanic, was eating a banana with the solemnity of a priest.

"Season opener," he said. "You do the thing?"

"I did the thing."

"Good. Tradition." He threw the peel at a bin and missed. "Kid's here."

The kid was Teodor Vashchenko, nineteen years old, promoted from the junior series after the team's senior driver had retired into a television contract and a vineyard. Teo had a long face and a way of standing with his arms folded, as if holding himself together. He'd won the feeder championship by forty points. He had also, according to the data engineers, never once in his life lifted off the throttle in a corner where he didn't have to, and in several where he did.

He was standing at the front of the car, looking at it the way you'd look at a horse you'd been told was gentle.

Marisol was the front-left wheel gun. In a pit stop there are four wheel guns, four people pulling the old wheels, four putting on the new, two on the jacks, a couple steadying the car, and one person with the lollipop or, now, the lights. The front left was the corner nearest the pit wall, and for reasons nobody fully understood, it was the one that went wrong most. Wheel nuts cross-threaded. Guns jammed. On the front left you could ruin a race in the space of a breath.

"You're Marisol," Teo said. He didn't phrase it as a question.

"I am."

"They said you're the fastest."

"They say a lot of things."

He nodded seriously. "I will try to stop in the right place."

"That would be nice," she said, and to her surprise he smiled, a quick crooked thing that made him look about fourteen.

---

He did not stop in the right place.

Lap twenty-three, he came in hot, the front wing twitching, and overshot his box by the length of a hand. The front jack man, a big quiet Welshman named Gethin, had to lunge. Marisol shuffled forward on her knees, found the nut, fired. The gun shrieked. The new wheel went on, the nut went on, the gun shrieked again, her hand went up.

Three point one seconds. Slow, by their standards. Not a disaster.

Teo finished ninth, scored two points, and came back to the garage with his helmet under his arm, looking for someone. He found Marisol.

"I'm sorry," he said. "I was late on the brakes. The line in the pit is different from the simulator."

"Everything is different from the simulator."

"Yes." He thought about this. "Yes, that is true."

Danny, passing, clapped him on the shoulder. "Two points, kid. Your first two points. Go enjoy them."

But Teo didn't look like a man enjoying anything. He looked like someone doing arithmetic.

---

They went to the Middle East, then to Australia, then back across the planet to China, and the season took on the shape seasons always took: hotel corridors that smelled of carpet shampoo, the hum of the freight containers, the strange intimacy of eating noodles at midnight with thirty people who would never be your friends anywhere else on Earth but were, here, something more like family.

Marisol had a daughter, Nkem, who was seven and lived with her grandmother in Northampton during the season. They spoke every night on video. Nkem had opinions about Teo.

"He looks sad," she said, the night after Melbourne, where he'd finished twelfth after a spin.

"He's not sad. He's concentrating."

"He looks sad when he's concentrating."

"Lots of people do."

"You don't," Nkem said. "You look angry."

Marisol laughed, and after the call she sat for a while on the edge of the hotel bed and thought about whether that was true.

---

The practising was the part nobody saw.

On Thursdays and Fridays, sometimes Saturdays, the crew rehearsed stops, dozens a day. A mechanic pushed the car into the box on its own wheels while the rest of them knelt and pounced and stood. They filmed every one. The pit-stop coach, a former rugby player named Ines who had the calm of someone who'd been stamped on by better men, slowed the footage down frame by frame and showed them where they'd lost a hundredth.

"Marisol, your left knee," Ines would say. "You're planting it, then adjusting. Plant it once."

"I plant it once."

"You plant it twice. Look." Frame, frame, frame. There it was: the tiny correction, the knee sliding two centimetres. Fifteen thousandths of a second, Ines said. Nothing. Everything.

Teo started coming to these sessions. Nobody asked him to. He'd turn up in a team hoodie with the hood up and stand at the back of the garage, watching. After the third time, Danny asked him what he was doing.

"Learning," Teo said.

"Learning what? You're not going to be holding a gun."

"I want to know what happens. When I stop. I want to know what I'm asking."

Danny looked at him for a long moment and then, unusually, didn't make a joke.

---

In Miami, Teo qualified sixth, by far his best result, and Marisol saw him in the paddock afterwards being interviewed by a reporter who was asking him whether he felt he'd finally arrived.

"No," Teo said. "I feel like I did one lap well."

The reporter waited for more. Teo, apparently, had nothing more. He folded his arms.

Later Marisol found him sitting on an equipment case behind the garage, eating dry cereal out of a plastic cup.

"That reporter wanted a headline," she said.

"I know. I am bad at headlines." He offered her the cup. She took a handful. "My father," Teo said, "used to fix trucks. In Odesa, and then in Kraków. He said, when you fix one thing well, you have not fixed the truck. You have fixed one thing."

"Sounds like a wise man."

"He was a very annoying man," Teo said, and there was a weight in the past tense that Marisol didn't push on. "But in this way he was right."

The next day he finished fifth. The stop was two point four. He hit his marks exactly, braking so precisely that Gethin barely had to move. When Marisol raised her hand she caught, through the visor, just a glimpse of his eyes, and she could have sworn he was looking at her.

---

Then came the summer, and the summer was bad.

In Spain, the front-left nut cross-threaded. It wasn't Marisol's fault, everybody said so afterwards, it was a machining defect, a burr on the thread that nobody could have seen, but it was her gun and her hand and her corner, and the stop took nine point eight seconds while she fought it and the team's mechanics swarmed and the race walked away down the pit lane. Teo came out sixteenth. He finished fourteenth.

She didn't throw up that time. She just sat in the back of the garage afterwards with her fireproofs peeled down to the waist, very still, while Ines played the footage over and over on a laptop and said, "Look, look, it's the thread, you can see it bind."

"I should have felt it."

"Nobody feels that."

"I should have."

Teo came back. He didn't go to the motorhome, didn't go to the debrief. He came straight to the back of the garage, still in his suit, and sat down on the floor next to her.

"I was fourth," he said. "Before."

"I know you were."

"I am not saying it to make you feel bad."

"I know."

"I am saying it," he said carefully, "because I want you to know that when I was fourth, I was fourth because of the stops. All the stops before. So we are even. We are still even."

Marisol looked at him. Nineteen. Long face, folded arms, all that arithmetic.

"That's not how even works," she said.

"It is how I want it to work," he said, "so that is how it works."

---

The next three races, he crashed twice.

The first was a racing incident, another driver squeezing him toward a wall in Austria. The second, at Silverstone, was entirely his fault. He tried to take the lead at Copse on lap four, from seventh, which was not possible, which everybody knew was not possible, and put the car into the gravel and then into the barrier and walked away with his helmet in his hand and his shoulders around his ears.

Marisol watched it on the garage monitors. She heard Danny say, quietly, "Oh, son."

In the debrief afterwards, which Marisol wasn't in but heard about, the team principal, a cold neat Frenchwoman named Hélène Marchetti, apparently said nothing at all for about forty seconds, and then said: "Why?"

And Teo, apparently, said: "Because I wanted to give them something."

"Who?"

"The crew. They have been so fast. I wanted them to see us at the front."

And Hélène, who was not known for warmth, apparently put her pen down and said: "Teodor. They do not need you to give them anything. They need you to bring the car back so that they can do their jobs again next week."

Gethin told Marisol all this over warm beer in the motorhome that night. "Poor kid," he said. "He thinks we're keeping score."

"We are keeping score," said Marisol. "Ines keeps it to the thousandth."

"Not of him."

She thought about the cereal and the trucks and the annoying father in the past tense, and she found that she wanted to go and find Teo and say something, though she had no idea what.

---

She found him the following week in Hungary, Thursday, at the back of the garage during practice stops, hood up, arms folded.

"Come here," she said.

He came.

She handed him the wheel gun. He took it carefully, surprised by the weight. A little over three kilos, but it torqued like something alive when you pulled the trigger, and the first time anyone used one, it nearly took their wrist off.

"Kneel," she said.

He knelt, and she knelt beside him, and Ines, without being asked, called for the car to be pushed in. It rolled into the box. Gethin dropped the jack in. Marisol said, "Now," and Teo pulled the trigger, and the gun shrieked and bucked and the nut spun off and skittered across the concrete. The wheel man pulled the wheel, and the new one went on, and Teo, scrabbling, got the new nut on crooked and fired and it bound, and he stopped, panicking, and Marisol reached over and backed it off and fired it again and it ran down sweet and clean.

Eight seconds, maybe. Ines didn't call the time out loud.

Teo stayed kneeling, breathing hard, holding the gun.

"Now," Marisol said. "Picture doing that with a car coming at you, and twenty cameras on you, and a man in Brazil you've never met who's put money on it. And picture getting it wrong, in the actual race, in front of all those people."

"I don't want to picture that."

"No. Nobody does. Spain, I got it wrong." He started to say something, and she held up a hand. "I got it wrong, whatever the thread was doing, it was my corner. And you know what I did the next week?"

"What?"

"I came back. And I knelt down. That's the whole job. That's all I'm asking you. Not to give me the front. Just to come back."

He was quiet a long time. Around them, the crew had found reasons to be busy with other things.

"My father," he said finally, "would have liked you."

"The annoying one."

"Yes." He handed her back the gun. "I think he would have found you very annoying also. It would have been a good friendship."

---

The rest of the season was not a fairy tale. Teo did not win a race. The car wasn't fast enough to win a race; it had probably never been fast enough. But he stopped crashing. He started finishing. Seventh in Belgium. Sixth in Monza, in the rain, where he passed two world champions in the same lap and then, on the radio, said nothing but "Okay," which was apparently the most excited anybody had ever heard him.

And he hit his marks. Every stop. Every time. Gethin said you could set a watch by where the front wing stopped.

In Singapore, under the lights, the crew did a two point zero one, the fastest stop of the season for the whole grid, and when Marisol's hand went up she felt it, the strange clean click of everything going right, twenty people moving like one body. Teo came on the radio afterwards and said: "That was beautiful. Tell them that was beautiful."

The race engineer, a dry man named Pruitt, said: "They heard you, Teo."

They did. Gethin had his hands on his knees and was laughing like a hyena.

---

The final race of the season was in Abu Dhabi, under the lights again, in the warm damp air that smelled of jet fuel and the sea. Teo was fighting for ninth in the drivers' championship, which nobody outside the team cared about, and the team was fighting for sixth in the constructors', which meant several million in prize money and therefore, quite possibly, Marisol's job and Gethin's and the jobs of half the people in the garage.

That morning, she threw up behind the hospitality unit. Second time of the season. Tradition.

When she came back in, Teo was standing at the front of the car, looking at it the way you'd look at a horse that you'd finally learned was not gentle but was, at least, yours.

"You did the thing," he said. Danny had evidently told him.

"I did the thing."

"Good." He hesitated. "I don't know if I am staying next year. Hélène hasn't said."

"I know."

"If I am not, I wanted to tell you—"

"Tell me after," Marisol said. "Tell me after we've done our jobs."

He nodded.

On lap thirty-one he came in for his only stop. She heard the car before she saw it, the downshifts echoing off the pit buildings, and then it was there, a shape coming at her, and she was kneeling on the concrete with her knee planted once, exactly once, and the car stopped where it was supposed to stop, a hand's width from her helmet.

The gun shrieked. The nut came off. The wheel came off, the wheel went on. The gun shrieked again, ran down clean. Her hand went up.

Two point one.

He went back out in eighth, held it to the flag, and the team finished sixth in the constructors' by three points.

Afterwards, in the chaos of the garage, champagne someone had smuggled in and Danny weeping openly into Ines's shoulder, Teo found her. He was still in his suit, sweat-dark and wild-haired, and he didn't fold his arms.

"Hélène told me," he said. "I'm staying."

"Good," Marisol said, and found, to her embarrassment, that her eyes were stinging. "Good. That's good."

"Now," he said, "I tell you the thing I wanted to tell you."

"Go on."

He thought about it seriously, the way he thought about everything.

"Thank you for kneeling down," he said. "Every time."

Over his shoulder, on her phone propped against a toolbox, Nkem, up absurdly late on a school night in Northampton, was waving with both arms and shouting something nobody could hear over the noise.

"She says you look happy," Teo said, leaning in to read the screen's messages. "She says it's weird."

"Tell her," said Marisol, "that I'm concentrating."
