**The Shields**

The state took Route 61 back on a Tuesday in October, which is to say it gave it away. For sixty-three years the road had belonged to Nebraska, all forty-one miles of it, from the junction outside Harlan Springs to the grain elevator at Ostrander where it simply stopped, as if it had lost its nerve. Now the legislature had voted it down to a county road, and somebody had to go out and take the numbers off.

Dale Ruhl had volunteered. He was sixty-four and eleven weeks from retirement. His supervisor, a kind man named Pruitt who was young enough to be his son, had tried to talk him out of it.

"It's a two-person job," Pruitt said. "You'll need somebody on the auger and somebody on the truck."

"Then give me somebody."

So they gave him Teresa Velasco, twenty-three, eight months on the district crew, who had never heard of Route 61 before the memo came down. She showed up at the yard at six-fifteen with a thermos and a look of patient suspicion, as if she had been warned about him.

"You're the guy who cried at the Christmas party," she said.

"That was allergies."

"In December."

"The tree was real." He tossed her the keys. "You drive. I want to look."

---

The truck was a three-quarter-ton flatbed with a post puller bolted behind the cab and a crate of county route markers strapped down in the back, blue pentagons with white numerals: 214. That was what the road would be now. County Road 214. Dale had looked at the new signs in the warehouse the week before and thought they looked like something you'd find on a utility box.

They headed out on 30 and turned north at the junction, and there it was, the first one, a black-and-white Nebraska shield on a green-painted steel post, the outline of the state like a cooking pan with a handle, and inside it, the number 61.

Teresa pulled onto the shoulder. Dale got out and stood in front of the sign for a moment with his hands in his jacket pockets.

"Do you want me to give you a minute," she said. She did not say it unkindly.

"No. Get the wrench."

The bolts had rusted into the bracket. She worked at them with the breaker bar while he held the shield so it wouldn't drop. When it came free he took it from her and looked at the back of it, where someone had written in silver marker, a long time ago, *J.K. 4-88.*

"Who's J.K.," she asked.

"Jerry Kolb. He ran this section before me. He signed everything he hung. Got written up for it twice."

"Why'd he do it?"

Dale slid the shield into the empty rack behind the cab. "He said a road ought to know who looked after it."

They put up the blue county marker on the same post. It took four minutes. Teresa stepped back and squinted at it.

"It's fine," she said.

"It's fine," he agreed, and they got back in the truck.

---

There were thirty-eight shields on Route 61, counting both directions and the reassurance markers past each crossroad. Dale knew because he'd counted them in 1991, his first summer, when Jerry Kolb had made him walk the whole length with a clipboard and inventory every sign, every culvert, every delineator post.

"He made you walk it," Teresa said, around mile six. "Like, on foot."

"Forty-one miles. Took me three days. He'd drop me off in the morning and pick me up at dark."

"That's insane. That's a labor violation."

"Probably." Dale watched the fields go by, the corn mostly down now, stubble rows running off toward a tree line that marked the creek. "But I knew the road after that. I knew where it held water and where the frost heaved it. I knew which farmers mowed their own ditch and which ones called us to complain we hadn't. You don't learn that from a truck."

"Is that what you're going to make me do?"

"You won't need to. It's not ours anymore."

She was quiet for a while. Then she said, "My dad drives for a feedlot. He says roads are roads."

"Your dad's not wrong."

"But you think he is."

Dale considered it. "I think he's never had one of his own."

---

At mile nine there was a church, white clapboard, Lutheran, with a gravel lot and a cemetery fenced in chain link. The shield there was bent, folded nearly in half along the bottom edge.

"Snowplow?" Teresa guessed.

"Kid in a Buick. Nineteen ninety-seven. Christmas Eve." Dale got out and put a hand on the post, which leaned just perceptibly toward the ditch. "He walked away. Came to the service an hour later with a cut on his forehead and sat in the back. The pastor made a joke about it in the sermon, something about the Lord's highway. Everybody laughed. I was there because my wife's family went to this church."

"You never straightened it."

"Kept meaning to."

She looked at him. "Do you want to keep this one?"

"They go back to the district. They get recycled."

"That's not what I asked."

He didn't answer. She went and got the wrench.

---

The middle stretch of 61 ran dead flat for eleven miles, an arrow pointed at nothing, and the wind came across it out of the northwest hard enough to rock the truck. Teresa drove with both hands. Dale had poured coffee from her thermos without asking and she let him.

"Can I ask you something," she said.

"You will anyway."

"Why'd they downgrade it? The memo just said 'functional classification review.'"

"Traffic counts." He held the cup with both hands against the jostle. "Used to be the Ostrander elevator took in grain from half the county. Trucks all day long in the fall, lined up a quarter mile. Then the co-op built the new terminal down on the railroad by Kearney, and nobody hauls up here anymore. There was a school at Ostrander. Consolidated in 2004. Post office closed in '11. Road doesn't go anywhere people need to go, the state doesn't want to pay for it."

"That's sort of reasonable."

"It's entirely reasonable." He drank. "It's still a hell of a thing."

"The county will maintain it, though, right? It's not like they're tearing it up."

"County's got two graders and a guy named Mel. They'll patch it when it breaks. They won't seal the cracks before winter. They won't come out at three in the morning when the drifts close it at the Hensley curve." He looked at her. "That's not a knock on Mel. Mel's doing four jobs."

"Did you come out at three in the morning?"

"Thirty-some winters."

"For a road nobody uses."

"Somebody always uses it," Dale said. "That's the thing nobody counts. There's a woman at the Hensley place, Arlene, she's ninety-one. Her daughter drives up from Grand Island twice a week. There's the Amish families past the creek who come down to the feed store. There's kids who learn to drive on it because there's nothing to hit. There was a fella with a heart attack in 2009 and the ambulance came up 61 because it was the only thing open. Traffic counter on the shoulder doesn't know who's in the car."

Teresa didn't say anything. Up ahead the road began, very gently, to curve.

"That's the Hensley curve," he said.

"I figured."

---

Arlene Hensley was in her yard when they stopped, a small woman in a barn coat and a knit cap, dragging a hose toward a stock tank that had no stock near it. She raised one hand and came down to the fence line as they unbolted the shield at the end of her drive.

"Dale Ruhl," she said. "They finally let you go."

"Not yet, Arlene. December."

"What are you doing to my sign?"

"State's giving the road to the county." He held up the blue marker. "It's 214 now."

She peered at it. "That's a phone extension."

Teresa laughed, then tried to swallow it, and Arlene turned her sharp gaze on her. "And who's this?"

"Teresa Velasco, ma'am. I'm with the district."

"Is he training you?"

"Sort of."

"He trained the last one too, and that one went to Omaha to sell insurance." Arlene leaned on the fence post. "Who's going to plow me out now?"

"Mel," Dale said.

"Mel." She said it like a diagnosis. "Well. Mel's a good boy." She looked at the old shield in Dale's hands. "My husband used to say you could tell the state road from the county road with your eyes shut. By the sound the tires made."

"He was right."

"I'll have to listen for it, then. Listen for it going." She seemed to shake something off. "You two want pie? It's yesterday's."

They stayed twenty minutes. The pie was rhubarb, from a freezer bag of stalks Arlene had put up in June. She asked Teresa where she was from, and Teresa said Lexington, and Arlene said she'd known a Velasco family in Lexington who had a tire shop, and Teresa said that was her uncle, and Arlene said, "Well, then," as if that settled something important, and maybe it did.

When they left, Arlene stood at the end of the drive and watched them put up the blue marker. She didn't wave. She just watched until they pulled away.

---

"Can I tell you something kind of embarrassing," Teresa said, three miles on.

"Sure."

"I almost didn't take this job. I had an offer from a logistics company in Lincoln. Desk job. Better money." She adjusted her grip on the wheel. "I took this one because I thought it'd be outside and I wouldn't have to talk to anybody."

"How's that going."

"You've talked more today than in the entire eight months I've been on crew."

"I'm retiring. I've got to use it all up."

She smiled, just slightly, at the windshield. "I don't think I get it. Not really. Why it matters so much to you. It's a road. It's asphalt. You'll go home in December and it'll still be here and somebody else will look after it."

"That's right."

"So why does it feel like a funeral?"

Dale was quiet long enough that she glanced over.

"My wife died in 2015," he said. "Joanne. You didn't know that."

"No. I'm sorry."

"Long time ago now. Cancer. The last year she was going to Kearney for treatments, three times a week, and I'd drive her, and we'd take 61 down to the junction because she liked it. Faster to go the other way, by eight minutes. She didn't care. She'd look out at the fields and tell me who farmed what, whose kids married whose. She grew up out here. She knew every place."

The wind pushed the truck and Teresa corrected without thinking about it.

"After she was gone I'd still drive it. On my own time. Weekends. Check the culverts, look at the shoulders. Pruitt thinks I was gunning for overtime." He laughed, short. "I wasn't putting in for it. I just liked knowing it was all right. That I'd looked after it. That's all I could do by then, I guess. Look after something."

"Dale."

"I know it's a road," he said. "I'm not confused. I know it's asphalt. But when they told me they were taking the number off, it felt like they were telling me nobody'd remember she rode up and down it. That's dumb. The number doesn't remember anything. It's just a number."

"It's not dumb."

"It's a little dumb."

"Okay," she said. "It's a little dumb." And then, after a mile: "I'd have driven her the slow way too."

---

The last shield was at Ostrander, where the road ran out against the concrete base of the old elevator. The elevator was still standing, a hundred and twenty feet of gray cylinders with the co-op's name half flaked off the headhouse, and a pair of pigeons circled it as they pulled up. There was a turnaround of cracked asphalt and a small green sign that said END 61, with an arrow pointing back the way they'd come, and beneath it the last shield.

The sun was low now. They'd taken longer than they should have. Pruitt had called twice and Teresa had told him they'd hit some rusted bolts, which was true.

Dale got out and looked up at the elevator for a long time.

"Joanne's dad hauled here," he said. "Every fall. I met her right over there." He pointed at a slab of concrete with weeds growing up through the expansion joints. "There was a scale house. She was working the window. Summer of 1990. I was a kid with a clipboard walking forty-one miles for Jerry Kolb, and I came up here dead on my feet the third day, and she gave me a Coke from the machine and didn't charge me."

"Is that why you remember every sign?"

"Maybe." He shrugged. "Maybe I'd have remembered them anyway."

Teresa went around to the rack behind the cab and lifted something out. It was the bent shield from the church, the one the kid in the Buick had folded. She held it out to him.

"I didn't put it back with the others," she said.

"They'll count them at the yard."

"I'll tell them it was too damaged to recycle. Which it is. Look at it." She pushed it into his hands. "Put it in your garage. Put it over your workbench. Whatever. Somebody ought to know who looked after it."

He held it and didn't say anything for a while.

"Jerry Kolb would like you," he said at last.

"Is he still alive?"

"No."

"Then I'll take your word for it."

---

She took the END 61 sign down herself, standing on the flatbed's tailgate with the breaker bar while he steadied the post. She put up the blue marker and a new END sign Pruitt had sent, END CO RD 214, and she stepped back and looked at it in the last orange light.

"It's fine," she said.

"It's fine."

"Should we say something?"

"Like what?"

"I don't know. You're the one who cries at parties."

He looked at the road running south, the long straight line of it going gold and then gray as the sun dropped, the fields on either side gone to stubble, a single pickup's headlights far off where it crossed the creek.

"Somebody always uses it," he said. That was all.

They drove back with the windows cracked, though it was cold. Teresa drove slowly. Somewhere past the Hensley curve she said, "Hey. Listen."

"To what?"

"The tires. Arlene said you could tell. State road or county road."

They both listened. The truck hummed on the old asphalt, the seams ticking under them at regular intervals, a sound like a clock running on a shelf in another room.

"Sounds the same," Teresa said.

"Give it a winter," said Dale. "Give it a winter, and then come out and listen."

"I will," she said, and he believed her, and the road carried them home in the dark.
