Eleanor Harbury

Eleanor Harbury (17 February 1826 – 9 October 1894) was an English novelist and essayist. Her fiction concerned provincial households, the administration of inherited property, and the position of women whose education exceeded their opportunities for employment. She published nine novels between 1855 and 1888, together with short stories and essays on reading, household management, and local history. Her best-known works were ''The Alder House'' (1859), ''A Measure of Rain'' (1867), and ''The Bell at Low Mere'' (1878).

Harbury initially published as E. M. Harbury. Contemporary reviewers frequently treated her early books as the work of a man, although her identity was known within the publishing trade. Her novels attracted a steady circulating-library readership but rarely achieved large sales in single-volume editions. After a decline in readership during the early twentieth century, several were reissued for their accounts of women’s work and provincial institutions.

= = Early life and education = =

Harbury was born Eleanor Mary Venn in the fictional market town of West Barcombe, Gloucestershire, the second of four children of solicitor Richard Venn and his wife, Anne, née Bellamy. Her father’s practice included the management of small estates and the preparation of tenancy agreements. Documents associated with his work later supplied subjects for her fiction, although no surviving manuscript directly reproduces an identifiable client’s affairs.

She received most of her education at home. Her mother taught French and drawing, while a neighbouring clergyman supervised Latin lessons shared with her elder brother. Between 1839 and 1841 she attended a small boarding school near Bath. Her surviving exercise books contain translations, botanical descriptions, and short accounts of journeys undertaken by members of her family.

Richard Venn died in 1843. The family remained in West Barcombe but moved to a smaller house, taking two pupils as boarders. Harbury assisted with their instruction and subsequently worked for eighteen months as a companion to an elderly widow in Somerset. Her correspondence from this period records reading, household expenditure, and disputes over the duties expected of a paid companion.

= = Marriage and literary beginnings = =

In 1848 she married Thomas Harbury, a surveyor employed on drainage and road projects. The couple lived first in Bristol and later in the cathedral town of Dunstead. They had two children: Margaret, born in 1850, and Philip, born in 1853.

Harbury began contributing unsigned reviews and descriptive sketches to the ''Western Household Register'' in 1851. Her earliest identified contribution, “A Parish Chest”, described the contents and condition of a collection of local records. Other pieces concerned village schools, domestic servants, and the preservation of footpaths.

Her first novel, ''The Narrow Garden'', appeared in three volumes in 1855. It followed the daughters of a retired schoolmaster as they attempted to maintain their household through teaching. Reviews were generally favourable, although several criticised the limited action and extended discussions of expenditure. The publisher, Bell and Kersey, paid Harbury a fixed sum rather than a royalty.

Thomas Harbury’s employment required frequent absences. Eleanor managed the household and corrected proofs while continuing occasional teaching. Her letters describe writing in the early morning and revising chapters aloud after the children had gone to bed.

= = Career = =

''The Alder House'' established Harbury’s reputation in 1859. Its treatment of an inheritance dispute attracted comment for making the practical management of property central to the plot. A second edition appeared within a year, and the novel remained available through circulating libraries into the 1880s.

Harbury followed it with ''Mrs Fenwick’s Visitors'' (1862), a comedy concerning a boarding house, and ''The Second Ledger'' (1864), which examined a partnership between two provincial merchants. ''A Measure of Rain'' appeared in 1867 and was her most commercially successful novel. Its publication coincided with public discussion of drainage, water supply, and the responsibilities of local authorities.

Thomas Harbury died in 1870 after a prolonged illness. Harbury subsequently moved to a smaller house outside Dunstead, where she lived with her daughter. Her fiction became less frequent, partly because she undertook more remunerative reviewing and editorial work. From 1872 to 1876 she supervised a series of abridged historical books intended for school libraries.

''The Bell at Low Mere'' was published in 1878. Its restrained account of a disputed charitable foundation divided reviewers, some of whom considered its conclusion insufficiently decisive. Her final novels were ''The Unlet Rooms'' (1882) and ''A Winter Partnership'' (1888). An unfinished work, provisionally entitled ''The East Window'', survives in manuscript.

= = Themes and style = =

Harbury’s novels generally employ third-person narration, moving between several members of a household or institution. Major events are often presented through their practical consequences: a death changes the allocation of rooms, a marriage alters an account book, and a financial loss requires the dismissal of a servant.

Her central characters are frequently women who possess useful knowledge but lack recognised authority. They teach, maintain records, supervise buildings, and interpret correspondence. Their competence does not necessarily lead to financial independence or marriage. Several novels conclude with arrangements that remain dependent on personal goodwill.

Letters, receipts, plans, and minutes recur throughout her fiction. Such documents may clarify an event, but they seldom provide a complete explanation of motive. Harbury distinguishes between what a record establishes and what characters wish it to establish.

Her dialogue is noted for its indirectness. Disagreements commonly concern apparently minor matters while revealing larger divisions over class, obligation, or ownership. Humour arises particularly from the language of committees and from discrepancies between charitable intentions and household practice.

= = Later life and reception = =

Harbury remained active in Dunstead’s subscription library and supported the establishment of an evening reading room for women employed in shops. She declined invitations to speak publicly, although she supplied books and assisted with cataloguing.

She died at her home in 1894 and was buried beside her husband. Margaret Harbury edited a selection of her letters in 1897, omitting most references to family disagreements and payments from publishers.

Early twentieth-century accounts generally described Harbury as a minor domestic novelist. Renewed attention began with a 1936 edition of ''The Alder House''. Later criticism examined her representation of unpaid labour, local government, and the relationship between documentary evidence and narrative authority. Much of her shorter journalism remains unattributed.

The Alder House

''The Alder House'' is an 1859 novel by Eleanor Harbury. Published in three volumes by Bell and Kersey, it concerns the inheritance of a substantial provincial house and the obligations attached to its occupation. The novel was Harbury’s second published work of fiction and the first to receive extensive attention in the London reviewing press.

The story centres on Catherine Wray, an unmarried woman who has managed her uncle’s household for twelve years. On his death, ownership passes to a distant male cousin, while Catherine receives a modest annuity and a right of residence whose practical meaning becomes disputed.

= = Plot = =

Following the death of Samuel Wray, his nephew Edward arrives at Alder House intending to sell it. Edward works in a London insurance office and regards the property as an inconvenient asset. Catherine considers it a household sustained by longstanding responsibilities to servants, tenants, and poorer relatives.

The will permits Catherine to occupy “her accustomed apartments” for life. Neither the rooms nor the associated rights are specified. Edward initially assumes that she will accept compensation and leave, but she refuses to negotiate until provision has been made for the elderly housekeeper, Mrs Berris, and a dependent cousin.

Preparations for a sale reveal that several outbuildings are occupied under informal arrangements. Catherine has allowed a carpenter to use a shed and has converted an unused laundry into a classroom. Edward’s solicitor advises that these activities may reduce the property’s value.

Edward’s younger sister Lucy comes to stay and begins helping Catherine with the school. She also discovers that the house requires extensive repairs. Catherine has concealed the scale of the deterioration because she fears that an inspection will make continued occupation impossible.

A winter storm damages the roof and forces the household into a smaller group of rooms. During the repairs, Edward and Catherine gradually acknowledge their mutual dependence. He cannot afford to restore the house without income from its grounds, while she cannot preserve the household by continuing her uncle’s informal arrangements.

The novel ends with part of the garden leased to a nursery business and the western wing adapted as a small school. Edward retains ownership, but Catherine receives a defined tenancy. Lucy remains as a paid teacher. The settlement preserves the house while ending several privileges associated with its former use.

= = Composition and publication = =

Harbury began the novel in 1857. An early outline gives Edward a fraudulent business partner and concludes with a contested court hearing. These elements were removed during revision. The published version confines most of the legal dispute to correspondence and private meetings.

Bell and Kersey requested a more explicit romantic plot. Harbury added several scenes involving Lucy and the nursery proprietor, Henry Sale, but left their eventual relationship unresolved. A surviving letter explains that Lucy’s decision to accept paid employment was intended to carry greater weight than the possibility of marriage.

The first edition appeared in October 1859. A cheaper edition followed in 1861, with minor corrections and a shortened description of Samuel Wray’s funeral.

= = Themes = =

The novel distinguishes legal ownership from the knowledge required to maintain a property. Edward possesses the house but initially understands little of its operation. Catherine understands its routines but has no authority to make permanent decisions.

Neither position is presented as sufficient. Catherine’s generosity depends on arrangements that are poorly recorded and sometimes unfair to those outside her immediate circle. Edward’s proposed sale overlooks the labour through which the property has retained its usefulness.

The deteriorating building also limits the characters’ choices. Its repair requires money, disclosure, and changes of use. Preservation is therefore presented as an alteration of existing practices rather than the continuation of an intact household.

= = Reception = =

Contemporary reviews praised Mrs Berris and the conversations between Catherine and Edward. Objections concentrated on the absence of a conventional marriage ending and the attention given to repairs, rents, and access rights.

Later critics have treated the final tenancy agreement as the novel’s principal resolution. Catherine acquires enforceable security, although she relinquishes the broader authority she exercised under her uncle.

A Measure of Rain

''A Measure of Rain'' is an 1867 novel by Eleanor Harbury, set in the fictional manufacturing town of Brackenford. It follows a dispute over drainage works through the experiences of a surveyor’s widow, a mill owner, and a newly appointed medical officer. The novel combines a domestic narrative with an account of municipal decision-making.

It was first serialised in the ''Monthly Hearth'' before publication in three volumes. Its sales exceeded those of Harbury’s other novels during her lifetime.

= = Background = =

Harbury developed the novel from notes made during journeys with her husband, whose surveying work included drainage schemes. Her notebooks contain descriptions of sluices, open channels, and meetings between landowners, but Brackenford does not correspond consistently to a single town.

The title refers to a rain gauge maintained by the central character, Lydia Dane. It also reflects the disagreement between residents who understand flooding through recorded measurements and those who rely on remembered experience.

= = Plot = =

Lydia Dane supports herself and her son by copying plans and preparing accounts after the death of her husband, a surveyor. She continues his practice of recording rainfall in their garden. When low-lying streets flood twice in one autumn, the local board commissions a drainage proposal from engineer Arthur Pell.

Pell employs Lydia to copy documents but excludes her from discussions concerning their interpretation. She notices that his calculations use a shortened series of rainfall records and omit an unusually wet season. Pell argues that the missing figures are unreliable.

Meanwhile, mill owner Josiah Creed opposes a proposed channel across his land. He claims that the works will threaten employment by interrupting the mill’s water supply. His daughter Ellen, who visits families affected by the flooding, begins to question his account.

Medical officer Stephen Rooke reports repeated illness in the lower streets. His evidence is challenged because he has held the post for only a few months. The board delays its decision while requesting further reports.

During a prolonged storm, a privately maintained embankment fails. Lydia’s house is flooded, and much of her husband’s archive is damaged. Her son is rescued by a mill worker whom Creed recently dismissed.

The disaster does not settle the technical dispute. Instead, surviving records reveal that several parties have accepted maintenance arrangements they knew to be inadequate. Pell revises his scheme, Creed grants access on negotiated terms, and the board approves a smaller initial programme.

Lydia is appointed to a paid clerical position overseeing the works accounts. The closing chapter records another wet season, during which some streets remain flooded despite measurable improvements.

= = Narrative structure = =

The novel is divided into four parts corresponding to successive periods of rainfall. Short extracts from Lydia’s observations appear at the beginning of selected chapters. These entries provide continuity while differing markedly from the uncertain recollections expressed in dialogue.

Harbury distributes information unevenly among the characters. Readers learn about defects in the drainage system before the board formally recognises them, but the causes of individual illnesses remain uncertain. The narrative avoids assigning every consequence to a single error or antagonist.

= = Themes and reception = =

A central subject is the conversion of knowledge into public authority. Lydia’s measurements are valued when treated as her husband’s work but questioned when she presents them independently. Rooke faces a related difficulty because professional qualifications do not immediately confer local credibility.

The novel also examines the limits of benevolence. Ellen’s visits provide immediate assistance but cannot remedy the conditions affecting the flooded streets. Her later involvement in committee work is presented as less personally gratifying but more consequential.

Reviewers praised the storm chapters, while some objected to the technical discussions. Later readings have emphasised the incomplete ending, in which improvements depend on continuing expenditure and maintenance.

The Bell at Low Mere

''The Bell at Low Mere'' is an 1878 novel by Eleanor Harbury. Set around an endowed almshouse in eastern England, it concerns the investigation of a charitable foundation whose surviving records conflict with local custom. Its principal character, Marian Voss, is employed to arrange the papers of a recently deceased trustee.

The novel is distinguished by its limited geographical setting and its refusal to resolve every historical uncertainty introduced by the plot.

= = Plot = =

Marian arrives at Low Mere expecting six weeks of clerical work. The foundation supports eight elderly residents and maintains a bell rung each evening. Local tradition holds that the bell commemorates fishermen drowned while returning from a winter market.

Among the trustee’s papers, Marian finds an incomplete account suggesting that the original endowment supported a ferry and shelter for travellers. Later payments appear to have been redirected towards residential accommodation without a surviving formal agreement.

The discovery divides the trustees. Rector Francis Yeo argues that established usage fulfils the founder’s charitable purpose. Landowner Robert Cleeve fears that an inquiry will expose the foundation to outside control. Schoolmistress Ada Nerin proposes using part of its income to support children who cross the mere to attend school.

Marian’s investigation is complicated by the residents, who fear losing their homes. One of them, former ferryman Isaac Dole, possesses a copy of an older agreement but refuses to surrender it until his daughter’s unpaid work at the almshouse is acknowledged.

The agreement establishes an obligation to assist crossings but does not specify how it must be fulfilled. A public meeting produces no consensus. The trustees eventually retain the almshouse and fund a supervised boat service from income previously spent on an annual commemorative dinner.

Marian completes a catalogue that records gaps and contradictions rather than supplying a continuous institutional history. She leaves Low Mere after declining an invitation to become the foundation’s permanent secretary. The evening bell continues to sound, although its origin remains uncertain.

= = Composition = =

Harbury drafted the novel between 1875 and 1877. Her correspondence indicates that she initially planned a discovery of deliberate fraud. During revision, the disputed expenditure became the cumulative result of decisions made by successive trustees.

The manuscript also contains a deleted chapter explaining the bell’s origin. Its removal preserved the distinction between a useful local tradition and an independently established historical account.

= = Interpretation and reception = =

The novel considers how institutions justify present practices through accounts of their beginnings. Each proposed history supports a different use of the foundation’s income, making historical interpretation part of the dispute over resources.

Marian’s catalogue provides the clearest statement of her professional position. She accepts responsibility for describing the evidence but declines to reconcile incompatible claims merely to produce a satisfactory narrative.

Contemporary reception was mixed. Admirers praised the residents’ dialogue and the treatment of communal memory. Other reviewers regarded the unresolved history as a defect. Subsequent criticism has treated that uncertainty as central to the novel’s account of charitable responsibility: the trustees must make decisions despite the surviving record’s limitations.
