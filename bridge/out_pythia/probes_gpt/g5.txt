The Caldern Valley Railway is a passenger and freight railway in the county of Merrow, in the northern part of the Republic of Aveland. It runs for 61.8 kilometres between Harbury Central and Bellscar, following the River Caldern for most of its length. The line connects Harbury and the lower valley industrial district with the market town of Merewick and the upland settlements around Bellscar.

The railway opened in stages between 1874 and 1882. It was constructed principally to carry limestone, timber and manufactured goods, although passenger traffic became important during the early twentieth century. The route remains unelectrified. Most passenger services operate between its two termini, with additional trains serving the southern section during weekday peak periods.

Five stations are currently open: Harbury Central, Lanton Mill, Merewick, High Fold and Bellscar. The line is administered by the Aveland Railway Authority, while passenger services are operated by Northern Counties Rail.

= = History = =

Proposals for a railway through the Caldern valley were first published in 1859. Local quarry owners supported a connection to the national railway network at Harbury, but disagreements over the route delayed the formation of a railway company. An eastern alignment would have served several large estates, while a western alternative provided easier access to the limestone workings above Merewick.

The Caldern Valley Railway Company was incorporated in 1868. Its authorised route followed the western bank of the river as far as Merewick before crossing to the eastern side. Construction began in April 1870 under the engineer Edwin Carset. Flooding and unstable ground increased costs on the lower section, requiring the replacement of several planned embankments with short viaducts.

The first section, between Harbury and Merewick, opened to goods traffic on 16 September 1874. Passenger services began six weeks later. The extension to High Fold opened in 1878, followed by the final section to Bellscar on 3 July 1882. The completed railway cost approximately 40 per cent more than the original estimate.

Traffic initially consisted mainly of limestone, sawn timber and coal brought into the valley. Passenger receipts increased after 1895, when Bellscar developed as a summer resort. Through carriages from the capital, Ordel, were introduced on summer Saturdays in 1907. These were attached to local trains at Harbury.

The company was absorbed into the Northern Provincial Railway in 1913. Under the new owner, passing loops were lengthened, the Harbury approach was doubled and a locomotive servicing facility was built at Bellscar. The railway entered public ownership in 1949.

Road competition reduced both passenger and freight traffic after the Second World War. Four minor stations closed in 1958, and ordinary goods facilities were withdrawn from most remaining stations during the following decade. A proposal to close the line north of Merewick was published in 1967. It was rejected after a transport inquiry found that winter road conditions would make a replacement bus service unreliable.

Passenger services were reorganised around diesel railcars in 1971. Infrastructure work undertaken between 1998 and 2004 replaced several bridges, renewed the track through Caldern Gorge and introduced centralised signalling. A further programme completed in 2019 provided step-free access at the three busiest stations.

= = Route = =

From Harbury Central, the railway runs north through the former engineering district before crossing the Harbury Navigation on a steel girder bridge. The first 14.2 kilometres, as far as Lanton Mill, are double track. Beyond Lanton Mill, the route is single track, with passing loops at Merewick and High Fold.

Between Lanton Mill and Merewick, the railway crosses low-lying agricultural land. Much of this section stands on an embankment constructed above the recorded flood level of 1863. Culverts allow water to pass beneath the formation during seasonal flooding.

North of Merewick, the valley narrows. The railway crosses the Caldern on the seven-arch Stonehaugh Viaduct and enters a sequence of cuttings along the eastern hillside. The 684-metre Bracken Tunnel carries the line beneath a projecting ridge south of High Fold.

The final section climbs at gradients reaching 1 in 68. Beyond High Fold, the railway leaves the main river valley and follows Bell Beck to Bellscar. The terminus stands 286 metres above sea level, compared with 38 metres at Harbury Central.

= = Services = =

The usual weekday service consists of one passenger train each hour in each direction, with a journey time of approximately 74 minutes. Additional morning and evening trains operate between Harbury Central and Merewick. Sunday services run every two hours, with extra trains during the summer.

Most services use two-car diesel multiple units. Three-car formations are provided for selected school services and summer weekend trains. All regular passenger trains call at every open station.

Freight operations principally serve the limestone terminal south of Merewick. Trains run to cement works in the eastern provinces, normally on four weekdays. Timber loading at Bellscar ceased in 1996, although occasional engineering trains continue to use the former goods yard.

= = Infrastructure = =

The line is built to the national standard gauge of 1,435 millimetres. Its maximum permitted speed is 100 kilometres per hour on the lower section, falling to 65 kilometres per hour through the gorge and 40 kilometres per hour on the approach to Bellscar.

Signalling is controlled from Harbury. The passing loops at Merewick and High Fold allow passenger trains to cross, although the regular timetable normally schedules crossings at Merewick. Most former level crossings have been replaced by bridges or closed to road traffic.

The principal maintenance depot is at Harbury East, reached by a connecting line outside the central station. Bellscar retains a siding for overnight stabling and a water supply used by visiting steam locomotives.

Harbury Central railway station

Harbury Central is the southern terminus of the Caldern Valley Railway and the principal railway station serving Harbury, the largest town in Merrow. It is also served by trains on the East Merrow Main Line. The station stands on Station Square, immediately east of the town centre.

The present station opened in 1909, replacing separate facilities operated by the valley railway and the main-line company. It has five platforms and is the busiest station on the Caldern Valley route.

= = History = =

The first main-line station at Harbury opened in 1856. The Caldern Valley Railway constructed its own terminus nearby in 1874 because the two companies could not agree on charges for using the existing station. Passengers transferring between them initially walked approximately 300 metres along a public road.

Negotiations for a joint station began in 1898. Harbury Corporation contributed land on the condition that the new building provide a direct pedestrian route between the railway and the commercial centre. Construction required the demolition of a grain warehouse and the diversion of a short section of Mill Street.

The joint station opened on 12 May 1909. Its principal building was designed by the railway architect Marion Selvet in red brick with pale sandstone dressings. A glazed roof covered the main concourse and the ends of the valley platforms.

The roof was removed in 1964 after corrosion was discovered in its supporting members. The concourse was subsequently enclosed beneath a lower steel structure. Restoration work in 2008 reinstated the original arrangement of the entrance windows and repaired the clock tower.

= = Layout = =

Platforms 1 and 2 are terminal platforms used mainly by Caldern Valley services. Platforms 3 to 5 serve the main line. A footbridge with lifts connects all platforms, while the terminal platforms also have level access from the concourse.

The former parcels office contains a café and bicycle hire facility. The booking hall retains its tiled floor, although the original wooden ticket counters were replaced in the 1980s.

A bus interchange occupies the southern side of Station Square. The principal taxi rank and short-stay parking area are beside the eastern entrance.

= = Operations = =

Most valley trains arrive at platform 1, with platform 2 used during peak periods or disruption. The track layout permits trains to run directly between the valley and the main line, but this connection is normally used only by freight trains and special passenger services.

The station is staffed throughout the operating day. Passenger information displays are supplemented by a mechanical departure board preserved in the booking hall, which is maintained as a static exhibit.

Lanton Mill railway station

Lanton Mill railway station serves Lanton, a village and former textile manufacturing centre 14.2 kilometres north of Harbury Central. It opened in 1874 and marks the northern end of the double-track section of the Caldern Valley Railway.

The station takes its name from the large weaving mill that formerly occupied land between the railway and the river. The mill closed in 1987, and its surviving buildings have been converted into workshops and housing.

= = History = =

The railway company originally intended to build a station closer to Lanton parish church. The site was moved south after the mill proprietors offered land and agreed to guarantee a minimum annual volume of freight.

The station opened with two passenger platforms, a goods shed and private sidings entering the mill complex. Coal deliveries provided much of its early traffic. Finished cloth was carried south to Harbury and transferred to main-line services.

A covered footbridge was added in 1902 after complaints about passengers crossing the tracks. The original bridge remained in use until 2001, when it was replaced by a steel structure.

Goods facilities closed in 1965, but the mill siding continued to receive occasional wagons until 1978. The goods shed was later demolished. Its site now forms the station car park.

= = Buildings and facilities = =

The main building stands on the southbound platform. It is a single-storey brick structure with a slate roof and a projecting canopy supported by cast-iron brackets. The former stationmaster’s accommodation occupies the northern end and is leased as a private residence.

A smaller shelter stands on the northbound platform. Both platforms have ticket machines and electronic information displays. The station is unstaffed.

Separate ramped entrances provide step-free access to each platform. Passengers unable to use the footbridge must travel approximately 250 metres by the adjacent road to change platforms.

= = Services and use = =

All regular Caldern Valley passenger services call at Lanton Mill. The station is used mainly by commuters travelling to Harbury and pupils attending schools in Merewick.

The village centre is reached by a footpath following the former mill boundary. A section of the old private siding has been retained beside the path, together with a small wagon turntable recovered during redevelopment.

Merewick railway station

Merewick railway station serves the market town of Merewick and the surrounding central Caldern valley. Situated 31.6 kilometres from Harbury Central, it is the principal intermediate station on the Caldern Valley Railway.

The station has two platforms on a passing loop. It also provides access to the branch serving Merewick limestone terminal, which diverges south of the passenger station.

= = History = =

Merewick was the northern terminus of the railway from 1874 until the extension to High Fold opened in 1878. Its original facilities included a locomotive shed, a turntable and extensive goods sidings.

The arrival of the railway encouraged the movement of the town’s livestock market from the High Street to land beside the station. Special cattle trains operated on market days, and pens occupied much of the eastern goods yard. Livestock traffic ended in 1962.

The locomotive shed closed in 1916 after servicing work was concentrated at Harbury and Bellscar. The building survived as a warehouse until a fire in 1973. Its stone foundations remain visible beyond the northern end of platform 2.

Merewick retained a staffed booking office when staffing was withdrawn from most intermediate stations in 1971. The decision reflected its role as a crossing point and the volume of school traffic.

= = Layout and buildings = =

The main station building is constructed from locally quarried limestone. Its central section contains the booking hall, with a waiting room to the south and former offices to the north. A broad canopy extends along most of platform 1.

Platform 2 has a stone waiting shelter built in 1886. A footbridge connects the platforms. Lifts installed in 2019 provide step-free access.

The former signal box stands at the southern end of platform 2. It closed when signalling control transferred to Harbury in 2004. The building is maintained by a local railway society and opens to visitors on selected weekends.

= = Freight facilities = =

The limestone terminal lies 1.8 kilometres south-west of the station on a branch partly following the alignment of an earlier quarry tramway. Loading takes place beneath an enclosed hopper.

Freight trains generally use the branch during gaps in the passenger timetable. A reception siding allows loaded wagons to await collection without occupying the running line.

= = Interchange = =

Bus services from the station forecourt connect Merewick with settlements in the western valley. The town centre and covered market are approximately ten minutes away on foot. A signed walking route follows the riverbank from the station approach.

High Fold railway station

High Fold railway station serves the villages of High Fold and Eastrigg in the upper Caldern valley. It is 47.9 kilometres from Harbury Central and is the least-used of the five stations on the line.

The station occupies a narrow shelf above the eastern bank of the Caldern. Access is by a steep road from High Fold, while a footbridge across the river provides a pedestrian route to Eastrigg.

= = History = =

High Fold opened on 21 June 1878 as the temporary northern terminus of the railway. Construction materials for the Bellscar extension were unloaded in its goods yard.

The station initially handled timber from nearby estates and stone from a small quarry at Eastrigg. A proposed branch to the quarry was never constructed; stone reached the station by horse-drawn carts.

Passenger traffic increased during the interwar period as walking holidays became more common. The railway promoted circular routes through Bracken Wood, and the waiting room displayed a large painted map of local paths.

The goods yard closed in 1961. The station became unstaffed in 1971, although its passing loop remained operational. Following storm damage in 1993, the original timber platform surfaces were replaced with concrete.

= = Facilities = =

High Fold has two platforms linked by a footbridge. The principal building, on the southbound platform, contains a waiting room and a small exhibition about forestry in the valley. Volunteers open the exhibition during summer weekends.

The northbound platform has a timber shelter modelled on the original structure. Access to this platform is by steps, and the station does not have full step-free access.

The former goods yard provides limited parking. There is no taxi rank or regular connecting bus service.

= = Passing loop = =

The loop is long enough to accommodate the passenger trains normally used on the route. It provides flexibility during late running and allows additional summer services to operate.

Under the regular timetable, most trains pass through without crossing another service. The loop is also used when engineering work prevents trains from meeting at Merewick.

Bellscar railway station

Bellscar railway station is the northern terminus of the Caldern Valley Railway. It serves Bellscar, an upland town known for its reservoir, walking routes and former timber industry. The station is 61.8 kilometres from Harbury Central.

Opened in 1882, Bellscar retains much of its original station building and a substantial part of its former goods yard.

= = History = =

The station was built on the southern edge of Bellscar, below the old town. An earlier proposal for a more central terminus was abandoned because it would have required a longer tunnel and a steeper approach.

Bellscar became an important destination for excursion trains during the early twentieth century. The station forecourt contained booking offices for horse-drawn vehicles carrying visitors to the reservoir and nearby hotels.

A second passenger platform was added in 1911. It was used mainly by excursion trains and closed to regular traffic in 1976. The track remains connected and can accommodate special trains.

Timber was the station’s principal freight traffic until loading transferred to a road depot in 1996. The goods shed subsequently became a community workshop.

= = Layout = =

The main platform is covered by a canopy attached to the station building. The building contains a ticket office, waiting room and café. Its upper floor, formerly the stationmaster’s residence, is used as railway staff accommodation.

A run-round loop allows locomotives to change ends. Beyond the platforms, the tracks terminate against buffer stops below Reservoir Road.

The former turntable pit survives north of the goods shed, although the turntable itself was removed in 1960.

= = Services = =

Bellscar is served by all full-length Caldern Valley passenger trains. Trains usually remain at the station for approximately twenty minutes before returning to Harbury.

The station is staffed from morning until early evening. Local buses depart from the forecourt for the reservoir and neighbouring villages. The town centre is reached by a rising footpath through Station Gardens.
