USS Alden Marsh (DD-984) is a fictional United States Navy destroyer whose imagined service history extends from the late Cold War to the early twenty-first century. In this account, the ship was commissioned in 1979 and decommissioned in 2006. It served principally with the Atlantic Fleet, undertaking anti-submarine patrols, carrier escort assignments, maritime interception operations and naval exercises. Its later deployments included service in the Mediterranean Sea, the Persian Gulf and the western Indian Ocean.

Alden Marsh was designed around the detection and prosecution of submarines, with a large sonar installation, helicopter facilities and a relatively quiet propulsion arrangement. Subsequent alterations broadened its employment in surface warfare and coastal operations. The ship underwent two substantial modernisations, although proposals for a more extensive reconstruction were abandoned because of cost and its declining remaining service life.

The destroyer was named for Commander Alden Robert Marsh, a fictional naval officer credited with organising the rescue of survivors from a damaged escort vessel during the Second World War. The ship’s motto, “Steadfast in Company”, referred both to that episode and to its intended role protecting other vessels.

= = Design and construction = =

Planning for Alden Marsh began in the early 1970s, when the navy sought additional destroyers capable of accompanying carrier groups during prolonged operations in the North Atlantic. The design emphasised endurance, helicopter operations and sufficient internal space to accommodate later electronic equipment. Air defence was initially limited to weapons intended primarily for the ship’s own protection.

The completed destroyer measured approximately 172 metres in length, with a beam of 17 metres and a full-load displacement of about 8,100 tonnes. Four gas turbines drove two shafts, giving a maximum speed exceeding 30 knots. Separate machinery spaces reduced the likelihood that a single casualty would disable the entire propulsion plant. Electrical generators were distributed through the hull for similar reasons.

The original armament consisted of two 127 mm guns, a rocket-assisted anti-submarine weapon launcher, lightweight torpedo tubes and short-range surface-to-air missiles. A hangar and flight deck accommodated two naval helicopters, although the ship frequently deployed with only one aircraft. Its principal underwater sensors comprised a bow-mounted sonar and a towed array.

Construction was authorised in 1973. The keel was laid at the fictional Seabrook Naval Shipbuilding Company in Virginia on 18 February 1975, and the ship was launched on 24 September 1977. Eleanor Marsh Bell, the namesake’s daughter, acted as sponsor.

Completion was delayed by defective reduction-gear components and changes to the combat information centre. During builders’ trials, excessive vibration developed near the port shaft at high speed. Replacement of a bearing assembly and modifications to the shaft supports reduced the vibration to acceptable levels. The navy accepted the ship in July 1979.

= = Commissioning and initial operations = =

Alden Marsh was commissioned at Norfolk on 6 October 1979 under Commander Peter W. Haviland. Its initial complement numbered approximately 290 officers and enlisted personnel, including a small aviation support section. The ship joined an Atlantic Fleet destroyer squadron and began a period of equipment testing and individual training.

Shakedown operations took place in the Caribbean between January and March 1980. These included gunfire exercises, engineering casualty drills and helicopter trials. During one replenishment exercise, the failure of a tensioning mechanism caused a fuel hose to separate before pumping began. No injuries occurred, but the incident led to changes in inspection procedures.

The ship subsequently returned to its builder for repairs and outstanding alterations. Work included improved ventilation in electronic equipment compartments and additional insulation around accommodation spaces adjoining machinery rooms. Sailors had reported persistent heat and noise during the Caribbean trials.

In September 1980, Alden Marsh participated in its first major fleet exercise off the eastern coast of the United States. Assigned to an outer anti-submarine screen, it operated with maritime patrol aircraft and another destroyer. The exercise demonstrated the usefulness of its towed sonar but also exposed difficulties in exchanging contact information between ships equipped with different tactical data systems.

= = Early Atlantic service = =

The destroyer’s first overseas deployment began in February 1981. It crossed the Atlantic with a carrier group before entering the Mediterranean, where it conducted exercises and visited ports in Spain, Italy and Greece. Much of the deployment involved maintaining an anti-submarine screen during carrier flight operations.

Alden Marsh also carried out surveillance of naval vessels operating near the group. Such encounters generally involved visual identification, photography and the recording of radar emissions. Standing orders required the destroyer to maintain sufficient distance to avoid interference with the other vessels’ movements.

Following its return to Norfolk in August, the ship entered a short maintenance period. Cracking around several external fittings required reinforcement, while corrosion in seawater piping prompted the replacement of valves and sections of pipe. These defects did not require major structural repairs.

During 1982, Alden Marsh operated primarily in the western Atlantic. It served as a training platform for prospective engineering officers and supported submarine exercises near the continental shelf. In November, it assisted a disabled fishing vessel approximately 160 nautical miles east of Cape Hatteras. A boarding party restored electrical power and provided medical treatment to a crew member with a fractured wrist. The destroyer remained nearby until a tug arrived.

A northern deployment in 1983 took the ship into the Norwegian Sea. Rough weather restricted helicopter operations and damaged exposed aerials. The deployment nevertheless provided experience in maintaining sonar coverage under variable water conditions. Its final report recommended additional cold-weather equipment and revised arrangements for securing stores.

= = Mediterranean deployments = =

Alden Marsh returned to the Mediterranean in April 1984. Its duties included carrier escort, independent surveillance patrols and exercises with allied navies. During periods of heightened regional tension, the ship remained available for the protection or evacuation of American nationals, although it did not conduct an evacuation.

In June, the destroyer was diverted to investigate a merchant vessel reporting smoke in its machinery spaces south of Crete. A damage-control team boarded the vessel and helped contain a fire involving electrical cabling. The merchant ship retained propulsion and proceeded towards port under its own power. Several members of the destroyer’s boarding party were treated for minor smoke inhalation.

The deployment included an extended period at sea after a scheduled port visit was cancelled. Additional provisions were transferred during replenishment, but the ship experienced shortages of several routine maintenance items. Its commanding officer subsequently argued for larger stocks of components associated with frequently serviced pumps and ventilation equipment.

After returning home in October, Alden Marsh entered a restricted availability lasting into early 1985. A close-in defensive gun system was installed, communications equipment was updated and modifications were made to the helicopter control station.

A further Mediterranean deployment in 1986 concentrated on surveillance and escort duties. The ship periodically operated separately from its carrier group, monitoring shipping routes and maintaining a visible naval presence. Despite several precautionary increases in readiness, it did not fire its weapons in combat.

= = First major overhaul = =

Alden Marsh entered a naval shipyard in January 1987 for its first major overhaul. The work lasted fourteen months and included repairs to propulsion machinery, replacement of electrical distribution equipment and extensive maintenance of the sonar systems.

The most significant combat-system alteration was the installation of an improved tactical data system. This allowed operators to combine information from shipboard sensors and external sources more effectively. Additional consoles were fitted in the combat information centre, requiring the relocation of storage spaces and changes to internal cabling.

The ship also received improved electronic countermeasures and launchers for anti-ship missiles. These additions strengthened its surface warfare capability, although anti-submarine operations remained its principal assigned function. The existing anti-submarine launcher was retained.

Habitability improvements included refurbished washrooms, modified galley equipment and better air conditioning in berthing compartments. Some planned work was deferred after inspections revealed more extensive deterioration in auxiliary piping than expected.

The destroyer returned to sea in March 1988. During post-overhaul trials, a faulty control component caused one gas turbine to shut down unexpectedly. The remaining machinery continued to operate normally. Replacement of the component and further testing delayed the completion of trials by nine days.

Refresher training followed in the Caribbean. By the end of the year, Alden Marsh had resumed routine fleet operations.

= = Persian Gulf service = =

In August 1989, Alden Marsh deployed to the Middle East, passing through the Mediterranean and the Suez Canal. Its assignment centred on the protection of commercial shipping and the observation of naval activity in the approaches to the Persian Gulf.

The destroyer escorted groups of merchant vessels through designated transit areas. These operations required coordination with naval helicopters, shore-based aircraft and other escorts. The possibility of mines imposed restrictions on manoeuvring and increased the importance of maintaining accurate navigation.

During one northbound transit, a lookout reported an object resembling a floating mine. Alden Marsh directed the merchant vessels away from the area and summoned a specialist unit. The object was subsequently identified as a partially submerged industrial container. The incident nevertheless delayed the convoy by several hours.

The ship returned to Norfolk in February 1990. Following maintenance and training, it deployed again in January 1991 to support operations associated with the Gulf conflict. Its principal tasks were escorting logistics vessels, monitoring merchant traffic and contributing to local air and surface surveillance.

Alden Marsh did not conduct land-attack missile strikes. Its weapons and assigned position instead suited it to screening and maritime control. Boarding teams inspected vessels when authorised, while the ship’s helicopter extended visual coverage beyond the horizon.

The deployment placed considerable demands on cooling systems and auxiliary machinery. Fine airborne dust accumulated in filters, and high seawater temperatures reduced the efficiency of some equipment. Engineering personnel adopted more frequent cleaning and inspection routines. The ship completed the deployment without a major propulsion failure and returned home in July.

= = Interception and rescue operations = =

Between 1992 and 1994, Alden Marsh alternated maintenance periods with deployments supporting maritime interception operations. Its boarding teams received additional training in document inspection, small-boat handling and the control of merchant crews during searches.

Most boardings were routine. Teams compared cargo manifests with visible cargo, checked the identity of the vessel and reported discrepancies to the authority directing the operation. Language difficulties and incomplete paperwork often lengthened inspections without establishing a violation.

In October 1992, the destroyer rescued eleven people from a coastal cargo vessel that had developed a severe list in the northern Arabian Sea. Its helicopter located two life rafts, while a ship’s boat recovered survivors remaining near the vessel. One survivor required emergency treatment for hypothermia despite the relatively warm conditions.

Alden Marsh remained in the area until further searches found no additional survivors. The rescued crew were transferred ashore the following day. Their vessel sank before a salvage tug could reach it.

During a 1994 Mediterranean deployment, the ship supported the enforcement of restrictions on maritime traffic connected with fighting in south-eastern Europe. Its duties involved identifying, querying and, when directed, inspecting merchant vessels. It also provided communications support when smaller patrol vessels operated beyond reliable contact with their headquarters.

These assignments increased the importance of legal guidance and accurate record-keeping aboard the destroyer. Boarding reports, photographs and radio logs were retained to document decisions made during interceptions.

= = Modernisation and changing employment = =

A second substantial modernisation began in late 1995. The navy considered fitting a vertical missile-launching system but rejected the proposal after reviewing installation costs and the ship’s projected service life. The approved programme concentrated on communications, defensive equipment and machinery reliability.

Satellite communications capacity was expanded, and new computer workstations improved the handling of operational messages. Navigation equipment was replaced, while modifications to the helicopter facilities allowed support for a newer aircraft configuration. The towed sonar received updated processing equipment.

The work also addressed recurring maintenance problems. Several seawater cooling sections were replaced with more corrosion-resistant material, and selected machinery-control components were renewed. Structural inspections identified fatigue cracking around fittings exposed to repeated vibration; these areas were repaired and reinforced.

Alden Marsh resumed operational service in September 1996. Its subsequent employment reflected a broader shift towards independent patrols and participation in smaller multinational groups. The ship continued to practise anti-submarine warfare, but actual deployments increasingly involved surveillance, maritime interception and regional engagement.

In 1997, it conducted exercises with several West African navies during an Atlantic deployment. These activities included communications drills, search-and-rescue practice and demonstrations of boarding procedures. Port visits also provided opportunities for maintenance assistance and exchanges between naval personnel.

The destroyer’s age became more apparent during this period. Spare parts for certain original systems were increasingly difficult to obtain, and maintenance planning depended on identifying suitable replacements before equipment failed.

= = Final operational years = =

Alden Marsh deployed to the Indian Ocean in 1999. During the outward passage, a lubricating-oil leak forced the temporary shutdown of one propulsion unit. Repairs were completed during a scheduled port visit, and the ship continued its deployment without returning to the United States.

Its duties included escorting support ships and monitoring traffic near major shipping routes. The embarked helicopter carried out reconnaissance and occasionally transferred small quantities of urgent stores between vessels. Restrictions on flying hours required the air detachment to balance operational demands against maintenance requirements.

Following the attacks on the United States in September 2001, the destroyer initially supported surveillance operations in the western Atlantic. It subsequently deployed overseas as part of a group assigned to maritime security duties. Boarding preparations were revised to account for greater concern about concealed weapons and hostile resistance.

In 2003, Alden Marsh again operated in the Middle East. It protected logistics movements and maintained surveillance stations, with frequent changes of assignment as operational requirements developed. The ship did not undertake sustained shore bombardment.

A machinery-space fire in February 2004 caused its most serious late-service casualty. The fire began when fuel escaping from a damaged connection reached a hot surface. Automatic alarms and a rapid response confined it to a limited area. Four sailors suffered minor injuries, and the affected machinery space was temporarily evacuated.

The destroyer returned to port using its remaining propulsion machinery. Repairs took approximately three months. An investigation identified deficiencies in the inspection of the connection and in the recording of earlier maintenance. Revised procedures were introduced throughout the squadron.

Its final overseas deployment began in May 2005 and included exercises, escort duties and port visits in the Mediterranean. By then, maintaining older electronic systems required frequent attention, although the ship remained available for its assigned tasks.

= = Decommissioning and disposal = =

Alden Marsh returned to Norfolk in November 2005. A subsequent material assessment found that continued service would require substantial investment in electrical systems, auxiliary machinery and hull preservation. The navy selected the destroyer for retirement.

Decommissioning took place on 18 August 2006. Former commanding officers, crew members and members of the Marsh family attended the ceremony. The ship’s bell, selected nameplates and other commemorative objects were retained for naval collections.

The hull entered reserve storage while officials considered possible transfer or preservation. A proposal to establish a museum ship failed to secure sufficient funding for berthing and long-term maintenance. No foreign transfer was completed.

In 2011, the navy approved disposal by dismantling. Removal of controlled equipment and hazardous materials preceded towing to a recycling facility. Dismantling was completed the following year, ending the ship’s fictional thirty-three-year existence from commissioning to final disposal.
