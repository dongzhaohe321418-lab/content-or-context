Matteo Bellori (12 February 1674 – 19 September 1743) was an Italian composer, organist and teacher. He worked principally in the northern Italian cities of Ferrara, Mantua and Verona, composing sacred vocal music, chamber cantatas and instrumental works. His surviving compositions include two settings of the Requiem, a collection of trio sonatas and several cycles of music for Vespers. Although he held no major court appointment, his music circulated among cathedral establishments and religious confraternities during the first half of the eighteenth century.

Bellori’s career was shaped by employment in institutions with limited musical resources. Much of his church music accommodates small ensembles, with instrumental parts that can be omitted or redistributed. His larger compositions combine contrapuntal choruses with solo movements influenced by the Italian chamber cantata. His instrumental music is characterised by compact forms, imitative exchanges and sustained passages over repeated bass patterns.

Most of Bellori’s works remained in manuscript during his lifetime. His reputation declined after his death, although several motets continued to be copied for provincial churches. Scholarly interest developed in the late nineteenth century following the identification of manuscripts in Verona and Ferrara.

= = Early life and education = =

Bellori was born in Ferrara, the third of five children of Giovanni Bellori, a cooper, and Caterina Malvezzi, the daughter of a parish clerk. The family lived near the church of San Michele, where Matteo probably received his first musical instruction. A parish account for 1683 records a payment for clothing supplied to “the boy Bellori”, generally identified as the composer.

By 1686 he was serving as a chorister at a collegiate church in Ferrara. His early education included plainchant, elementary Latin and keyboard playing. The organist Lorenzo Vanneschi, with whom Bellori later maintained a correspondence, appears to have supervised his training. A surviving exercise book attributed to Bellori contains figured bass realisations, short contrapuntal studies and extracts from liturgical texts.

Following the breaking of his voice, Bellori remained attached to the church as an assistant to the organist. His duties included tuning small organ pipes, copying parts and rehearsing younger singers. These activities gave him practical experience of preparing music for performance, which subsequently became an important part of his professional work.

An often-repeated account that Bellori studied in Bologna rests on a nineteenth-century interpretation of an undated letter. The letter refers to a proposed journey rather than a completed visit. His familiarity with Bolognese instrumental music can also be explained by the circulation of printed collections in Ferrara.

Bellori’s earliest securely dated composition is a short setting of the Marian antiphon *Alma Redemptoris Mater*, copied in 1693. Scored for two voices and continuo, it already displays his preference for deriving successive phrases from a small number of melodic figures.

= = Career = =

= = Ferrara appointments = =

In 1695 Bellori obtained a salaried position as organist at a small religious foundation in Ferrara. The appointment included responsibility for teaching four boys and providing music for principal feast days. His annual salary was supplemented by payments for weddings, funerals and performances organised by lay confraternities.

The foundation’s accounts indicate that Bellori frequently hired additional singers rather than instrumentalists. His compositions from this period are predominantly for two or three voices with continuo. Several survive in performing parts bearing corrections in his hand, including alterations that lowered demanding passages for particular singers.

Bellori also worked as a copyist. Between 1697 and 1701 he supplied music to at least three churches outside Ferrara. This activity brought him into contact with clerical patrons who later commissioned original compositions. His correspondence suggests that copying was financially important, although he complained that customers sometimes treated the preparation of instrumental parts as part of the original commission.

A dispute over teaching duties contributed to his departure in 1702. The governing chapter expected him to provide daily instruction throughout the year, while Bellori maintained that his contract excluded certain holiday periods. The disagreement was settled without litigation, and he continued to receive occasional commissions from Ferrara after leaving the city.

= = Mantua = =

Bellori moved to Mantua in the autumn of 1702. He initially supported himself through private teaching and temporary church engagements before becoming organist and music director of a confraternity dedicated to the Holy Cross. The confraternity maintained a small body of singers and employed instrumentalists for major observances.

His Mantuan years produced his first substantial sacred works. These included a sequence of Lenten motets and a Requiem in F minor, probably composed for a collective memorial service in 1706. The Requiem circulated in several versions, reflecting the different resources of institutions that performed it.

Bellori also supplied chamber cantatas for domestic gatherings. His principal patron in this field was Countess Livia Ardesi, whose household employed two singers and occasionally engaged visiting violinists. Eleven surviving cantatas can be associated with her circle through dedications, paper types or references in household accounts.

Political disruption and reductions in private expenditure affected Mantuan musical employment during the following years. Bellori’s payments became irregular, and he sought positions elsewhere. A letter of 1709 describes the difficulty of maintaining singers when funds were released only after performances had taken place.

He left Mantua in 1711. His departure appears to have been amicable: the confraternity retained his music, and he later sent it revised parts for two motets. The relationships established in Mantua remained significant to the circulation of his secular compositions.

= = Verona = =

After a brief return to Ferrara, Bellori settled in Verona in 1712. He became organist at the church of Santa Cecilia al Ponte, an institution supported by local merchants and several minor noble families. In 1716 his responsibilities were expanded to include the direction of all music on Sundays and principal feast days.

This was the longest and most stable appointment of his career. The church possessed an organ, a small permanent choir and funds for additional musicians on specified occasions. Bellori negotiated a separate allowance for copying and maintenance, reducing the need to meet these expenses from his salary.

His published collection of twelve trio sonatas appeared in 1718. Dedicated to the merchant Antonio Roveda, it was financed largely by advance subscriptions. The subscription list included clerics, amateur instrumentalists and music teachers from several northern Italian cities. No second edition is known.

During the 1720s Bellori composed much of his surviving Vespers music. Rather than assembling a single fixed cycle, he produced alternative settings of individual psalms and antiphons. Copyists subsequently combined these into different sequences, sometimes obscuring the original dates and relationships between movements.

Bellori applied unsuccessfully for a more prestigious directorship in 1727. The appointment went to a candidate with stronger institutional connections and experience directing larger ensembles. Bellori nevertheless remained active in Verona’s musical life, examining prospective organists and advising on repairs to church instruments.

= = Final years = =

Bellori’s health began to deteriorate in the late 1730s. Church accounts record payments to substitutes, initially during winter months and later for extended periods. His assistant, Paolo Nerini, assumed most routine rehearsals by 1741, although Bellori continued to compose and supervise copying.

His final dated work is a setting of *Salve Regina* for alto, two violins and continuo, completed in March 1743. Its relatively restrained vocal writing may reflect the capabilities of the intended singer rather than a general change in style.

Bellori died in Verona on 19 September 1743 after an illness described in the burial register as a prolonged fever. He was buried in the church where he had served. His will divided his manuscripts between Nerini and his nephew Giovanni, a schoolteacher in Ferrara. This division accounts for the two principal groups of surviving sources.

= = Music = =

= = Sacred vocal works = =

Sacred music forms the largest part of Bellori’s surviving output. His settings range from brief motets for a single voice to extended works for soloists, chorus, strings and continuo. Trumpets appear in two festive compositions, but woodwind instruments are not specified in any securely attributed score.

Bellori generally organised longer texts into contrasting sections. Solo passages present individual verses, while choruses mark structural divisions or passages of collective affirmation. Changes of metre and texture often correspond to changes in the text, although he rarely interrupts a movement solely to illustrate a single word.

His choral writing combines passages of chordal declamation with short imitative sections. Fully developed fugues are uncommon. More typically, a subject passes through the voices before yielding to a cadence or a return to homophonic writing. This approach allowed contrapuntal variety without requiring a large or exceptionally trained choir.

The two Requiem settings demonstrate different responses to institutional needs. The F minor Requiem uses divided vocal groups and several independent instrumental passages. The later D minor setting, dated 1724, is shorter and can be performed with continuo alone. Both avoid elaborate solo display.

Bellori’s treatment of plainchant is selective. Some antiphon settings preserve recognisable chant melodies in long notes, usually in an upper voice. Elsewhere, chant provides only an opening phrase or a melodic outline. Nineteenth-century descriptions of his music as consistently chant-based therefore overstate the evidence.

Several scores contain instructions allowing instrumental passages to be omitted when players were unavailable. These provisions appear to be original rather than later adaptations and illustrate his concern with the practical conditions of church performance.

= = Cantatas and other secular music = =

Twenty-three chamber cantatas are attributed to Bellori with reasonable confidence. Most are for soprano and continuo; four add violins, and two are written for alto. Their texts concern separation, jealousy, pastoral retreat and the instability of romantic attachment.

The usual design consists of two arias linked by recitative, sometimes preceded by an introductory recitative. Bellori’s recitatives are generally concise, with changes of harmony supporting rhetorical turns. His arias favour clearly articulated phrases, although sequences and extensions occasionally disrupt their symmetry.

The cantata *Sotto l’ombra d’un alloro* is unusual in replacing its concluding aria with a brief arioso. Another, *Non più catene*, incorporates a recurring bass figure across both vocal and instrumental passages. Such features suggest experimentation within the modest scale of domestic performance.

The authors of most texts are unknown. Three poems occur in a manuscript anthology associated with the Ardesi household, but their presence does not establish authorship. Bellori occasionally altered a text during composition, repeating phrases or replacing words to accommodate musical stresses.

No complete opera by Bellori survives. A reference to payment for “music for the comedy” in 1708 may concern incidental songs or an interlude. Later claims that he composed several operas derive from an inventory in which dramatic cantatas were listed under a broad theatrical heading.

= = Instrumental works = =

The twelve trio sonatas of 1718 are Bellori’s only securely documented lifetime publication. They are scored for two violins and continuo, with a separate bass part suitable for cello or another bowed bass instrument. Most contain four movements, alternating slow and fast tempos.

The opening movements frequently use suspensions and overlapping entries. Faster movements depend on short subjects, sequential repetition and exchanges between the violins. The bass is usually supportive but occasionally introduces material subsequently taken up by the upper parts.

Several sonatas include dance-derived movements without dance titles. Their rhythms suggest the courante, sarabande and gigue, although Bellori modifies phrase lengths and cadential patterns. The collection consequently combines features associated with both church and chamber sonatas.

A group of keyboard pieces survives in a teaching manuscript compiled over several decades. Attribution is complicated by the presence of works by other composers. Seven pieces bear Bellori’s name, including three short toccatas and a set of variations over a descending bass.

These keyboard works make limited use of virtuoso passagework. Their main technical demands involve the coordination of imitative lines, sustained dissonances and changes between chordal and contrapuntal textures. They may have served both instructional and liturgical purposes.

= = Style and compositional practice = =

Bellori’s music belongs to the transition between late seventeenth-century contrapuntal traditions and the more regularly phrased idioms of the early eighteenth century. Older techniques remain prominent in his sacred works, while his cantatas and sonatas show greater reliance on repeated melodic patterns and clearly differentiated sections.

His harmonic language is predominantly diatonic, with chromatic writing concentrated in slow introductions, lament texts and passages concerning death or penitence. Descending bass lines recur throughout his output, but their treatment varies from literal repetition to freely developed sequences.

Surviving drafts show that Bellori frequently revised inner parts after establishing the melody and bass. He also shortened ritornellos and simplified vocal passages when preparing performance copies. These revisions suggest that he regarded instrumentation and surface detail as adaptable while preserving the broader structure.

Similarities between certain sacred and secular works indicate occasional reuse of musical material. In most cases, however, the relationship involves a theme or bass pattern rather than the wholesale transfer of a movement. Different versions should therefore be distinguished from compositions sharing common material.

= = Teaching and personal life = =

Bellori taught singers, keyboard players and amateur composers throughout his career. His pupils included Paolo Nerini and the organist Cecilia Roncati, who later worked in a Veronese convent. Lessons appear to have centred on figured bass, singing from written parts and practical counterpoint.

A notebook associated with his teaching presents exercises in progressively more complex textures. Short bass patterns are followed by examples of imitation and the preparation and resolution of dissonances. The annotations emphasise clear part-writing and the avoidance of awkward vocal intervals.

Bellori never married. During much of his residence in Verona he shared a household with his widowed sister Lucia and her two children. His correspondence concerns domestic expenses, teaching arrangements and the movement of manuscripts more often than aesthetic questions.

His estate was modest. It included two keyboard instruments, a small library, copying materials and several outstanding debts owed by pupils. The will made provision for his sister and cancelled the lesson fees of one unnamed student.

= = Sources and reception = =

Approximately ninety compositions survive under Bellori’s name, although the number of distinct works depends on how revisions and incomplete settings are counted. The principal sources are church partbooks, household manuscripts and copies made by pupils. Only a minority survive in autograph.

Attribution is particularly difficult where manuscripts identify the composer simply as “Bellori” or “M. B.”. Some nineteenth-century catalogues also confused him with a later violinist of the same surname. Modern inventories exclude several concertos formerly assigned to him.

Bellori’s music remained in limited use after his death. Copies of his shorter motets were made into the 1770s, often with altered bass lines or reduced vocal parts. His larger works disappeared from regular performance sooner, probably because their materials required greater preparation.

Interest revived when the Veronese librarian Ettore Salvi described a group of manuscripts in 1889. Salvi praised Bellori’s contrapuntal craftsmanship but interpreted his career largely through the decline of provincial church patronage. Subsequent scholarship has placed greater emphasis on adaptation, circulation and the relationship between composition and local resources.

Modern performances have concentrated on the trio sonatas, chamber cantatas and shorter sacred works. Bellori is generally regarded as a representative provincial composer whose surviving music documents practices less fully preserved in the careers of prominent court and operatic musicians.
