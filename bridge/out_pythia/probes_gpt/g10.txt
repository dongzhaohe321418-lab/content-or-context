Oregon Route 417

Oregon Route 417 (OR 417) is a state highway in the north-western part of the US state of Oregon. It runs for 47.6 miles (76.6 km) from OR 18 near Alder Junction to US Route 101 (US 101) at Bracken Bay. The highway connects the agricultural communities of the lower Bellweather Valley with the Pacific coast, crossing the northern Coast Range at Martin Pass. Most of the route is a two-lane rural road, with short sections of additional climbing lanes on the eastern approach to the pass.

The highway serves the communities of Alder Junction, Westhaven, Bellweather, Cedar Fork and Bracken Bay. Its eastern section carries local agricultural traffic and provides access to packing facilities and small industrial sites. The central section passes through commercial timberland and state forest, while the western section follows the Little Bracken River towards the coast. Recreational traffic increases during the summer, particularly between Cedar Fork and US 101.

The route developed from county roads constructed between the 1880s and the early twentieth century. A continuous road across Martin Pass opened in 1928, although parts remained gravelled until the 1950s. The state assumed responsibility for the corridor in 1937 and assigned the OR 417 designation in 1941. Subsequent improvements included the replacement of narrow bridges, reconstruction of the pass approaches and a bypass of central Bellweather. The highway retains several sections of its original alignment.

= = Route description = =

OR 417 begins at a signal-controlled junction with OR 18, approximately 3 miles (4.8 km) east of Alder Junction. From this intersection, it travels north-west across a broad terrace above the Bellweather River. The surrounding land is principally used for grass seed production, orchards and pasture. Farm entrances occur frequently, and several roadside drainage channels pass beneath the highway through concrete culverts.

After 2.1 miles (3.4 km), the route crosses Alder Creek and enters Alder Junction. Within the community, the highway is named Mill Street and passes a primary school, a volunteer fire station and a small commercial area. A former railway goods depot stands south of the road. The railway tracks were removed in 1986, but the highway continues to cross the former railway corridor at a shallow angle.

West of Alder Junction, OR 417 turns north and follows the eastern side of the Bellweather Valley. This section has relatively gentle gradients and long straight stretches interrupted by curves around low hills. At Westhaven, the route intersects County Road 26, which provides access to the eastern part of the valley. A roadside agricultural weigh station operates seasonally near the junction.

The highway crosses the Bellweather River on the Westhaven Bridge, a three-span concrete structure completed in 1974. Beyond the bridge, the valley narrows and the road follows the river’s northern bank. The surrounding farmland becomes interspersed with wooded slopes. Near milepost 12, the route passes the entrance to Bellweather County Park, which contains a riverside picnic area and a boat launch used mainly by small fishing craft.

= = Bellweather and the eastern foothills = =

OR 417 enters Bellweather from the south-east along a bypass completed in 1983. The former route through the town centre follows River Street and Main Street. The bypass runs beside a light industrial area and crosses the Bellweather River for a second time before meeting North Main Street at a roundabout. This is the only roundabout on the route.

Beyond Bellweather, the highway begins a sustained climb into the Coast Range. Agricultural land gives way to Douglas fir woodland and commercial forestry plantations. The route follows Millstone Creek through a narrow valley, crossing the stream three times within 4 miles (6.4 km). Several logging roads meet the highway, although most are gated or subject to seasonal access restrictions.

At milepost 20.8, OR 417 passes the former settlement of Millstone. The settlement developed around a sawmill and logging camp but lost most of its population after the mill closed in 1962. A small cemetery and the foundations of the mill office remain visible from a county-maintained access road. There are no commercial services along the highway between Bellweather and Cedar Fork.

The climb becomes steeper west of Millstone. Two westbound climbing lanes allow slower vehicles to be overtaken before the road reaches Martin Pass. The highway crosses the pass at an elevation of 1,684 feet (513 m). A gravel parking area on the southern side provides access to a forest trail, but there is no developed viewpoint at the summit.

= = Western descent and coastal section = =

West of Martin Pass, OR 417 descends through a series of broad curves into the headwaters of the Little Bracken River. This section lies within North Bracken State Forest. The slopes are heavily wooded, and small streams cross beneath the carriageway at frequent intervals. Retaining walls support the road at several locations where the valley sides are particularly steep.

The highway reaches Cedar Fork at milepost 34.2. The community occupies a small area of level ground at the confluence of Cedar Creek and the Little Bracken River. A general shop, service station and maintenance depot stand near the junction with South Fork Road. The depot is used for winter road maintenance and the storage of emergency equipment.

Beyond Cedar Fork, the route follows the Little Bracken River westwards. The gradient decreases, although the road remains winding. Several short sections lie close to the river and have been protected with rock revetments. At Elk Meadow, the forest opens into a floodplain used for grazing. A small rest area on the northern side of the highway provides toilets, picnic tables and an interpretative panel describing the river’s salmon habitat.

The final 5 miles (8 km) pass through increasingly developed land on the eastern outskirts of Bracken Bay. The highway crosses the Little Bracken River on the Bayview Bridge and continues along Harbour Road. Pavements and cycle lanes begin near Bracken Bay High School. OR 417 ends at a signal-controlled intersection with US 101, approximately 0.7 miles (1.1 km) east of the town’s waterfront. Harbour Road continues west as a municipal street.

= = History = =

= = Early roads = =

The earliest portions of the corridor were local roads linking farms in the Bellweather Valley with mills and river landings. County records describe a wagon road between Alder Junction and Westhaven by 1884. Its alignment followed property boundaries and avoided poorly drained ground, producing a less direct route than the modern highway.

A separate road extended inland from Bracken Bay during the 1890s. Initially, it served dairy farms and timber claims along the lower Little Bracken River. Construction was financed through a combination of county expenditure and labour contributed by local landowners. By 1906, the road reached Cedar Fork, but travel farther east remained dependent on pack trails and temporary logging tracks.

Proposals to connect the two road systems emerged in 1911. Supporters argued that a through road would shorten journeys between the coast and inland railway stations. Opposition centred on construction costs and the difficulty of maintaining a mountain road through unstable ground. A preliminary survey identified three possible crossings, of which Martin Pass offered the lowest estimated construction cost.

Work began on the eastern approach in 1919. Progress was slow because funding was allocated annually and much of the excavation was carried out with horse-drawn equipment. Construction from the western side began in 1923, using a mixture of county crews and private contractors.

= = Completion and state adoption = =

The two construction projects met near Martin Pass in September 1928. A public opening ceremony took place the following month, with vehicles travelling in convoy from Bellweather to Bracken Bay. The completed road was approximately 52 miles (84 km) long and included steep gradients, narrow timber bridges and numerous bends.

Although described locally as an all-weather road, the mountain section was frequently closed by snow, fallen trees and landslides. Most of its surface consisted of compacted gravel. During prolonged rainfall, heavy vehicles could create deep ruts, and county authorities periodically imposed load restrictions.

The state highway commission accepted the road into the state system in 1937 after negotiations with the counties responsible for its maintenance. The transfer agreement required the counties to secure outstanding rights of way and remove several encroachments. State maintenance began that July.

The OR 417 designation was introduced in 1941 as part of a programme to provide consistent route signing on secondary highways. Early signs were installed at principal junctions and community boundaries. Wartime restrictions on materials delayed more extensive improvements, although maintenance crews replaced damaged culverts and strengthened two bridges used by timber lorries.

Following the war, rising motor traffic and increased timber production led to renewed investment. Paving proceeded in stages from each end of the route. The final gravel section, extending approximately 7 miles (11 km) across Martin Pass, received a bituminous surface in 1956.

= = Reconstruction and bypass proposals = =

Engineering surveys undertaken in the late 1950s identified the eastern pass approach as the route’s principal constraint. Several curves had advisory speeds below 25 miles per hour (40 km/h), and the road lacked sufficient width for large vehicles to pass comfortably. Reconstruction between Millstone and the summit began in 1961.

The project reduced the maximum gradient, widened the carriageway and replaced several sharp bends with longer curves. Excavated material was used to build embankments across minor gullies. Two sections of the former road remained accessible as forestry roads, while others were abandoned. The reconstructed approach opened in 1965.

A broader proposal to rebuild the entire mountain crossing was considered in 1968. It included a short summit tunnel and a new alignment on the southern side of the Little Bracken valley. The estimated cost exceeded the funding available for the corridor, and projected traffic volumes did not justify proceeding. The proposal was removed from the state’s construction programme in 1972.

In Bellweather, through traffic continued to use narrow streets in the town centre. Logging vehicles passed close to shops, and queues formed at the junction of River Street and Main Street. A bypass was first formally proposed in 1969, but disagreements over its alignment delayed detailed design.

The selected route followed the town’s southern edge and required a new river crossing. Construction began in 1981, and the bypass opened in November 1983. Responsibility for the former highway streets passed to the town the following year.

= = Later improvements = =

From the late 1980s onwards, work on OR 417 concentrated on individual safety and maintenance projects. These included wider shoulders, replacement guardrails, improved junction visibility and drainage reconstruction. A westbound climbing lane east of Martin Pass opened in 1992, followed by a second climbing lane in 1998.

The Bayview Bridge was replaced between 2004 and 2006. Its predecessor, a steel truss bridge completed in 1932, had a narrow deck and required restrictions on some heavy vehicles. The replacement was built immediately downstream, allowing the old bridge to remain in use during most of the work. A short section of its eastern approach became a riverside footpath.

In 2013, the North Main Street junction at Bellweather was converted into a roundabout. The scheme followed a series of collisions involving vehicles turning across opposing traffic. Associated works included pedestrian crossings and a shared walking and cycling path connecting the bypass with nearby housing.

Resurfacing and drainage work between Cedar Fork and Elk Meadow took place in 2018 and 2019. The project also replaced two culverts that restricted fish passage. Temporary signals controlled traffic through the construction areas.

= = Flooding and landslides = =

OR 417 crosses terrain prone to slope movement, particularly west of Martin Pass. Deeply weathered sedimentary rocks, steep valley sides and heavy seasonal rainfall contribute to recurrent landslides. Maintenance records distinguish between shallow slips affecting the roadside and larger movements extending beneath the road.

Severe flooding in December 1964 damaged bridge approaches and washed out several sections beside the Little Bracken River. The highway remained closed to through traffic for 19 days. Temporary repairs restored a single lane before permanent reconstruction was completed the following summer.

A major landslide near milepost 31 occurred in February 1996 after prolonged rainfall. Approximately 180 feet (55 m) of carriageway subsided towards the river. An initial diversion used a forestry road, but its steep gradients limited the vehicles permitted to use it. The highway reopened after engineers constructed a supported embankment and installed additional drainage.

Further slope movement at the same location prompted installation of monitoring equipment in 2007. Measurements are used to assess changes in ground conditions and determine whether additional inspections are required. Smaller rockfalls and fallen trees continue to cause occasional short closures during winter storms.

= = Traffic and operation = =

Traffic volumes vary considerably along the route. The busiest sections are near Alder Junction and within Bracken Bay, where the highway carries local journeys as well as through traffic. The mountain crossing has substantially lower volumes.

A traffic survey in 2022 recorded an annual average daily traffic count of approximately 9,800 vehicles near the western terminus, compared with 1,700 vehicles near Martin Pass. The Bellweather bypass carried about 5,600 vehicles per day. Heavy vehicles accounted for a larger share of traffic in the central section than at either end.

Timber transport remains an important use of the route. Agricultural vehicles are more common in the eastern valley, particularly during harvesting periods. Summer traffic includes visitors travelling to coastal accommodation, forest campsites and fishing locations.

Speed limits generally range from 25 to 35 miles per hour (40 to 56 km/h) within settlements and reach 55 miles per hour (89 km/h) on rural sections. Numerous curves have lower advisory speeds. Overtaking opportunities are limited in the mountains, and roadside signs identify several places where slower vehicles can pull off the carriageway.

The highway is maintained throughout the year. Snowfall at Martin Pass is intermittent, but ice can persist on shaded bends. Maintenance crews apply grit and de-icing material when required. During severe weather, temporary restrictions may apply to vehicles without suitable traction equipment.

= = Environmental features = =

The highway passes through farmland, managed forest and river floodplain habitats. Douglas fir is the principal tree species on the mountain slopes, accompanied by western hemlock, red alder and bigleaf maple. Sitka spruce becomes more common towards the coast.

The Little Bracken River and several tributaries support migratory salmon and trout. Road construction altered some stream channels and created barriers at older culverts. Later maintenance projects have included wider crossings with natural streambed material to improve fish passage.

Roosevelt elk frequently cross the highway near Elk Meadow and along the western forest boundary. Warning signs mark the principal crossing areas. Vegetation is cut back at selected locations to improve visibility, although steep slopes and nearby watercourses limit the width of cleared verges.

Roadside invasive plants are managed through mowing, selective herbicide application and removal during construction work. Drainage projects are scheduled to reduce the amount of sediment entering streams. Where work occurs close to the river, temporary barriers separate excavation areas from flowing water.

= = Former alignments = =

Several portions of the original road survive outside the present state-maintained alignment. In Bellweather, River Street and Main Street retain the commercial buildings associated with the former through route. The town has installed small historical markers identifying the old highway corridor.

East of Martin Pass, abandoned curves can be recognised by level benches cut into the hillside. Most are overgrown, although two remain in use for forestry access. The former summit approach is closed to public motor vehicles because of deteriorating drainage structures.

At Bracken Bay, the approach to the original Bayview Bridge forms part of a short riverside trail. A salvaged bridge plaque is displayed beside the path. No part of the former steel superstructure remains at the crossing.
