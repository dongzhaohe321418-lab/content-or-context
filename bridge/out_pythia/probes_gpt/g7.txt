Castell Nantglas

Castell Nantglas is a ruined medieval castle overlooking the Nant Glas valley in western Powys, Wales. It occupies a rocky spur above the confluence of the Nant Glas and the Afon Celyn, approximately 6 kilometres north-west of the village of Llanfihangel-y-Graig. The surviving structure consists of an irregular stone enclosure, a twin-towered gatehouse, the lower courses of a residential tower and the foundations of domestic buildings. Earthworks on the western approach mark the position of an outer enclosure.

The castle developed from a fortified residence established in the late twelfth century by the Welsh lord Madog ap Rhirid. Most of its surviving masonry dates from two building campaigns between approximately 1230 and 1290. During the later Middle Ages, it served as an administrative centre for a small upland lordship and as a residence for officials responsible for collecting rents and supervising woodland. Its military importance declined during the fifteenth century, and parts of the complex were subsequently adapted for agricultural use.

Castell Nantglas was largely abandoned by the early seventeenth century. Antiquarian interest developed during the eighteenth century, followed by limited consolidation in the 1890s. Archaeological investigations have identified evidence of metalworking, food storage and substantial alterations to the water supply. The castle is a scheduled monument and is accessible by a public footpath from the valley road.

= = Name and location = =

The name Castell Nantglas means “castle of the green stream” or “castle of the blue stream”, reflecting the range of meanings associated with the Welsh word glas. Medieval documents use several forms, including Nantlas, Nant Glas and Castrum de Nantglas. The spelling Nantglass occurs in some English-language estate records of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

The castle stands approximately 210 metres above sea level on a spur of fractured siltstone. Steep slopes provide natural protection to the north and south-east. On the west, where the spur joins higher ground, a rock-cut ditch separates the inner enclosure from the approach. The surrounding landscape comprises enclosed pasture, small areas of broadleaved woodland and unenclosed grazing on the higher ridges.

The site overlooks a former route between the upper Celyn valley and markets farther west. Although unsuitable for heavy wheeled traffic, this route supported the movement of livestock, packhorses and seasonal agricultural workers. A ford below the castle was replaced by a narrow stone bridge during the post-medieval period.

The location offered oversight of local movement rather than control of a major regional corridor. The castle’s modest scale and limited accommodation suggest that it was intended principally to support lordship within the valley.

= = History = =

= = = Early occupation = = =

The earliest securely dated activity on the castle spur belongs to the late twelfth century. Excavation beneath the eastern curtain wall revealed postholes, a shallow drainage channel and fragments of coarse pottery. These remains have been interpreted as part of a timber hall and its associated enclosure. Scattered worked flints indicate earlier human presence but do not establish continuous occupation.

A later copy of a land agreement attributes the foundation of a fortified residence at Nantglas to Madog ap Rhirid in about 1185. The agreement concerns grazing rights and payments owed by neighbouring settlements. Its description of the property is brief, and it is uncertain whether the residence already included substantial defences.

The first enclosure probably consisted of a timber palisade above an earthen bank. A ditch across the western approach may also date from this period, although subsequent widening removed much of its original profile. The excavated timber buildings were arranged around an open yard rather than a raised mound.

Nantglas consequently differs from the motte-and-bailey castles found elsewhere along the Welsh border. Its earliest layout appears to have developed around an existing residential site, with the natural rock spur providing much of its defensive strength.

= = = Thirteenth-century rebuilding = = =

Madog’s grandson, Rhys ap Gruffudd of Nantglas, began replacing the timber defences with stone during the second quarter of the thirteenth century. The earliest surviving masonry comprises sections of the northern curtain and the foundations of a rectangular tower at the eastern end of the enclosure.

The stone came principally from shallow quarries on the hillside west of the castle. More workable sandstone was brought from farther down the valley for doorways, window surrounds and other architectural details. Variations in mortar and coursing indicate that construction proceeded in stages, probably as resources became available.

A dispute over inheritance interrupted work in the 1250s. Two branches of the family claimed revenues from the same settlements, and an arbitration agreement divided several obligations between them. The castle itself remained with Rhys’s eldest son, Owain, while his younger brother received rights to a mill and part of the summer pasture.

The gatehouse and southern curtain belong to a later campaign, conventionally dated to the 1270s or 1280s. These works strengthened the most accessible side of the site and provided more formal residential accommodation above the entrance passage. Their construction may reflect both regional insecurity and an attempt to display the status of the resident family.

Following Edward I’s conquest of Wales, the lordship retained a measure of local continuity under new political arrangements. A survey compiled near the end of the century recorded a hall, chapel, kitchen, granary and two serviceable towers at Nantglas.

= = = Later medieval administration = = =

During the fourteenth century, Castell Nantglas became increasingly associated with the administration of its surrounding estate. Accounts record payments to a constable, a porter and a small household staff. There is little evidence for a permanent garrison of substantial size.

The constable supervised repairs, received certain rents and maintained stores. Some obligations were paid in grain, livestock or labour rather than money. Tenants from three nearby settlements were required to assist with transporting timber, while others supplied oats or undertook seasonal work on the castle’s roofs.

The residence was occupied intermittently by its lords, who also held property elsewhere. Their absence increased the importance of the constable and steward. A chamber above the gate passage was used for meetings concerning tenancy, boundaries and unpaid dues.

Building accounts from the middle decades of the century mention repairs to gutters, the replacement of a kitchen roof and the purchase of iron fittings for the gate. A new stone-lined cistern was also constructed after complaints about the reliability of the existing supply.

The estate suffered falling income during the later fourteenth century. Several holdings were recorded as vacant, and some labour services were converted into cash payments. These changes reduced the castle’s direct involvement in agricultural production without ending its administrative role.

= = = Fifteenth-century conflict and recovery = = =

The castle’s condition during the rebellion of Owain Glyndŵr is imperfectly documented. A retrospective account states that the gate and part of the hall were burned during disturbances in the early fifteenth century. Excavation has identified fire-damaged deposits near the entrance, but the material cannot be assigned securely to a particular event.

There is no clear evidence for a prolonged siege. The limited destruction and survival of much of the domestic accommodation suggest either a brief attack or the deliberate burning of buildings following abandonment.

Repairs undertaken during the 1420s concentrated on making the residential and storage areas usable. The eastern tower received a new timber floor, while a smaller hall was created within the footprint of its predecessor. Some defensive features were retained, although expenditure increasingly favoured roofs, drainage and domestic fittings.

By the middle of the century, Nantglas functioned mainly as an estate residence. Its household remained small, and visits by the owning family were irregular. An inventory lists bedding, cooking vessels, chests, agricultural tools and a limited quantity of arms.

The outer enclosure was probably brought into more intensive agricultural use during this period. A building against its southern boundary has been interpreted as a barn or cattle shelter.

= = = Decline and abandonment = = =

Ownership passed through marriage to the Vaughan family of Cwmderwen in the late fifteenth century. Their principal residence lay in a more accessible part of the valley, and Castell Nantglas gradually lost its remaining residential functions.

Sixteenth-century accounts describe expenditure on a storehouse and cattle accommodation but make few references to the main hall. Stone was removed from some internal buildings, apparently for repairs elsewhere on the estate. The chapel had ceased to be maintained by the 1560s.

A survey of 1608 described the castle as partly roofless. The gatehouse still contained a habitable chamber, occupied by an estate servant, while the former kitchen was being used for storage. The eastern tower was already considered unsafe.

There is no evidence that Nantglas was fortified during the Civil Wars. Later traditions connecting its destruction with parliamentary troops appear to have developed after the buildings had fallen into ruin.

By the end of the seventeenth century, agricultural occupation was confined to the outer enclosure. The inner court accumulated rubble and soil, and young trees became established along the walls.

= = Architecture = =

= = = General plan = = =

The inner enclosure measures approximately 49 metres from east to west and 31 metres at its widest point. Its irregular shape follows the edge of the rock spur. Curtain walls connect the gatehouse on the west with the residential tower at the eastern extremity.

The arrangement provided a compact court containing the principal domestic buildings. Structures were built against the walls wherever possible, leaving the central space available for circulation, deliveries and household work.

Surviving wall heights vary considerably. The northern curtain reaches almost 7 metres in places, while much of the southern side survives only slightly above foundation level. The original wall-walk has disappeared, although offsets and beam sockets indicate its position.

The outer enclosure occupies a broader terrace west of the ditch. Its boundary is marked by a low bank and discontinuous stone foundations. It probably accommodated livestock, workshops and buildings considered unsuitable for the restricted inner court.

= = = Gatehouse = = =

The gatehouse consists of two projecting towers flanking a narrow passage. Their outer faces are curved, while the elevations facing the courtyard are comparatively flat. This arrangement provided space beside the entrance without requiring fully cylindrical towers.

The passage was protected by timber doors and a portcullis. Grooves for the portcullis survive on either side, together with sockets associated with the door fittings. The evidence for an additional barrier at the inner end is less certain.

A chamber above the passage was reached by a stair within the southern tower. It contained a fireplace, a small window overlooking the approach and access to a latrine projecting beyond the curtain wall. These features indicate regular domestic use rather than purely defensive occupation.

The northern tower contained storage space at ground level. Its upper floor may have housed the porter or members of the constable’s household. Differences in floor levels and masonry joints show that the two towers were altered after their initial construction.

= = = Hall and residential tower = = =

The principal hall stood along the northern side of the courtyard. It was approximately 18 metres long and had a central hearth during its earlier phase. A passage at the western end separated the hall from service rooms.

Fragments of dressed stone suggest that the hall had relatively large windows facing the court. Openings in the outer wall were smaller and placed higher above ground level. The roof was supported by timber trusses resting on stone or timber corbels.

The eastern tower provided more private accommodation. Its lowest level was used for storage, with residential chambers above. A projecting latrine and the remains of a fireplace distinguish it from a tower designed solely for military purposes.

A short connecting passage may have allowed movement between the hall and tower without crossing the courtyard. The surviving evidence is incomplete because later stone removal affected this part of the site particularly heavily.

= = = Service buildings and water supply = = =

The kitchen occupied the south-eastern corner of the enclosure, separated from the hall by a small yard. Its foundations contain a broad hearth and the base of an oven. Repeated resurfacing indicates that the building remained in use through several phases of occupation.

A narrow room beside the kitchen has been identified as a pantry or dry store. Farther west, a rectangular building traditionally described as the chapel contained traces of painted plaster and an east-facing recess. These features support the identification, although no altar survives.

Water was collected from roofs and channelled towards a stone-lined cistern. A second tank near the eastern tower may have held water for kitchen use. No deep well has been identified within the enclosure.

The storage system depended on maintaining gutters, channels and impermeable linings. Repairs recorded in the medieval accounts suggest that leakage was a recurring problem. During dry periods, additional water probably had to be carried from springs below the spur.

= = Economy and daily life = =

The castle’s household was supported by a mixed farming economy. The valley floor provided meadow and arable land, while the higher slopes were used for cattle and sheep. Woodland supplied fuel, building timber and seasonal grazing for pigs.

Animal bones recovered from the site are dominated by cattle and sheep or goats. Pig, domestic fowl and small quantities of deer are also represented. The distribution of cuts suggests that animals were butchered locally and that the household consumed a range of meat rather than only selected joints.

Charred plant remains include oats, barley and smaller quantities of wheat. Oats were important both as food and as fodder for horses. Quern fragments show that some grain processing took place within or near the castle, despite the presence of an estate mill downstream.

Iron slag and hearth waste from the outer enclosure indicate small-scale smithing. This probably supported routine activities such as repairing tools, making fittings and shoeing horses. There is no evidence for substantial industrial production.

The material record shows differences in access to goods within the household. Most pottery was locally or regionally produced, while a small number of finer vessels probably arrived through wider trading networks.

= = Antiquarian interest and conservation = =

Castell Nantglas began to attract antiquarian attention during the eighteenth century. Early descriptions emphasised its isolated position and the vegetation covering its walls. Several drawings show architectural features that have since collapsed, although their proportions are not always reliable.

The first measured plan was prepared in 1847. It distinguished the main enclosure from the outer earthworks and identified the hall, gatehouse and eastern tower. Some interpretations, particularly the supposed location of a dungeon, were repeated in later guidebooks without supporting evidence.

Limited clearance and consolidation took place in the 1890s under the estate owner. Loose masonry was reset around the gatehouse, and rubble was removed from the entrance passage. These works improved access but were poorly recorded.

A more systematic conservation programme began in the twentieth century. Repairs used lime mortar, and unstable sections of wall were secured. Tree growth was reduced where roots threatened the masonry, while vegetation on the surrounding slopes was retained to limit erosion.

The castle remains a roofless ruin. Conservation is directed towards stabilising surviving fabric and managing drainage rather than reconstructing missing buildings.

= = Archaeological investigation = =

Archaeological work has consisted of measured surveys, limited excavation and the recording of masonry during repairs. Investigations in the inner court established the sequence from timber buildings to stone construction and identified changes in the arrangement of the service ranges.

Excavation near the gatehouse recovered pottery, iron nails, window glass and fragments of roofing material. Deposits associated with rebuilding contained both burned timber and reused stone, indicating that damaged material was incorporated into later surfaces.

Survey of the outer enclosure identified building platforms and a possible track leading towards the former ford. Geophysical investigation suggested additional structures, although shallow bedrock complicated interpretation.

Several aspects of the castle remain uncertain, including the original height of the eastern tower and the extent of the earliest enclosure. The surviving buildings nevertheless provide evidence for the gradual adaptation of a small Welsh lordly residence from a fortified household into an estate centre and, eventually, a group of agricultural buildings.
