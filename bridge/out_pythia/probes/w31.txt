The Harrowfield and Calder Valley Railway was a standard-gauge branch line in northern England which ran for 23 miles (37 km) from a junction with the main line at Harrowfield to the market town of Calderbridge, following the course of the River Calden for most of its length. The line was promoted by a local company in the 1860s, opened in stages between 1871 and 1874, and was absorbed into a larger railway company in 1892. Passenger services were withdrawn in 1962, and the last goods traffic ran in 1968. Parts of the trackbed now form the Calden Valley Way, a shared-use path, and a short section near Calderbridge is operated as a heritage railway.

= = History = =

= = = Origins and promotion = = =

The upper Calden valley was dominated in the first half of the nineteenth century by woollen manufacture, small-scale lead mining and limestone quarrying. Goods were carried to the canal head at Harrowfield by packhorse and, after 1824, along a turnpike road which was frequently impassable in winter. Several schemes to build a railway into the valley were put forward during the speculative period of the 1840s, including an 1845 proposal for a line continuing over the moors to the west coast, but none progressed beyond the preliminary survey.

Renewed interest followed the opening of the main line through Harrowfield in 1858. A meeting of mill owners and landowners held at the Black Bull Hotel in Calderbridge in October 1863 resolved to form a company to construct a branch, and the engineer Thomas Ashworth Pell was engaged to prepare plans. Pell's route kept to the valley floor wherever possible to avoid heavy earthworks, though this required eleven crossings of the river. The Harrowfield and Calder Valley Railway Act received royal assent on 29 June 1865, authorising share capital of £180,000 and borrowing of a further £60,000.

= = = Construction = = =

Raising the capital proved difficult following the financial crisis of 1866, and the first contract was not let until the spring of 1868. The contractors, Brierley and Hoyle of Bradford, began work at the Harrowfield end, where the line left the main line by a junction facing towards the north. The most substantial engineering work was the Scarth Viaduct, a structure of fourteen stone arches carrying the line 72 feet (22 m) above the Scarth Beck, and the 412-yard (377 m) Hollin Bank Tunnel, which was driven through shale and required extensive brick lining after a partial collapse in February 1870.

The section from Harrowfield to Netherby opened to goods on 3 April 1871 and to passengers on 1 June of the same year. Progress beyond Netherby was delayed by a dispute with the owner of the Ravensholme estate, who objected to the line passing within sight of his house; the matter was resolved by a slight deviation and the construction of an ornamental screen wall, portions of which survive. The line reached Thornley Bridge in September 1872, and the final section to Calderbridge opened on 14 May 1874, the occasion being marked by a public holiday in the town and a procession led by the Calderbridge Brass Band.

= = = Independent operation = = =

The company owned four tank locomotives, built by Manning Wardle, and a modest stock of carriages and wagons, but from the outset the main-line company worked a share of the traffic under a running-powers agreement. Passenger services typically consisted of six trains each way on weekdays, with an additional market-day train on Thursdays. Goods traffic was more significant to the company's finances; by 1880 limestone from the quarries at Greystone Edge accounted for nearly half of all tonnage carried, and a 1¼-mile (2 km) mineral branch was opened to the quarries in 1879.

The company never paid a dividend on its ordinary shares higher than 2 per cent, and the cost of repairs to the Hollin Bank Tunnel, which suffered further water ingress in 1884 and 1887, placed it under considerable strain. Negotiations for sale began in 1889, and the line was transferred to the main-line company under an act of 1892, with shareholders receiving £62 of the purchaser's stock for every £100 held.

= = = Under the main-line company and grouping = = =

The new owners carried out a programme of improvements in the 1890s, including the replacement of the original timber platforms at several stations, the installation of interlocked signalling throughout, and the doubling of the line between Harrowfield and Netherby in 1898 to accommodate increasing suburban traffic. A steam railmotor service was introduced between Harrowfield and Netherby in 1905, serving two new halts, but it was withdrawn in 1917 as a wartime economy and not reinstated.

Following the Railways Act 1921, the line passed to one of the "Big Four" companies in 1923. The interwar period saw a gradual decline in the textile trade of the valley, and several mills closed during the depression of the early 1930s. Passenger numbers were sustained to some extent by excursion traffic, as the upper valley had become popular with walkers and cyclists, and on summer Sundays special trains ran from the industrial towns to the east. Bank holiday traffic at Calderbridge in 1937 was recorded as exceeding 4,000 passengers in a single day.

= = = Nationalisation and closure = = =

The line became part of British Railways in 1948. Diesel multiple units replaced steam on most passenger services in 1957, but this did little to reverse the fall in traffic caused by increasing car ownership and competing bus services along the valley road. The intermediate stations at Ravensholme and Lumb Mill closed in 1955.

A closure proposal for the passenger service was published in 1960. Objections were heard by the regional Transport Users' Consultative Committee at Calderbridge Town Hall in July 1961, where opponents emphasised the difficulty of winter road travel in the upper valley. The committee reported that some hardship would result, but the Minister of Transport consented to closure, and the last passenger train ran on 3 March 1962. A replacement bus service was introduced, operated by a local company under contract.

Goods services continued to Calderbridge until 1965, after which the line was cut back to the Greystone Edge quarry branch. Limestone traffic ended when the quarry operator switched to road haulage, and the last train ran on 27 September 1968. The track was lifted in 1969 and 1970, except for the section at Calderbridge, which was purchased by a preservation society.

= = Route = =

From Harrowfield the line diverged northwards from the main line at Harrowfield North Junction, about half a mile beyond the main station, and turned west to follow the southern bank of the River Calden. The first 4 miles (6.4 km) passed through the industrial outskirts of the town, with numerous sidings serving mills, a gasworks and a coal yard.

Beyond Netherby the valley narrows, and the line crossed the river five times in the next 3 miles (4.8 km) on short iron girder bridges. At Scarth the line crossed the tributary valley of the Scarth Beck on the viaduct, before entering Hollin Bank Tunnel. The ruling gradient of 1 in 68 was encountered on the climb from the tunnel's western portal to Thornley Bridge, and heavy goods trains frequently required banking assistance on this section.

West of Thornley Bridge the valley opens into a broad upland basin, and the line ran across open moorland pasture before descending gently into Calderbridge, where the terminus stood on the eastern edge of the town centre. The Greystone Edge mineral branch left the line at Greystone Junction, between Thornley Bridge and Calderbridge, and climbed steeply to the north to reach the quarry faces.

= = Stations = =

= = = Harrowfield = = =

Trains on the branch used a bay platform on the north side of the main-line station at Harrowfield, which had been built in 1858. A second bay was added in 1898 when services were increased. Following closure of the branch, the bays were filled in and the area was used for car parking. Harrowfield station remains open on the main line.

= = = Harrowfield Bridge Street = = =

Bridge Street was a halt opened with the railmotor service in 1905, located beneath a road overbridge in the industrial district of the town. It consisted of two short timber platforms reached by staircases from the road. It closed in 1917 and no trace remains.

= = = Calden Mills = = =

Calden Mills station opened with the line in 1871 to serve a cluster of worsted spinning mills and the workers' housing built around them. It had a single platform on the down side, with a brick building containing a booking office and waiting room, and a goods yard with a five-ton crane. A second platform was added when the line was doubled in 1898. The station closed with the passenger service in 1962; the building was converted to a dwelling in 1974 and is still occupied.

= = = Low Cross Halt = = =

Low Cross Halt, the second of the railmotor halts of 1905, served a residential area built on former farmland. Unlike Bridge Street it was retained after 1917 and became a regular stop for all trains, being upgraded with concrete platforms in 1938. It closed in 1962.

= = = Netherby = = =

Netherby was the largest intermediate station on the line and served as its temporary terminus between 1871 and 1872. The station had two platforms connected by a lattice footbridge, a substantial stone building in a plain Italianate style designed by Pell, a two-road engine shed and a turntable. The engine shed housed the branch locomotives until 1931, when it was closed and engines were provided from Harrowfield. Netherby was the end of the double-track section from 1898, and a signal box of 35 levers controlled the junction between single and double lines. The station building is now a community centre, and the platforms survive alongside the Calden Valley Way.

= = = Ravensholme = = =

Ravensholme was a small station built partly at the request of the owner of the Ravensholme estate, as a condition of the settlement of his objection to the line. It had a single platform and a building with an ornate bargeboarded gable, reflecting its status as the station serving the estate. Traffic was always light, and in 1935 it was reduced to an unstaffed halt. It closed on 12 September 1955. The building was demolished in 1963.

= = = Scarth = = =

Scarth station stood immediately east of the Scarth Viaduct and served the village of the same name, which lay in the tributary valley below. Passengers reached the station by a steep footpath with 112 steps, a feature that was frequently noted in guidebooks. The station had a single platform and a small goods siding used mainly for agricultural traffic and coal. It closed in 1962. The viaduct is a Grade II listed structure and now carries the Calden Valley Way.

= = = Lumb Mill = = =

Lumb Mill, west of Hollin Bank Tunnel, served a single large cotton mill and a small hamlet. It was opened in 1872 and had a timber platform and a waiting shelter. The mill closed in 1931, and the station's traffic declined to a handful of passengers each day; it closed in 1955 along with Ravensholme.

= = = Thornley Bridge = = =

Thornley Bridge was the principal crossing place on the single-track section beyond Netherby and had two platforms and a passing loop. The station was popular with walkers visiting Thornley Force, a waterfall about a mile to the south, and was frequently busy on summer weekends. The goods yard handled wool, livestock and coal, and a cattle dock was added in 1886 to serve the monthly livestock auction. The station building is now a café serving users of the Calden Valley Way.

= = = Greystone Junction = = =

Greystone Junction was not a passenger station but a signal box and set of exchange sidings where wagons from the quarry branch were marshalled into trains. The signal box, of the main-line company's standard brick-based design, was built in 1896 and remained in use until 1968. Its foundations are visible beside the path.

= = = Calderbridge = = =

The terminus at Calderbridge was the most elaborate building on the line. Designed by Pell and opened in 1874, it was built of local gritstone and had a single island platform partly covered by an iron and glass train shed, a goods warehouse, a cattle dock, a coal yard and a small engine shed. The train shed was removed in 1951 because of corrosion and replaced with a canopy. Following closure to passengers in 1962 and to goods in 1965, the station was bought in 1970 by the Calden Valley Railway Preservation Society, which restored the building and reopened it in 1976 as the headquarters of a heritage line.

= = Rolling stock and operation = =

The original company's four locomotives were 0-6-0 saddle tanks named Calden, Harrowfield, Scarth and Thornley. Two were sold in 1893 shortly after the takeover, and the others were scrapped in 1908 and 1911. Under the main-line company, services were usually worked by 0-6-2 and 2-4-2 tank engines, with heavier 0-6-0 tender engines used for the limestone trains. A two-car diesel multiple unit service was introduced in 1957 and worked the line until closure.

Passenger services reached their peak in 1910, when eighteen trains ran in each direction between Harrowfield and Netherby and nine continued to Calderbridge. Journey times for the full length of the line varied between 52 and 70 minutes depending on the number of stops. By the final timetable of 1961 the service had been reduced to seven trains each way, with none on Sundays outside the summer months.

= = Heritage railway = =

The Calden Valley Railway Preservation Society was founded in 1966 with the aim of saving the entire line, but lacked the funds to purchase more than the Calderbridge station site and the first 1½ miles (2.4 km) of track towards Greystone Junction. Services began on the restored section in 1976, initially with an industrial tank locomotive acquired from a local gasworks. The society extended the line to a new terminus at Greystone in 1989, giving a total running length of 2¼ miles (3.6 km).

The railway operates on weekends from Easter to October and on selected days in December, and carries around 30,000 passengers a year. Its collection includes five steam locomotives, two diesel shunters and a number of restored carriages, among them a six-wheeled composite carriage built in 1882 for the original company, believed to be the only surviving item of its rolling stock. Proposals to extend the line further towards Thornley Bridge have been discussed, but would require removal of the path from a section of the trackbed and have not progressed.

= = Calden Valley Way = =

Following the lifting of the track, the trackbed between Calden Mills and Thornley Bridge was acquired by the county council in stages between 1979 and 1986. It was resurfaced and opened as the Calden Valley Way, a path for walkers, cyclists and horse riders, in 1988. Hollin Bank Tunnel was reopened to path users in 2009 after a restoration project that included repairs to the brick lining and the installation of lighting. The path is approximately 15 miles (24 km) long and forms part of a regional cycle route.

= = Legacy = =

Several structures associated with the line have been given listed status, including the Scarth Viaduct, the station buildings at Netherby and Calderbridge, and the eastern portal of Hollin Bank Tunnel, which bears the company's carved coat of arms. The line has been the subject of a number of local histories, and a collection of its records, including the minute books of the original company, is held by the county record office at Harrowfield.
