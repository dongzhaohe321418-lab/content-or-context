= = 2034 South Pacific cyclone season = =

The 2034 South Pacific cyclone season was a slightly below-average tropical cyclone season in which eight tropical cyclones formed within the South Pacific Ocean basin, east of 160°E. The season officially ran from November 1, 2033, to April 30, 2034, although one tropical disturbance developed in late October and was included in the season's statistics. The strongest storm of the season was Severe Tropical Cyclone Iolani, which reached Category 4 intensity on the Australian tropical cyclone intensity scale near the southern Cook Islands in February. The deadliest system was Tropical Cyclone Davu, whose slow movement across Viti Levu in January produced prolonged flooding that killed eleven people in Fiji.

Within the basin, tropical cyclones were monitored by the Fiji Meteorological Service (FMS), which operates the Regional Specialized Meteorological Centre in Nadi, and by the Meteorological Service of New Zealand (MetService) for systems south of 25°S. The United States Joint Typhoon Warning Center (JTWC) also issued unofficial warnings for several systems. A total of nineteen tropical disturbances were tracked during the season, of which eleven became tropical depressions.

= = Seasonal forecasts = =

Ahead of the season, the FMS, the National Institute of Water and Atmospheric Research (NIWA) of New Zealand, and several other Pacific meteorological agencies issued the Southwest Pacific Tropical Cyclone Outlook in mid-October 2033. The outlook predicted between seven and ten named tropical cyclones, compared with a long-term average of approximately 8.5, and forecast that at least three would reach severe tropical cyclone status. The forecast was influenced by weak La Niña conditions in the central equatorial Pacific, which were expected to transition toward neutral conditions by early 2034.

Under these conditions, the outlook anticipated reduced activity east of the International Date Line and elevated risk for New Caledonia, Vanuatu, and Fiji. Island nations including Kiribati, Tuvalu, and the northern Cook Islands were assessed as having below-normal risk, while Tonga and Niue were placed in the normal-risk category. The FMS separately issued its own outlook, predicting six to eight tropical cyclones and noting that the South Pacific Convergence Zone was expected to lie slightly southwest of its climatological position.

In its post-season review, NIWA described the outlook as "largely accurate," noting that the number of named storms fell within the forecast range but that the season's most intense cyclone developed in a region assessed as having normal risk.

= = Seasonal summary = =

The season began early, with Tropical Disturbance 01F forming near Tuvalu on October 28, 2033, though the system dissipated within three days without significant development. Activity remained subdued throughout November, with only two weak disturbances observed. The first named storm, Tropical Cyclone Ana, developed on December 9 near the Solomon Islands before moving into the basin and affecting Vanuatu.

December and January were the most active months of the season, with five named storms forming during the period. Sea surface temperatures in the Coral Sea and around Fiji were between 0.5 and 1.0 °C above normal, supporting intensification. However, periods of strong vertical wind shear associated with an enhanced subtropical jet stream limited the strength of several systems, and three of the season's cyclones peaked at Category 1 intensity.

February was marked by Severe Tropical Cyclone Iolani, which formed from a monsoon trough extending eastward from Samoa. Iolani became the first Category 4 system in the basin since the 2031 season. Activity declined substantially afterward, with only one named storm forming in March and none in April. The final system of the season, Tropical Depression 19F, dissipated on April 14, 2034.

Overall, the season produced eight named storms, three severe tropical cyclones, and an accumulated cyclone energy (ACE) index of approximately 62 units, as estimated by the JTWC. Total damage across the basin was estimated at US$214 million, the majority of which was attributed to Cyclones Davu and Iolani. Fifteen deaths were directly or indirectly attributed to the season's systems.

= = Systems = =

= = = Tropical Cyclone Ana = = =

A tropical disturbance designated 04F formed on December 6 within an active monsoon trough to the east of the Solomon Islands. The system initially remained disorganized because of moderate wind shear, but convection consolidated near its center on December 8 as it drifted southeastward. The FMS upgraded the system to a tropical depression later that day, and the JTWC issued a Tropical Cyclone Formation Alert shortly afterward.

On December 9, the FMS reported that the system had developed gale-force winds and named it Ana. The cyclone tracked south-southeastward toward the northern islands of Vanuatu, reaching peak 10-minute sustained winds of 75 km/h (45 mph) and a minimum central pressure of 990 hPa (29.23 inHg). Ana passed between Espiritu Santo and Maewo on December 10, bringing heavy rainfall and minor coastal flooding. The Vanuatu Meteorology and Geo-Hazards Department issued a yellow alert for Sanma and Penama provinces.

Ana weakened as it encountered increasing shear and drier air on December 11, and it was downgraded to a tropical depression the following day before dissipating west of Fiji. Damage in Vanuatu was limited to crops, small structures, and footbridges, with losses estimated at 48 million vatu (US$400,000). No fatalities were reported.

= = = Tropical Cyclone Bale = = =

Tropical Disturbance 06F developed on December 17 approximately 300 km (185 mi) north of Wallis Island. Moving slowly southward, the disturbance strengthened into a tropical depression on December 19 and was named Bale on December 20 after scatterometer data revealed gale-force winds in its southern quadrant.

Bale remained a minimal tropical cyclone for most of its existence. Its low-level circulation was partially exposed for much of December 21 because of northwesterly shear. The system passed approximately 120 km (75 mi) west of Wallis Island on December 21, producing wind gusts of up to 85 km/h (53 mph) and about 140 mm (5.5 in) of rainfall. Bale transitioned into a subtropical low on December 23 north of Tonga and was last noted on December 25.

Minor damage occurred on Wallis and Futuna, including fallen breadfruit and coconut trees and disruption to electricity distribution. Authorities in the French territory activated their cyclone preparedness plan, and schools were closed for one day.

= = = Severe Tropical Cyclone Cerise = = =

Cerise originated from a tropical disturbance that formed near the Loyalty Islands on December 28. The system developed rapidly in a low-shear environment with sea surface temperatures of around 29 °C, becoming a tropical depression on December 29 and being named Cerise early on December 30. It became the season's first severe tropical cyclone later that day, reaching Category 3 intensity with 10-minute sustained winds of 130 km/h (80 mph) and a central pressure of 965 hPa (28.50 inHg).

Météo-France New Caledonia issued a pre-alert and subsequently a level 1 cyclone alert for the Loyalty Islands Province and the eastern coast of Grande Terre. However, Cerise turned toward the south-southeast on December 30, passing approximately 90 km (55 mi) east of Maré. The eye remained offshore, and the strongest winds stayed away from populated areas. Maré recorded sustained winds of 72 km/h (45 mph) and a peak gust of 109 km/h (68 mph).

Cerise weakened rapidly on December 31 as it moved over cooler waters and was declared extratropical on January 1, 2034, after which MetService monitored its remnants as they passed well north of New Zealand's North Island. Damage in New Caledonia was minor, consisting mainly of downed vegetation and localized power outages affecting approximately 2,300 customers.

= = = Tropical Cyclone Davu = = =

Tropical Cyclone Davu was the deadliest storm of the season. It originated from Tropical Disturbance 09F, which formed on January 8 about 400 km (250 mi) west of Rotuma. Steering currents were weak, and the disturbance meandered for several days while gradually organizing. The FMS upgraded it to a tropical depression on January 10 and named it Davu on January 11.

Davu reached its peak intensity on January 12 as a Category 2 tropical cyclone, with 10-minute sustained winds of 100 km/h (65 mph) and a minimum pressure of 978 hPa (28.88 inHg). A building subtropical ridge to the south caused the system to drift south-southeastward at speeds below 8 km/h (5 mph). Davu made landfall near Ba on the northwestern coast of Viti Levu late on January 13 and remained over or near the island for approximately 36 hours.

Rainfall totals on Viti Levu were exceptionally high. The FMS recorded 612 mm (24.1 in) at a station near Nadarivatu over a 72-hour period, while Nadi received 389 mm (15.3 in). The Ba, Nadi, and Sigatoka rivers all exceeded major flood levels, inundating parts of Ba, Nadi, and Lautoka. More than 7,400 people sought refuge in 186 evacuation centers. Landslides blocked sections of the Kings Road and the Queens Road, isolating several interior villages for up to a week.

Eleven people were killed in Fiji. Most deaths resulted from drowning in floodwaters, including four people whose vehicle was swept off a crossing near Tavua. Sugar cane farms in the Western Division suffered significant losses, and the Fiji Sugar Corporation reported that roughly 9 percent of the crop planted for the 2034 harvest was damaged. Total damage was estimated at F$186 million (US$83 million).

The Fijian government declared a state of natural disaster for the Western Division on January 14, which remained in effect for 30 days. Australia, New Zealand, and the European Union provided assistance, including emergency supplies and funding for water purification. Davu exited Viti Levu on January 15, weakened to a tropical depression, and dissipated near Kadavu on January 17.

= = = Tropical Cyclone Elisa = = =

Elisa developed from a tropical disturbance in the northern Coral Sea on January 19, initially within the Australian region before crossing 160°E on January 21. Upon entering the South Pacific basin, the system was named Elisa by the FMS as it reached Category 1 intensity. Elisa moved generally southeastward and remained over open water for its entire lifetime.

The cyclone attained peak 10-minute sustained winds of 85 km/h (50 mph) on January 22. Strong upper-level westerlies sheared the system on January 23, and it became post-tropical on January 24, south of New Caledonia. Elisa's remnants produced large swells along the southern coast of Grande Terre and the Isle of Pines, but no significant damage was reported.

= = = Tropical Cyclone Fetu = = =

Tropical Disturbance 11F formed on January 27 near the northern Cook Islands, in a region of the basin that had been assessed as having below-normal cyclone risk. The system was named Fetu on January 29 after strengthening into a Category 1 tropical cyclone approximately 250 km (155 mi) east of Pukapuka.

Fetu was a small cyclone, with gale-force winds extending no more than 60 km (37 mi) from its center. It reached peak sustained winds of 75 km/h (45 mph) on January 29 before moving southward into an area of strong shear. The cyclone weakened to a tropical depression on January 30 and dissipated on January 31. Fetu brought heavy rain to Suwarrow but caused no recorded damage.

= = = Severe Tropical Cyclone Iolani = = =

Severe Tropical Cyclone Iolani was the most intense tropical cyclone of the season. Its precursor, Tropical Disturbance 14F, formed on February 9 along a monsoon trough located northeast of Samoa. Favorable conditions, including low wind shear, strong upper-level outflow, and sea surface temperatures exceeding 30 °C, allowed the system to strengthen steadily. It was upgraded to a tropical depression on February 10 and named Iolani on February 11.

Iolani underwent a period of rapid intensification between February 12 and 13, with its 10-minute sustained winds increasing from 95 km/h (60 mph) to 175 km/h (110 mph) within 24 hours. A well-defined eye approximately 25 km (16 mi) in diameter appeared on satellite imagery. The FMS estimated that Iolani reached its peak intensity on February 14 as a Category 4 severe tropical cyclone with 10-minute sustained winds of 185 km/h (115 mph) and a minimum central pressure of 935 hPa (27.61 inHg). The JTWC assessed peak 1-minute sustained winds of 215 km/h (130 mph), equivalent to Category 4 on the Saffir–Simpson scale.

Initially moving southeastward, Iolani passed approximately 140 km (85 mi) east of Niue on February 13, producing tropical storm-force winds and heavy surf along the island's western coast. Several boat sheds and a section of the wharf at Alofi sustained damage. The cyclone then turned south-southeastward toward the southern Cook Islands, where the Cook Islands Meteorological Service issued hurricane warnings for Rarotonga, Mangaia, and Mauke.

The center of Iolani passed between Rarotonga and Mangaia late on February 14, with the eye coming within about 55 km (35 mi) of Mangaia. Wind gusts on Mangaia reached an estimated 190 km/h (120 mph) before the island's anemometer failed. Extensive damage occurred to houses, community halls, and the island's taro plantations. On Rarotonga, a storm surge of approximately 1.4 m (4.6 ft) combined with large waves to flood low-lying areas of Avarua, damaging shops and the harbor. Power was lost to most of Rarotonga for up to four days.

Two people died in the Cook Islands: a man who was struck by debris on Mangaia and a woman who suffered a fatal medical emergency during evacuation on Rarotonga. Damage across the Cook Islands was estimated at NZ$162 million (US$97 million), making Iolani one of the costliest cyclones in the nation's recorded history. New Zealand deployed a Royal New Zealand Air Force C-130J and a naval vessel carrying relief supplies, engineers, and generators.

After passing the southern Cook Islands, Iolani accelerated southeastward and began weakening as it encountered cooler waters and increasing shear. It transitioned into an extratropical cyclone on February 17 and was last tracked by MetService on February 19 well southeast of the Kermadec Islands.

= = = Severe Tropical Cyclone Jone = = =

The final named storm of the season, Jone, developed from Tropical Disturbance 17F, which formed northeast of Vanuatu on March 6. The system strengthened gradually and was named Jone on March 8 while located near Tafea Province, Vanuatu. It moved southeastward, passing south of Aneityum on March 9.

Jone intensified into a Category 3 severe tropical cyclone on March 10 while over open water between Vanuatu and Fiji, attaining peak 10-minute sustained winds of 120 km/h (75 mph) and a minimum central pressure of 970 hPa (28.64 inHg). The cyclone then curved southward and passed west of Tonga's ʻEua and Tongatapu groups on March 11, bringing heavy rain and gusty winds. The Tonga Meteorological Service recorded gusts of 98 km/h (61 mph) at Fuaʻamotu International Airport.

In Tonga, Jone caused moderate damage to crops, particularly bananas and root vegetables, and brought down several power lines. One person drowned after being swept from rocks along Tongatapu's southern coast. Jone weakened over cooler waters on March 12 and became extratropical the following day.

= = = Other systems = = =

Several tropical disturbances and depressions did not develop into named tropical cyclones. Tropical Disturbance 01F formed near Tuvalu in late October 2033 and dissipated on October 31. Tropical Depression 05F, which formed on December 13 near Samoa, produced heavy rainfall over Upolu and Savaiʻi, with Apia recording 210 mm (8.3 in) of rain in 24 hours. Flash flooding damaged several roads, and one man died after being swept away by a stream near Faleolo.

Tropical Depression 15F developed between Fiji and Tonga on February 21 and briefly produced gale-force winds in its southern semicircle, though the FMS assessed that its circulation was too asymmetric to qualify as a tropical cyclone. Tropical Depression 19F, the season's final system, formed near Tokelau on April 10 and dissipated on April 14 without affecting land.

= = Storm names = =

Within the South Pacific basin, a tropical depression is named when it is judged to have 10-minute sustained winds of at least 65 km/h (40 mph) and gale-force winds occurring at least halfway around its center. Names are assigned by the FMS for systems between the equator and 25°S, and by MetService for systems south of 25°S. The names used during the 2034 season were drawn from List B of the regional naming lists. The names Ana, Bale, Cerise, Davu, Elisa, Fetu, Iolani, and Jone were used, with the names between Fetu and Iolani on the list skipped in accordance with an administrative adjustment made by the World Meteorological Organization's RA V Tropical Cyclone Committee earlier in the decade.

= = = Retirement = = =

At its biennial meeting in 2035, the RA V Tropical Cyclone Committee retired the names Davu and Iolani from the naming lists because of the loss of life and damage they caused in Fiji and the Cook Islands, respectively. They were replaced with Delana and Ivoni.

= = Season effects = =

The following summary describes the effects of the season's tropical cyclones. Davu was the costliest and deadliest system in the basin, causing eleven deaths and US$83 million in damage in Fiji. Iolani caused two deaths and US$97 million in damage, concentrated in the southern Cook Islands and Niue, and was responsible for the largest share of the season's economic losses. Jone caused one death in Tonga, and Tropical Depression 05F caused one death in Samoa. Ana, Bale, and Cerise caused minor damage in Vanuatu, Wallis and Futuna, and New Caledonia, respectively, while Elisa and Fetu caused no significant damage.

In total, fifteen deaths were associated with the season's systems, and damage was estimated at US$214 million. Following the season, the governments of Fiji and the Cook Islands commissioned independent reviews of their disaster response procedures. The Fijian review recommended improved flood early-warning systems along the Ba and Nadi river catchments, while the Cook Islands review led to revisions of building codes for coastal structures on Rarotonga.
