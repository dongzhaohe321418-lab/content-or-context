The Harbor at Midnight

The Harbor at Midnight is a 1954 American film noir crime drama directed by Leonard Halvorsen and starring Frank Delacourt, Marian Shaw, and Victor Krell. Produced by Meridian Pictures and released through Allied Artists Distribution, the film follows a disgraced former longshoreman who becomes entangled in a smuggling operation on the San Pedro waterfront after the disappearance of his younger brother. The screenplay was written by Ruth Abernathy and Samuel Pell, adapted from Pell's 1951 novel Night Tide at Terminal Island.

The film was shot largely on location in the Port of Los Angeles over a period of thirty-one days in the autumn of 1953. It was noted at the time for its use of real dockworkers as extras and for its extensive night photography by cinematographer Arthur Lindqvist. Initial reviews were mixed, with several critics praising the visual style while faulting the pacing of the film's second act. The film earned a modest profit on its release and was largely forgotten until the 1970s, when it was reassessed by film historians as a late example of the location-shot noir cycle. In 2009 it was restored by the UCLA Film and Television Archive.

= = Plot = =

Eddie Moran (Frank Delacourt) is a former longshoreman who was blacklisted from the San Pedro docks three years earlier after testifying against a corrupt union official. He now works as a night clerk at a run-down hotel near the waterfront. When his younger brother Tommy (Russell Payne), who still works on the docks, fails to return home after a night shift, Eddie begins to search for him.

Eddie's inquiries lead him to Lorraine Vance (Marian Shaw), a singer at a dockside bar called the Anchor Room, who had been seeing Tommy. Lorraine is reluctant to help, but eventually tells Eddie that Tommy had recently come into money and had been meeting with a man named Carl Sorensen (Victor Krell), a shipping agent with ties to an import firm. Eddie confronts Sorensen, who denies knowing Tommy and warns Eddie to stay away from the harbor.

Over the following nights, Eddie discovers that Sorensen is using a legitimate freight business to bring narcotics into the port aboard Pacific cargo ships. Tommy had been paid to look the other way during unloading but had begun to keep a ledger of shipments, intending to go to the authorities. Eddie also learns that Lieutenant Frank Dorsey (Harold Teague) of the harbor police is on Sorensen's payroll, leaving him with no one to trust.

With Lorraine's help, Eddie locates Tommy's ledger hidden in a locker at the union hall. Sorensen's men abduct Lorraine and offer to trade her for the book. The exchange is arranged at a deserted cannery on Terminal Island. During the confrontation, Eddie learns that Tommy was killed on the night of his disappearance and that his body was disposed of in the harbor. A gunfight follows among the cannery's fish tanks and conveyor lines, in which Dorsey is killed and Sorensen is wounded. Sorensen flees onto the deck of a departing freighter, where Eddie pursues him. Sorensen falls into the cargo hold and dies.

In the final scene, Eddie delivers the ledger to a federal investigator, Paul Hendricks (George Arliss Moore). Hendricks tells Eddie that the union has agreed to reinstate him. Eddie walks along the dock at dawn with Lorraine, looking out at the ships, before the film ends.

= = Production = =

= = = Development = = =

Samuel Pell's novel Night Tide at Terminal Island was published by Graystone Press in March 1951. Pell had worked briefly as a reporter for a Long Beach newspaper and drew on his coverage of waterfront labor disputes in the late 1940s. The novel sold moderately well and received favorable notices for its depiction of dock life. Meridian Pictures purchased the film rights in the summer of 1952 for a reported $12,500.

Meridian was a small independent company founded in 1949 by producer Walter Ainsley, who had previously worked as an associate producer at Columbia. Ainsley had produced two low-budget crime films for the company, and he intended The Harbor at Midnight to be a more ambitious project. He initially approached director Robert Kessler, who declined, and then offered the film to Leonard Halvorsen, who had directed several documentary shorts for the United States Navy during the Second World War and one feature, the 1950 western Dust on the Mesa.

Pell was hired to write the screenplay but had no experience in screenwriting, and Ainsley brought in Ruth Abernathy to collaborate with him. Abernathy had written dialogue for several radio dramas and had a co-writing credit on the 1952 film The Locked Room. According to later interviews, Abernathy was responsible for restructuring the novel, which was narrated in flashback, into a linear story. She also expanded the role of Lorraine Vance, who appears in only two chapters of the book.

= = = Censorship = = =

The first draft of the screenplay was submitted to the Production Code Administration in April 1953. The PCA objected to the depiction of narcotics, which was prohibited under the Production Code at the time, as well as to the portrayal of a police officer as corrupt without a clear indication that he would be punished. In response, the writers changed the smuggled cargo in several scenes to be described only as "contraband," although dialogue elsewhere in the film makes clear that the goods are drugs. The PCA also requested that the character of Lorraine be shown as having no romantic relationship with Eddie before the final scene, and that a line suggesting Mrs. Kowalski was running an unlicensed rooming house be removed. A revised script was approved in July 1953.

= = = Casting = = =

Frank Delacourt had been a contract player at Paramount through the late 1940s, appearing mostly in supporting roles. The Harbor at Midnight was his first leading role in a feature. Halvorsen later said he chose Delacourt because "he looked like a man who had carried something heavy for a long time." Delacourt reportedly spent two weeks working alongside longshoremen in San Pedro before filming began.

Marian Shaw was cast after Ainsley saw her performance in a stage production of The Petrified Forest in Pasadena. She had appeared in only one film previously, in a small part. Shaw performed her own songs in the film, including "Low Water," written for the production by composer Daniel Ostrow and lyricist Bea Hollins.

Victor Krell, a Danish-born actor who had emigrated to the United States in 1938, was cast as Sorensen. Krell had played villains in several Republic Pictures serials and later remarked that the role was "the first time I was allowed to be a bad man who also wore a good suit."

= = = Filming = = =

Principal photography began on October 5, 1953, and concluded on November 12, 1953. Approximately two-thirds of the film was shot on location in San Pedro, Wilmington, and Terminal Island, with interiors filmed at the Motion Picture Center studio in Hollywood. The production obtained permission from the Port of Los Angeles to film on active wharves, but shooting was restricted to nighttime hours and weekends to avoid disrupting cargo operations.

The cannery sequence was filmed over four nights at a fish processing plant on Terminal Island that had closed earlier that year. The production built additional catwalks and lighting rigs inside the building. Several local residents who had worked at the plant were hired as extras for the opening portion of the sequence, which depicts the cannery in operation.

Cinematographer Arthur Lindqvist shot the film in black and white using fast film stock and a limited number of portable lights, which allowed the crew to work quickly in confined spaces. Lindqvist made use of existing light sources on the docks, including sodium lamps and ship floodlights, and he later described the harbor as "a set that someone else had already lit." Some reviewers noted the grainy texture of the night scenes, which Lindqvist attributed to the need to push the film during development.

Filming was interrupted for three days in late October when heavy fog made exterior photography impossible. Halvorsen used the time to rehearse the final confrontation aboard the freighter, which was staged on a cargo ship, the SS Carolyn Hale, docked at Berth 154. The scene in which Sorensen falls into the hold was performed by stuntman Ray Corliss, who fell onto a padded platform constructed below the frame line.

The film's budget was approximately $385,000, which was considered moderate for an independent production of the period. The production went roughly $30,000 over budget, largely because of the weather delays and the cost of additional night shoots.

= = = Music = = =

The score was composed by Daniel Ostrow, who had written music for radio and for a number of industrial films. Ostrow used a small ensemble dominated by woodwinds, muted brass, and piano, and avoided the large orchestral style common in studio films of the era. The main theme is introduced by a solo clarinet over the opening credits, which are shown over footage of the harbor at night. The song "Low Water" is performed twice by Shaw in the film, once in full at the Anchor Room and once in a shortened reprise. A recording of the song by Shaw was released as a single by Coral Records in 1954 but did not chart.

= = Release = =

The Harbor at Midnight premiered at the Fox Theatre in San Pedro on March 18, 1954, with members of the cast and several local union officials in attendance. It opened in Los Angeles on March 24 and in New York at the Globe Theatre on April 9. The film was distributed in the United Kingdom by Eros Films under the title Midnight Harbour in September 1954.

Allied Artists promoted the film with posters emphasizing its location photography, using the tagline "Filmed where it happened — on the waterfront at night." Some newspaper advertisements also featured a photograph of Marian Shaw that did not appear in the film, which drew complaints from Halvorsen.

= = = Box office = = =

The film earned an estimated $910,000 in North American rentals during its initial release, according to trade publication figures. It performed better in West Coast port cities than elsewhere, and was held over for a third week in Seattle and San Francisco. Meridian Pictures reported a profit of approximately $160,000 on the film, making it the company's most successful production.

= = Reception = =

= = = Contemporary reviews = = =

Critical reaction on the film's release was mixed. The Los Angeles Examiner praised the "authentic grime" of the harbor scenes and called Delacourt's performance "solid and unshowy." Variety described the film as "a competent programmer elevated by its camerawork," but felt that the middle section, in which Eddie investigates Sorensen's business, "moves at the speed of a tugboat against the tide."

The New York Times reviewer wrote that the film "makes a good deal of its real locations and very little of its story," and singled out Krell as "the only character who appears to be enjoying himself." Several reviewers commented favorably on Marian Shaw, with one critic in the Hollywood Reporter calling her "a singer of genuine smoky appeal and an actress of some promise."

Some labor publications responded to the film's depiction of the waterfront. The ILWU newspaper The Dispatcher praised the casting of union members and the film's portrayal of dockworkers as ordinary people, while criticizing the suggestion that union halls were vulnerable to criminal influence. Conversely, a reviewer in a business-oriented trade magazine described the film as "unduly sympathetic to the longshoreman's grievances."

= = = Comparison with On the Waterfront = = =

The film was released several months before Elia Kazan's On the Waterfront, which opened in July 1954 and dealt with corruption on the New York docks. Although the two films were developed independently, critics at the time frequently compared them, typically to the disadvantage of The Harbor at Midnight. Halvorsen expressed frustration with the comparisons in a 1955 interview, noting that his film had been completed first. Later historians have pointed out that the two films differ considerably in tone and theme, with The Harbor at Midnight functioning primarily as a crime thriller rather than a social drama.

= = = Later assessment = = =

The film was rarely screened after its initial run and was shown occasionally on local television stations in the late 1950s and 1960s. Interest in it revived in the 1970s, when film scholars began to examine low-budget and independent crime films of the postwar period. A 1976 essay by critic Harold Breen in the journal Film Quarterly Review described the film as "one of the last and most underrated of the location noirs," and argued that its depiction of the Los Angeles harbor deserved comparison with the New York street photography of the late 1940s.

Lindqvist's cinematography has received particular attention in later criticism. Historians have cited the film's use of existing light and its long night tracking shots along the wharves as examples of the techniques that independent productions used to compensate for limited budgets. The cannery sequence is frequently cited as the film's highlight.

Writer Ruth Abernathy's contribution has also been reassessed. In a 1988 interview, she stated that she had been responsible for much of the film's dialogue but that her name had been placed second in the credits at Pell's insistence. Some scholars have noted that the character of Lorraine is more fully developed than many female characters in comparable crime films of the time, and have attributed this to Abernathy's revisions.

= = Restoration and home media = =

For several decades the film was available only in poor-quality 16 mm television prints. The original camera negative was believed lost after Meridian Pictures ceased operations in 1961. In 2004, a fine-grain master positive was discovered among materials donated to the UCLA Film and Television Archive by the estate of producer Walter Ainsley.

The archive completed a restoration in 2009, funded in part by a grant from a film preservation foundation. The restored version premiered at the UCLA Festival of Preservation in March 2009, with Marian Shaw, then 81 years old, in attendance. The restoration reinstated approximately four minutes of footage that had been cut from television prints, including part of the Anchor Room performance and a scene between Eddie and Mrs. Kowalski.

The restored film was released on DVD in 2011 and on Blu-ray in 2017, the latter including an audio commentary by a film historian, a short documentary on the location shoot, and a selection of Lindqvist's production photographs.

= = Legacy = =

The Harbor at Midnight was the most commercially successful film produced by Meridian Pictures. Following its release, Halvorsen directed three more features, including the 1956 crime drama The Long Shift, before moving into television, where he directed episodes of several anthology and western series. Frank Delacourt continued to play leading roles in lower-budget films through the late 1950s, and later became a familiar character actor in television. Marian Shaw appeared in four more films before retiring from acting in 1960 to pursue a career as a nightclub singer.

The film has been screened in several retrospectives devoted to film noir and to Los Angeles on film. Its waterfront locations have since been significantly altered by the expansion of the port and the decline of the Terminal Island fishing industry, and historians of the region have cited the film as a visual record of the harbor in the early 1950s. The closed cannery used in the climactic sequence was demolished in 1967.

In 2014, a stage adaptation of the film, written by a Long Beach playwright, was produced by a community theater in San Pedro. The production used the original film's title and incorporated the song "Low Water."
