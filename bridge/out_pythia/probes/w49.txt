Kalymnae

Kalymnae (Ancient Greek: Καλύμναι, Kalymnai) was an ancient Greek city on the southern coast of the Thermaic Gulf, situated on a low limestone promontory between the mouths of two seasonal streams, the Helissos and the Pheron. Founded in the late 8th century BC by colonists from Chalcis in Euboea, the city became a modest but persistent regional centre whose economy rested on the export of salted fish, timber from the hinterland, and a distinctive grey-slipped pottery. It was a member of the Chalcidian League in the 4th century BC, was absorbed into the Macedonian kingdom under Philip II, and continued as a small civic community through the Hellenistic and Roman periods before being largely abandoned in the 6th century AD. The site was identified in 1887 and has been the subject of intermittent excavation since 1924.

= = Name = =

The name Kalymnae is attested in several forms. Inscriptions from the city itself consistently use the plural Καλύμναι, and the ethnic adjective appears as Καλυμναῖος (Kalymnaios). Literary sources occasionally give the singular Κάλυμνα, which has led some scholars to suggest that the plural form originally referred to a cluster of settlements on and around the promontory that were later unified. The etymology of the name is uncertain. An ancient folk explanation, preserved in a scholion on Lycophron, derived it from καλύπτω ("to cover, conceal"), claiming that the harbour was hidden from passing ships by the headland. Modern linguists generally regard the name as pre-Greek, comparing it to other toponyms in -mn- found across the Aegean, including the island of Kalymnos in the Dodecanese, with which the city has no known historical connection.

During the Roman period the city is referred to in Latin sources as Calymnae, and a 5th-century AD episcopal list gives the form Kalymnē.

= = Geography = =

The ancient city occupied a promontory roughly 1.2 kilometres long and 400 metres wide at its broadest point, rising to a maximum height of about 38 metres above sea level at its northern end, where the acropolis stood. The western side of the promontory sheltered a natural harbour, which was enclosed in the 5th century BC by a rubble mole. The eastern side was exposed to prevailing winds and was used chiefly as a beach for drawing up small fishing vessels.

Inland, the city controlled a coastal plain of approximately 60 square kilometres, bounded to the south by the foothills of the Kissos range. The plain was well suited to cereal cultivation and olive growing, while the wooded slopes of the hills provided pine and oak timber. Ancient authors note the abundance of tunny and mackerel in the waters off the promontory, a fact corroborated by the large number of fish-salting vats found in the lower town.

Silting from the Helissos gradually altered the coastline. By late antiquity the harbour had become partially landlocked, and today the ancient shoreline lies some 300 metres inland from the modern beach.

= = History = =

= = = Foundation = = =

According to a tradition recorded by the local historian Damokritos of Kalymnae (fl. 3rd century BC), whose work survives only in fragments, the city was founded by a band of Chalcidians led by an oikistes named Archelas. Damokritos dates the foundation to the generation after the establishment of Mende, which would place it in the final decades of the 8th century BC. Archaeological evidence broadly supports this chronology: the earliest Greek pottery on the site, consisting of Euboean skyphoi and amphora fragments, dates to around 720–700 BC.

Excavations beneath the later agora have also revealed traces of an earlier settlement of the Early Iron Age, with handmade pottery of local type and post-built houses. The relationship between the indigenous population and the Greek settlers is unclear. A burial ground in use from about 750 to 650 BC contains both cremations accompanied by Euboean vessels and inhumations with local bronze ornaments, which some archaeologists interpret as evidence of intermarriage or coexistence during the city's first century.

= = = Archaic period = = =

During the 7th and 6th centuries BC Kalymnae grew steadily. The city's first stone temple, dedicated to Apollo Delphinios, was built on the acropolis around 580 BC. At about the same time the city began minting silver coins on the Euboic standard, bearing a leaping dolphin on the obverse and an incuse square on the reverse. The dolphin remained the principal civic emblem throughout the city's history.

Little is known of the city's internal politics in this period. A fragmentary law inscribed on a bronze plaque, dated on letter forms to around 540 BC, regulates the distribution of land in the plain and refers to a council of "the Hundred" (οἱ ἑκατόν), suggesting an oligarchic constitution. A later tradition speaks of a tyrant named Hippokles who ruled briefly in the late 6th century and was expelled by the citizens, but the historicity of this figure has been questioned.

= = = Classical period = = =

During the Persian invasion of 480 BC Kalymnae, like most communities in the region, submitted to Xerxes and provided ships to the Persian fleet. After the Persian defeat the city joined the Delian League, appearing in the Athenian tribute lists from 454/3 BC onwards. Its assessment was initially set at one talent, later raised to one and a half talents in 438/7 BC, a figure that places it among the smaller tributary cities of the Thracian district.

At the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War Kalymnae remained loyal to Athens. In 424 BC, however, following the arrival of the Spartan general Brasidas in the north, a pro-Spartan faction seized control of the city and opened its gates to a Peloponnesian garrison. Thucydides does not mention the city by name, but an Athenian decree of 422 BC, partially preserved, records the restoration of "the exiles of the Kalymnaians" and the imposition of a garrison. The city remained within the Athenian sphere until the end of the war.

In the early 4th century BC Kalymnae joined the Chalcidian League centred on Olynthus. Its membership brought economic benefits, including shared access to timber resources and a common coinage, but also drew the city into the League's conflicts with Sparta. During the Spartan campaign against Olynthus in 382–379 BC, Kalymnae was briefly besieged; a defensive wall enclosing the lower town, whose foundations survive along much of its circuit, is generally dated to this period.

= = = Macedonian rule = = =

Following Philip II's destruction of Olynthus in 348 BC, Kalymnae surrendered to the Macedonians without resistance. Unlike several neighbouring cities, it was not razed, and its citizens were permitted to retain their lands. A number of Macedonian settlers were nevertheless granted estates in the plain, and a royal official known as the epistates is attested in the city by the late 4th century BC.

Under Cassander and his successors the city prospered modestly. The founding of Thessalonike in 315 BC drew trade toward the head of the gulf, but Kalymnae's fish-salting industry continued to supply both Macedonian markets and the wider Aegean. Several honorific decrees of the 3rd century BC record grants of proxenia to merchants from Rhodes, Delos, and Byzantium, indicating the extent of the city's commercial contacts.

= = = Roman period = = =

After the Roman victory at Pydna in 168 BC and the division of Macedonia into four republics, Kalymnae was assigned to the second district. When Macedonia became a Roman province in 146 BC, the city retained its civic institutions but paid tribute to Rome. It suffered during the incursions of Thracian tribes in the 1st century BC, and a decree of about 85 BC honours a citizen named Menekrates son of Aristion for financing the repair of the city walls and ransoming captives.

The early imperial period was one of renewed prosperity. The harbour mole was rebuilt, a small bath complex was constructed near the agora, and the temple of Apollo was restored with funds provided by a wealthy Roman resident, Gaius Vibius Rufus, whose dedicatory inscription survives. Latin inscriptions become increasingly common from the 2nd century AD, though Greek remained the dominant language of public life.

= = = Late antiquity and abandonment = = =

Christianity is attested at Kalymnae from the early 4th century. A bishop of Kalymnae, Eusebios, is recorded among the signatories of a provincial synod held at Thessalonica in 449, and a three-aisled basilica was built over the ruins of the agora in the late 5th century. Silting of the harbour, combined with Slavic and Avar raids in the late 6th century, led to the gradual decline of the city. Coin finds cease after the reign of Maurice (582–602), and the site appears to have been abandoned by the early 7th century. A small fortified hamlet occupied the acropolis intermittently during the Middle Byzantine period.

= = Government = =

The constitution of Kalymnae is known chiefly from inscriptions. In the Archaic period the city appears to have been governed by an oligarchic council of one hundred members. By the 4th century BC a more broadly based system was in place, with an assembly (ekklesia) of citizens, a council (boule), and annually elected magistrates. The chief officials were a board of three archons, one of whom gave his name to the year. Other attested offices include the tamias (treasurer), the agoranomoi (market overseers), and the limenophylakes (harbour guardians), the last of which reflects the importance of maritime trade to the city.

Citizens were divided into four tribes, named after Apollo, Poseidon, Herakles, and the local hero Archelas. Under Macedonian rule the royal epistates supervised the city's affairs, though he seems to have interfered relatively little in routine administration.

= = Economy = =

The economy of Kalymnae was based on three principal activities: fishing, agriculture, and forestry. The salting of tunny and mackerel was the city's most distinctive industry. More than forty rectangular salting vats, lined with waterproof mortar, have been excavated in the lower town near the eastern beach, and transport amphorae of a characteristic local type, with a short neck and a knobbed toe, have been found at sites throughout the northern Aegean and as far afield as the Black Sea.

Cereals, olives, and vines were cultivated in the coastal plain. Timber from the Kissos foothills was exported for shipbuilding, and a 4th-century BC decree of the Chalcidian League refers to the right of Kalymnae to sell pitch and oak beams to Athens.

The city was also known for a type of fine tableware covered in a lustrous grey slip, produced from the late 5th to the 2nd century BC. Kilns for its manufacture were discovered in 1962 outside the southern gate.

= = Religion = =

The principal deity of Kalymnae was Apollo Delphinios, whose temple stood on the acropolis. The cult was probably introduced by the Euboean founders and was associated with seafaring and the protection of the harbour. An annual festival, the Delphinia, included a procession from the harbour to the acropolis and a regatta in which crews from each of the four tribes competed.

Other cults attested in inscriptions include those of Poseidon, Demeter and Kore, Dionysos, and Artemis Agrotera. A small sanctuary of the Nymphs stood at a spring at the foot of the acropolis, and votive deposits there include numerous terracotta figurines of the 5th and 4th centuries BC. The oikistes Archelas received hero cult at a tomb near the agora, which was maintained into the Roman period. In the 2nd century AD a shrine of Isis and Sarapis was established near the harbour, reflecting the city's connections with Egyptian traders.

= = Archaeology = =

The site was first identified in 1887 by the French traveller Léon Marchais, who recognised the course of the city walls and copied several inscriptions. Systematic excavation began in 1924 under the direction of the Greek archaeologist Konstantinos Rhomaios-Pappas, who uncovered the temple of Apollo and part of the acropolis fortifications. Work was interrupted by the Second World War and resumed in 1958, when the Greek Archaeological Service, in collaboration with a team from a German university, began excavations in the lower town.

Major discoveries include the agora, a rectangular open space of about 70 by 45 metres bordered by stoas on three sides; the bouleuterion, a small rectilinear building with stone benches for around 200 people; the fish-salting complex; the pottery kilns; and a theatre built into the eastern slope of the acropolis, with an estimated capacity of 3,000 spectators. The Archaic cemetery northeast of the city has yielded more than 400 burials.

Underwater surveys conducted between 1991 and 1998 mapped the ancient harbour mole and recovered a cargo of Kalymnaean amphorae from a wreck dating to around 300 BC.

Finds from the site are displayed in the Archaeological Museum of Thessaloniki and in a small site museum opened in 2004. Among the most notable objects are a marble kouros of about 540 BC, found reused in the wall of the basilica, and a bronze plaque bearing the Archaic land law.

= = Notable people = =

Damokritos of Kalymnae (3rd century BC), local historian, author of a lost work on the history and antiquities of the city.

Philonides son of Kleon (4th century BC), athlete, victor in the stadion race at the Pythian Games, commemorated by a statue base found in the agora.

Menekrates son of Aristion (1st century BC), civic benefactor who financed the repair of the walls after the Thracian raids.

Eusebios (5th century AD), the only known bishop of the city.

= = Legacy = =

Kalymnae is of particular interest to historians as one of the few Euboean colonies in the north Aegean whose stratigraphy spans continuously from the Early Iron Age to late antiquity. Its fish-salting installations are among the earliest securely dated examples of the industry in the Greek world, and the distribution of its amphorae has been used to trace patterns of maritime trade in the region. The modern village of Paralia Kalymnis, located 2 kilometres to the south, takes its name from the ancient city.
