**Oregon Route 271**

Oregon Route 271 (OR 271), also known as the Calder Valley Highway, is a state highway in the U.S. state of Oregon. It runs 47.6 miles (76.6 km) from an intersection with U.S. Route 20 (US 20) near the unincorporated community of Harlow Junction in Linn County northeast to Oregon Route 22 (OR 22) at the city of Marrowfield in Marion County. The highway follows the Calder River for most of its length and links several small agricultural and timber communities in the western foothills of the Cascade Range with the larger towns of the Willamette Valley. The Oregon Department of Transportation (ODOT) maintains it, and it is designated in the state's internal highway numbering system as the Calder Valley Highway No. 217.

The route traces its origins to a wagon road built in the 1860s between farms in the lower Calder Valley and sawmills upstream. The state took over the corridor as a secondary highway in 1921. The highway was paved in stages between 1928 and 1941. It was rebuilt in the 1950s and 1960s, partly in connection with the construction of Halvorsen Dam. Route numbers were assigned to Oregon's named highways beginning in 2002, and the OR 271 designation was applied in 2004. Average daily traffic ranges from about 1,900 vehicles on the most remote section to more than 14,000 vehicles near Marrowfield.

= = Route description = =

OR 271 begins at a signalized intersection with US 20 about 2 miles (3.2 km) east of Harlow Junction. This point lies in the transition between the flat farmland of the southern Willamette Valley and the first ridges of the Cascade foothills. The highway heads north-northeast as a two-lane road through grass seed fields and hazelnut orchards. After about 3 miles (4.8 km), it crosses Tenmile Creek on a concrete girder bridge built in 1964 and enters the small community of Ostrander. There it passes a grain elevator, a volunteer fire station, and the former Ostrander School, which is now used as a community hall.

North of Ostrander the road climbs gradually onto a terrace above the Calder River. It meets the river at the community of Pell's Ferry, named for a ferry that operated until the first bridge was built in 1889. The highway does not cross the river here. It turns east and follows the south bank upstream through a valley that narrows steadily. Between Pell's Ferry and the town of Wendle, the road alternates between open pasture and stands of second-growth Douglas fir. Several turnouts offer access to the river, which is popular with anglers for its winter steelhead run.

Wendle, at milepost 14, is the largest community on the southern half of the route, with a population of 1,312 at the 2020 census. In town the highway is called Main Street. It passes the Wendle Historic Commercial District, a row of brick and frame storefronts from the early 1900s. At the east end of town, OR 271 crosses the Calder River on the Wendle Bridge, a steel truss span completed in 1937 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places in 2005. Past the bridge, Linn County Road 614 (Upper Calder Road) branches off to the southeast toward recreation sites in the Willamette National Forest.

The highway continues northeast along the north bank of the river through the Calder Narrows. This is a 6-mile (9.7 km) section where the valley closes into a basalt canyon. The road runs on a shelf cut into the canyon wall, with tight curves, a posted advisory speed of 35 miles per hour (56 km/h) on several bends, and a 1,060-foot (320 m) tunnel known as the Narrows Tunnel. The tunnel is the only one on a state highway in Linn County. It was bored in 1939 and lengthened at its north portal in 1958 after repeated rockfalls. Rockfall fencing and catchment ditches line much of the canyon, and ODOT closes the section several times a year for slides, mostly in winter.

The highway leaves the canyon at Halvorsen Lake, a reservoir formed by Halvorsen Dam on the Calder River. OR 271 skirts the western shore for about 4 miles (6.4 km) and passes the Halvorsen Lake State Recreation Area, which has a boat ramp, a campground, and a day-use area. Near the north end of the reservoir the highway crosses into Marion County. Before the dam was built, the highway ran along the valley floor. The present alignment sits 120 to 180 feet (37 to 55 m) above that original grade, and segments of the old road can still be seen when the reservoir is drawn down in late summer.

Beyond the reservoir the route bends north. It climbs over Bassett Ridge through a broad saddle at an elevation of 1,842 feet (561 m), the highest point on the highway. A truck climbing lane serves northbound traffic on the approach to the summit. On the north side of the ridge, the road descends through timberland managed by the Oregon Department of Forestry and private timber companies. It then enters the valley of Silver Fork Creek, a tributary of the North Santiam River.

The final 9 miles (14 km) of the highway pass through rolling farmland and rural residential development. Traffic volumes rise steadily toward Marrowfield. About 2 miles (3.2 km) south of town, the highway widens to four lanes with a center turn lane, a section built in 1998. It enters Marrowfield as Calder Avenue, passes the Marrowfield campus of Chemeketa Community College and a shopping center, and ends at a signalized intersection with OR 22 on the west side of downtown. Calder Avenue continues north of the intersection as a city street.

= = History = =

= = = Early roads = = =

The lower Calder Valley was settled by Euro-American farmers in the late 1840s and 1850s. Most arrived over the Oregon Trail and filed claims under the Donation Land Claim Act of 1850. Early travel through the valley used informal tracks along the river terraces. In 1864 the Linn County Court authorized a county road from the Harlow area to a sawmill operated by brothers Elias and Thomas Wendle near the site of the present town. This Wendle Road was surveyed by county surveyor J. M. Oakes. It followed roughly the same line as the modern highway between Ostrander and Wendle.

Above Wendle, the canyon of the Calder Narrows blocked through travel for decades. A pack trail crossed Bassett Ridge to the north. It was used mainly by sheepherders moving flocks to summer range in the Cascades and by miners bound for the short-lived Silver Fork gold district in the 1870s. In 1882 a private company, the Calder & Santiam Wagon Road Company, received a charter from the state legislature to build a toll road connecting the Calder Valley with the Santiam country. The company graded a narrow road over Bassett Ridge by 1886. It avoided the canyon by climbing well above it on the western slopes. Tolls were collected at a gate near the present site of Halvorsen Dam until 1904, when Linn and Marion counties jointly bought the road and made it free.

= = = State highway = = =

The Oregon State Highway Commission was created in 1913, and the legislature passed a series of highway bills over the following decade. In 1921 an act designated the route from Harlow to Marrowfield by way of Wendle as a secondary state highway. It received the name Calder Valley Highway and the number 217 in the state's internal system. At the time it was a mostly unsurfaced road. Some sections near the towns were graveled. During the wet season, the climb over Bassett Ridge was frequently impassable to automobiles.

The commission's first major improvement was a regrading and graveling of the section between Harlow Junction and Wendle, completed in 1924. Paving followed in stages. The 14 miles (23 km) from US 20 to Wendle received an oil-macadam surface in 1928. The section from Marrowfield to the foot of Bassett Ridge was paved in 1931. In 1934 the Highway Commission adopted a plan to replace the old toll-road grade above Wendle with a new alignment through the Calder Narrows. Engineers estimated that the canyon route would cut about 4 miles (6.4 km) and more than 600 feet (180 m) of climbing from the trip.

Construction in the canyon began in 1936. Federal relief funds paid for part of the work, which drew heavily on labor organized through the Works Progress Administration. The new Wendle Bridge opened in 1937. The Narrows Tunnel was holed through in late 1938, and the canyon section opened to traffic in October 1939. A dedication ceremony at the tunnel's south portal was attended by Governor Charles A. Sprague. The paving of the remaining gravel sections over Bassett Ridge was finished in 1941. For the first time, a hard-surfaced road ran the full length of the valley.

= = = Halvorsen Dam and realignment = = =

After severe floods on the Calder River in 1945 and 1948, the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers studied a flood-control dam on the river as part of its broader program for the Willamette basin. Congress authorized Halvorsen Dam in 1950. The project was named for Nils Halvorsen, a local farmer and county commissioner who had campaigned for flood control after losing much of his property in the 1945 flood. The proposed reservoir would flood about 7 miles (11 km) of the Calder Valley Highway, as well as the small settlement of Bridgeton.

The Corps of Engineers paid for a replacement highway on higher ground along the western side of the reservoir. Construction of the relocated road ran from 1952 to 1955 and included two new bridges over side creeks and extensive cuts and fills. The new alignment opened in September 1955. Dam construction was completed in 1957, and the reservoir filled for the first time in the spring of 1958. Bridgeton's remaining residents were relocated, and several of its buildings were moved to Wendle and Marrowfield. Halvorsen Lake State Recreation Area opened on the reservoir's western shore in 1960, built on land transferred from the Corps.

Around the same time, the state began a broader modernization of the highway. In 1958 ODOT's predecessor, the Oregon State Highway Department, extended the north portal of the Narrows Tunnel by 140 feet (43 m). A rock slide in January 1956 had buried the original portal and closed the canyon for 23 days. Between 1961 and 1966 the department widened and resurfaced most of the route south of Wendle, replaced several timber bridges with concrete structures, and eased curves near Ostrander and Pell's Ferry.

= = = Later developments = = =

The decline of the timber industry in the 1980s hit the communities of the upper Calder Valley hard. Wendle's two remaining sawmills closed in 1983 and 1991, and heavy log-truck traffic on the highway dropped considerably. At the same time, residential growth around Marrowfield raised traffic on the northern end of the route. In 1998 ODOT widened the 2-mile (3.2 km) section entering Marrowfield to four lanes and added a signal at the intersection with Silver Fork Road.

In February 1996, flooding across western Oregon damaged several sections of the highway. A large landslide near milepost 22 destroyed about 400 feet (120 m) of roadway in the Calder Narrows. The canyon section was closed for nearly five months, and traffic was detoured over county roads that roughly follow the old toll-road route. ODOT rebuilt the damaged section on a retaining wall anchored into bedrock and reopened it in July 1996. The closure led the agency to begin a long-term rockfall mitigation program in the canyon. The program has installed draped mesh, rock bolts, and catchment fences at more than 30 sites.

The Oregon Transportation Commission began assigning signed route numbers to named highways that lacked them in 2002. The Calder Valley Highway was signed as OR 271 in 2004. The number was chosen because it was unused and fell within the range ODOT had set aside for routes in the region. The Wendle Bridge was rehabilitated between 2011 and 2013. Its steel truss was retained, while the deck was replaced and the substructure strengthened against earthquakes. Wendle residents had opposed an earlier proposal to replace the bridge entirely.

In 2019 ODOT completed a safety project on the Bassett Ridge section. The project added the northbound truck climbing lane, widened shoulders, and installed centerline rumble strips. Further studies of the canyon section have considered a second tunnel or a long bridge to bypass the most slide-prone stretch. As of 2024, no construction funding had been allocated.

= = Traffic and safety = =

According to ODOT's 2022 traffic count data, the busiest section of OR 271 is the four-lane segment in Marrowfield, which carried an average of 14,300 vehicles per day. The lowest volumes are on the canyon and reservoir sections between Wendle and Bassett Ridge, which averaged about 1,900 vehicles per day. Summer traffic on that stretch can more than double because of visitors to Halvorsen Lake. Trucks make up about 11 percent of traffic on the route as a whole.

The Calder Narrows section has a crash rate well above the statewide average for rural two-lane highways. Most collisions there involve single vehicles leaving the road on curves, along with collisions with fallen rock. ODOT installed variable message signs at each end of the canyon in 2008 to warn drivers of closures and hazards. Commercial vehicles longer than 65 feet (20 m) are advised to use alternate routes because of the tight curves and the limited clearance in the Narrows Tunnel, which is 14 feet 6 inches (4.42 m) high at the edges of the travel lanes.

= = Communities = =

The highway serves the following communities, from south to north:

Harlow Junction (unincorporated), at the southern terminus area
Ostrander (unincorporated)
Pell's Ferry (unincorporated)
Wendle (city)
Marrowfield (city), at the northern terminus

The former settlement of Bridgeton was located on the old highway alignment. Its site now lies beneath Halvorsen Lake.
