**Sestianus** (Latin: *Gaius Aelius Valerius Sestianus Augustus*; c. 197 – 14 October 251) was Roman emperor from 246 to 251. A provincial equestrian from Pannonia who rose through the officer ranks of the Danubian legions, he was proclaimed emperor by the troops of Moesia Superior after the death of his predecessor and was confirmed by the Senate several months later. His five-year reign fell during the period of political and military instability later called the Crisis of the Third Century. Ancient sources credit him with a temporary stabilization of the Danubian frontier, a reform of the provincial grain levy, and an attempted restoration of the silver coinage. He died at Viminacium, apparently of illness, during preparations for a campaign against the Carpi, and was succeeded by his son-in-law, Lucius Domitius Faustinus.

Sestianus is known chiefly from the fragmentary *Breviarium* of Annius Maximus, a short biography in a lost section of a fourth-century imperial history preserved in Byzantine excerpts, and a substantial body of coinage, inscriptions and papyri. Modern historians generally consider him a capable but conventional soldier-emperor whose reforms were undone within a decade of his death.

= = Early life = =

Sestianus was born around 197 in or near Mursa (modern Osijek, Croatia), in the province of Pannonia Inferior. The *Breviarium* says that his father, Valerius Sestus, was a veteran of the Legio II Adiutrix who had received land near the Drava river on discharge, and that his mother, Aelia Prisca, came from a family of local magistrates. The cognomen Sestianus, derived from his father's name, was the one by which he was commonly known; the *nomen* Aelius likely came through his mother's family, whose ancestors had probably received citizenship under Hadrian.

Little is known of his childhood. A later tradition that he worked as a herdsman before enlisting is regarded by most scholars as a literary commonplace borrowed from accounts of other soldier-emperors. A funerary inscription found at Mursa in 1911, commemorating a "Valeria Sesti filia," has been tentatively identified as belonging to his sister.

= = Military career = =

Sestianus enlisted in the Legio II Adiutrix at Aquincum around 215. An altar dedicated to Jupiter Dolichenus at Aquincum, dated to 229, names a *centurio* of that legion called G. Aelius Valerius Sestianus, which is generally accepted as the earliest firm evidence of his career. He is thought to have served in the Persian campaign of Severus Alexander in 231–233, as the *Breviarium* records that he "learned the arts of siegecraft beneath the walls of the Persians."

Under Maximinus Thrax, Sestianus advanced to *primus pilus*, the senior centurion of a legion, and on that basis entered the equestrian order. During the civil conflict of 238 he seems to have stayed loyal to Maximinus until the emperor's murder at Aquileia, after which he submitted to the senatorial regime. This did not apparently harm his prospects. By 240 he was *praefectus castrorum* of the Legio VII Claudia at Viminacium, and in the early 240s he commanded a combined vexillation of Danubian troops against the Carpi and Goths in Lower Moesia.

In 244 or 245 he was appointed *dux* with overall authority over the forces of Moesia Superior and Dacia. This post was an extraordinary command of a type that became common later in the century, but it was still unusual at the time. An inscription from Ratiaria calls him *vir perfectissimus* and *dux per Moesiam et Daciam*, which would make him one of the earliest attested holders of such a title.

= = Accession = =

The circumstances of Sestianus's proclamation are poorly documented. According to the *Breviarium*, the previous emperor died in the spring of 246 "neither by the sword nor by poison, but by the failing of his strength." The news reached the Danube at a time when the troops were owed a donative and were alarmed by Carpian raids across the river. On the urging of his officers, Sestianus was hailed as emperor at Viminacium, probably in late May or early June 246.

At least two other claimants are attested. Titus Clodius Varro, governor of Syria Coele, was proclaimed by his legions at about the same time; his coinage, struck at Antioch, survives in very small numbers. A second, otherwise unknown claimant named Marinus is mentioned briefly in a papyrus from Oxyrhynchus, which dates a contract "in the first year of Marinus" and then strikes the name out. Varro was killed by his own troops in the autumn of 246, reportedly after news arrived that the Danubian army was marching east. Marinus disappears from the record.

The Senate formally conferred the imperial titles on Sestianus in September 246, after negotiations carried out by his envoy, the senator Quintus Anicius Lepidus. The emperor did not travel to Rome until the following spring. His *dies imperii* was officially reckoned from his acclamation at Viminacium rather than from the senatorial decree, a practice that some historians see as an early sign of the declining constitutional role of the Senate.

= = Reign = =

= = = Relations with the Senate = = =

Sestianus came to Rome in April 247 and stayed for about eight months, the longest period he spent in the capital. He held the consulship in 247 with Anicius Lepidus as his colleague, and again in 249. The *Breviarium* describes his behavior toward the Senate as "courteous but sparing," and says he attended its meetings regularly while in the city but gave senators few military commands. Of the twenty-one provincial governors known from his reign, thirteen were equestrians, a markedly higher proportion than under his predecessors.

He is credited with restoring a number of senatorial privileges suspended during earlier civil wars. These included the right of senators accused of capital crimes to be tried by their peers. A fragmentary *senatus consultum* from 247, preserved on a bronze tablet found at Ostia, records the Senate's thanks to the emperor for "the restoration of ancestral custom." Modern historians have argued that these gestures were mainly symbolic and that real power stayed with the emperor's equestrian officials and the army.

= = = Military campaigns = = =

The main concern of Sestianus's reign was the defense of the lower Danube. In 248 he led an expedition across the river against the Carpi. The campaign ended in a victory near the river Hierasus (the modern Siret), after which the emperor took the title *Carpicus Maximus*. Coins issued in 248 and 249 carry the legend VICTORIA CARPICA and show a Roman soldier standing over a captive.

The following year he rebuilt or strengthened a series of fortifications between Novae and Durostorum. Building inscriptions naming the emperor have been found at Sexaginta Prista, Iatrus and Transmarisca. Archaeological excavation at Iatrus in the 1960s found a phase of rebuilding in the late 240s, including a new gateway with projecting U-shaped towers. Some scholars have linked this to the emperor's programme, although the dating is not certain.

In the east, Sestianus avoided direct conflict with Sasanian Persia. A treaty concluded in 248 is mentioned in a single passage of the Byzantine chronicler George Syncellus. Under it, the Romans apparently agreed to pay an annual subsidy in exchange for Persian recognition of Roman control in upper Mesopotamia. The terms are unclear, and some historians have doubted that the treaty existed, suggesting that Syncellus confused it with agreements from other reigns.

A revolt in Mauretania Caesariensis in 249–250, led by a tribal chief named Faraxen in some sources, was suppressed by the provincial procurator Marcus Iunius Celer without imperial involvement. It is mentioned in two inscriptions from Auzia.

= = = Fiscal and monetary policy = = =

Sestianus is best known to modern historians for his attempt to reform the silver coinage. During the preceding decades, the silver content of the *antoninianus*, the double-denarius coin introduced by Caracalla, had fallen sharply. Metallurgical analysis of coins from Sestianus's first issue (246–247) shows a silver content of about 38 percent. Coins of his "reformed" issue of 248, distinguished by a new radiate portrait and the reverse legend RESTITVTOR MONETAE, contain between 46 and 49 percent silver, a substantial rise.

The reform seems to have been funded partly by plunder from the Carpian campaign and partly by the recall of older, debased coins for reminting. A papyrus from Panopolis dated to 249 records a provincial official exchanging "old coins" at the treasury at a fixed rate. The improvement did not last. Coins struck in the last year of his reign show a return to about 40 percent silver, and under his successor the decline continued.

The emperor also reorganized the collection of the *annona militaris*, the levy of food and supplies for the army. An edict of 249, known from a copy preserved in the Theodosian Code and attributed there to "the deified Sestianus," required that requisitions be recorded by provincial officials and credited against a landowner's tax liability. Earlier practice had allowed troops to requisition supplies more or less at will. Historians have described the edict as an important step toward the regular tax-in-kind system later formalized under Diocletian, although its practical effect in the 250s is uncertain.

= = = Religious policy = = =

There is no evidence that Sestianus carried out any general persecution of Christians. Eusebius of Caesarea does not mention him among the persecuting emperors. A letter of Dionysius of Alexandria refers to "the peace under Sestianus," which implies that Christians were left largely undisturbed during his reign.

The emperor was a known devotee of Jupiter Dolichenus, a cult popular among soldiers in the Danubian provinces. He also favored Sol Invictus. Coins from 250 show the sun god on the reverse with the legend SOLI COMITI AVG ("to the Sun, companion of the Emperor"). A temple to Sol at Viminacium was dedicated in his name, according to an inscription now in the National Museum of Serbia, Belgrade.

= = = Building projects = = =

Outside the frontier fortifications, Sestianus's building activity was limited. In Rome he restored the Porticus of Octavia after a fire. He also completed a bath complex on the Esquiline begun by an earlier emperor, which was afterwards known as the *Thermae Sestianae*. Only foundations of this complex survive, beneath buildings on the modern Via Merulana. At Mursa, his birthplace, he granted the status of *colonia* and paid for a new aqueduct, parts of which were excavated in 1979.

= = Family = =

Sestianus married Ulpia Marcella, apparently the daughter of an equestrian officer from Sirmium, before 225. She received the title *Augusta* in 247 and appears on coinage with the reverse legends PIETAS AVGVSTAE and IVNO CONSERVATRIX. She outlived her husband and is last attested in 253.

The couple had at least two children. A son, Gaius Aelius Valerius Marcellus, was named *Caesar* in 248 but died in 249, possibly at about twenty years of age. Commemorative coins struck after his death bear the legend DIVO MARCELLO and an image of a funeral pyre. A daughter, Aelia Sestia, married Lucius Domitius Faustinus, a senior officer on her father's staff. After Marcellus's death Faustinus became the emperor's most likely successor, although he was never formally given the title of Caesar.

= = Death and succession = =

In the summer of 251 Sestianus gathered an army at Viminacium for a new expedition against the Carpi and their Gothic allies, who had crossed the Danube in force earlier that year. The *Breviarium* says that the emperor fell ill "with a fever of the bowels" in early October, and died on 14 October after ten days of illness. Writing more than a century later, the historian Aurelius Victor states briefly that he died "of sickness, which was then rare for an emperor." No ancient source suggests foul play, and most modern historians accept a natural death. Some have proposed that the illness was an early case of the epidemic later called the Plague of Cyprian, which spread through the empire during the 250s.

The army at Viminacium proclaimed Faustinus emperor within days. The Senate confirmed him in November and deified Sestianus as *Divus Sestianus*. His ashes were carried to Rome and placed, according to the *Breviarium*, in the Mausoleum of Hadrian, although no inscription confirming this has been found. Faustinus continued the Carpian campaign but was killed in battle in 253, and the dynasty ended with him.

= = Sources = =

The principal literary source for Sestianus's reign is the *Breviarium*, a collection of short imperial biographies attributed in the manuscript tradition to an otherwise unknown Annius Maximus. It survives only in excerpts in the tenth-century *Excerpta de virtutibus et vitiis* compiled for Constantine VII Porphyrogenitus. The author appears to have drawn on a lost Latin history of the third century, sometimes identified with the hypothetical *Enmannsche Kaisergeschichte*. Its account of Sestianus is about 1,800 words long and is generally regarded as more reliable than its treatment of other emperors of the period, perhaps because its source had access to Danubian military records.

Shorter notices appear in Aurelius Victor, Eutropius, the *Epitome de Caesaribus*, Zosimus and George Syncellus. These are often inconsistent. Eutropius gives the reign as "four years and some months," while the *Epitome* says "six years." The chronology accepted by most modern scholars depends mainly on the evidence of coinage and dated papyri from Egypt.

The *Historia Augusta*, which might be expected to include a life of Sestianus, has a gap in the manuscripts that covers the years 244 to 253. Whether this gap is accidental or was part of the original composition remains disputed.

= = Coinage = =

Coins of Sestianus were struck at Rome, Viminacium, Antioch, and, from 249, a new mint at Siscia. The Siscia mint is thought to have been opened to pay the Danubian armies more directly. Sestianus's coinage is relatively common, and several large hoards have been found, most notably the Brigetio hoard (1934), which contained over 4,200 *antoniniani* of the reign.

His portraits show a broad-faced, close-bearded man with short hair, following the military style of portraiture common under Maximinus Thrax and his successors. Coins of the reformed issue of 248 show a noticeably older face with heavier lines, which some numismatists interpret as a deliberate effort to project gravity and experience.

Provincial bronze coinage was struck in his name by more than sixty cities in the eastern provinces. This was among the last reigns in which such civic coinage was produced on a large scale.

= = Legacy and historiography = =

Ancient writers generally regarded Sestianus favorably. Aurelius Victor calls him "rough in learning but just in judgment," and the *Breviarium* praises his "frugality and diligence." He is often grouped with the other Illyrian soldier-emperors of the later third century, although he came to power about two decades before the best known of them.

Edward Gibbon, in *The History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire* (1776), gave Sestianus a single paragraph, describing him as "an honest soldier whose virtues were insufficient to the times." Nineteenth-century historians paid more attention to his monetary reform, which Theodor Mommsen called "the last serious attempt to save the silver currency before Aurelian."

More recent scholarship has emphasized the administrative side of his reign. In *Soldiers and Tax-Gatherers: The Danubian Emperors, 235–284* (1998), the historian Miriam Hollander argued that his *annona* edict and his reliance on equestrian governors foreshadowed the reforms of Diocletian, and that his reign should be understood as an early stage in the transformation of the Roman state rather than as a failed restoration. Other historians, notably Karl-Heinz Ebner, have cautioned that the evidence for these reforms is thin and that their attribution to Sestianus may partly reflect later legal compilers giving older measures to a well-regarded emperor.

A marble head in the Archaeological Museum of Zagreb, found near Osijek in 1887, has been identified as a portrait of Sestianus on the basis of its resemblance to his coin portraits. The identification is widely but not universally accepted.
