= Matthias Lorenz Strehlow =

Matthias Lorenz Strehlow (14 March 1811 – 2 November 1879) was a Württemberg lawyer, journalist and liberal politician who served as Minister of Finance of the Kingdom of Württemberg from 1864 to 1870. He was a member of the Württemberg Chamber of Deputies for nearly three decades and represented the district of Esslingen in the Frankfurt National Assembly during the revolutionary period of 1848–1849. Strehlow was associated with the moderate wing of southern German liberalism. He was known for his advocacy of railway expansion, the reform of municipal finance and the gradual integration of the south German states into a broader German customs and monetary framework. Later in his career he became a cautious supporter of union with the North German Confederation, a position that made him unpopular with both the democratic left and the Catholic and particularist right in Württemberg.

= = Early life and education = =

Strehlow was born in Esslingen am Neckar, the second of five children of Johann Georg Strehlow, a cloth merchant, and his wife Christiane Friederike, née Haug, the daughter of a Protestant pastor from Nürtingen. The family belonged to the prosperous commercial class of the town. The elder Strehlow served for several years on the Esslingen town council and had a reputation as a careful but conservative businessman.

Strehlow attended the Latin school in Esslingen and later the Eberhard-Ludwigs-Gymnasium in Stuttgart, where he boarded with an uncle. In 1829 he enrolled at the University of Tübingen to study law and cameral science. At Tübingen he joined a student association with liberal sympathies and came into contact with several men who would later become prominent in Württemberg politics. He was briefly investigated by university authorities in 1832 following the Hambach Festival, when student societies throughout southern Germany came under suspicion, but no charges were brought against him. Contemporaries described him as studious and reserved rather than politically militant.

After passing the first state examination in law in 1834, Strehlow spent a year at the University of Heidelberg, where he attended lectures on political economy. He completed his practical training in the courts of Esslingen and Stuttgart and passed the second state examination in 1837.

= = Legal and journalistic career = =

In 1838 Strehlow established a legal practice in Esslingen. His clients were drawn largely from the town's merchants and manufacturers, and he acquired a specialist knowledge of commercial law, bankruptcy proceedings and disputes over guild privileges. Esslingen was at this time one of the more industrialised towns in Württemberg, with textile, metalworking and machine-building enterprises, and Strehlow's work brought him into close contact with the problems of early industrial enterprise in a largely agrarian state.

From 1840 Strehlow contributed regularly to the Stuttgart newspaper Der Beobachter and to several regional commercial papers. His articles, published at first anonymously and later under his own name, dealt chiefly with economic questions. He argued for the abolition of internal restrictions on trade, the reform of the guild system and greater public investment in transport. A series of essays published in 1843 under the title Über den Eisenbahnbau in Württemberg (On Railway Construction in Württemberg) attracted wide attention. In them Strehlow criticised what he regarded as the slow pace of the government's railway planning and argued that the state should build and operate a main line connecting Stuttgart with Ulm and the Bavarian border, as well as a line along the Neckar valley toward Heilbronn. Several of his proposals corresponded closely to routes later adopted by the state railway administration.

In 1844 Strehlow married Luise Emilie Kienlin, the daughter of a Heilbronn paper manufacturer. The marriage produced four children, three of whom survived to adulthood.

= = Early political career = =

= = = Chamber of Deputies = = =

Strehlow was first elected to the Württemberg Chamber of Deputies in 1845 as the representative of the Oberamt of Esslingen. He sat with the liberal opposition but quickly became known as a specialist in budgetary matters rather than as an orator on constitutional questions. He served on the chamber's finance committee from 1846 and was responsible for a detailed report on the state debt in that year, which criticised the government's practice of financing railway construction through short-term borrowing.

During the economic crisis of 1846–1847, which brought high food prices and widespread distress to Württemberg, Strehlow supported measures to suspend grain exports and to fund public works, but opposed proposals for state price controls, which he regarded as damaging to commerce. His position on these questions placed him among the moderate liberals and distinguished him from the more radical democrats who were gaining strength in the chamber.

= = = Revolution of 1848 = = =

Following the outbreak of revolution in France in February 1848 and the subsequent unrest in the German states, King William I of Württemberg dismissed his conservative ministry and appointed a liberal government in March 1848. Strehlow supported the new ministry and was offered a post as an under-secretary in the Ministry of Finance, which he declined, preferring to stand for election to the all-German parliament then being convened in Frankfurt.

In the elections of April 1848 Strehlow was returned as deputy for the Esslingen constituency to the Frankfurt National Assembly. In Frankfurt he joined the Württemberger Hof faction, a centre-left grouping, but transferred in the autumn of 1848 to the more moderate Casino faction, a change that was criticised by many of his constituents. He served on the Assembly's economic committee, where he worked on draft legislation concerning a unified German customs tariff, a common system of weights and measures and the regulation of navigation on the Rhine and its tributaries.

Strehlow was a supporter of the so-called "Lesser German" solution, which envisaged a German national state excluding the Austrian Empire and headed by the King of Prussia. This was an unusual position for a south German deputy, since opinion in Württemberg generally favoured the inclusion of Austria. He voted in March 1849 for the election of Frederick William IV of Prussia as German Emperor. After the king's refusal of the crown in April 1849, Strehlow withdrew from the Assembly together with many other moderate deputies and did not participate in the rump parliament that moved to Stuttgart in June 1849 and was dispersed by Württemberg troops.

His conduct in 1848–1849 damaged his standing among Württemberg democrats, who regarded his support for Prussian leadership and his withdrawal from the Assembly as a betrayal. In the democratic press he was frequently described as a "Prussian" or a "Gothaer", the latter term referring to the moderate liberals who met at Gotha in June 1849 to support the Prussian-led union project. Strehlow attended the Gotha meeting but did not play a prominent role.

= = Reaction period = =

The period following the suppression of the revolution saw the restoration of conservative government in Württemberg under the ministry of Joseph von Linden, which governed from 1850 to 1864. Strehlow lost his seat in the Chamber of Deputies in the elections of 1850, in which the democrats performed strongly, and was not re-elected until 1856.

During these years Strehlow concentrated on his legal practice and on business interests. He was a founding shareholder and from 1853 a member of the supervisory board of the Württembergische Notenbank, a private bank of issue, and he was involved in the establishment of a mutual fire insurance society for manufacturers in the Neckar district. He continued to write on economic subjects. His most substantial work from this period, Die Gemeindefinanzen in Württemberg (Municipal Finance in Württemberg, 1854), was a detailed study of the revenues and expenditures of Württemberg towns and villages, based in part on returns he had collected from municipal officials. The book argued that the existing system of local taxation, which relied heavily on levies on landed property, placed an excessive burden on agricultural communities while undertaxing growing commercial wealth. It recommended a reform of municipal taxation to include a local income tax. The work was well received by administrators and is still occasionally cited by historians of Württemberg local government.

After returning to the Chamber of Deputies in 1856, Strehlow adopted a conciliatory attitude toward the Linden government on economic matters, supporting its railway programme and its continued participation in the Zollverein, the Prussian-led customs union of which Württemberg had been a member since 1834. He opposed the government, however, on questions of press freedom and the rights of associations. During the Zollverein crisis of 1862–1864, when Prussia's commercial treaty with France threatened to split the customs union, Strehlow was among the most outspoken supporters in the chamber of accepting the Prussian position. He argued that Württemberg's economic interests depended on access to northern German markets and that an alternative customs union with Austria was impracticable. His speech on the subject in February 1863 was printed and widely distributed by commercial associations.

= = Minister of Finance = =

= = = Appointment = = =

The death of King William I in June 1864 and the accession of King Charles I brought a change of government. The Linden ministry was replaced by a cabinet under Karl von Varnbüler, who held the foreign ministry, and which sought to accommodate moderate liberal opinion. Strehlow was appointed Minister of Finance in September 1864. His appointment was regarded as a concession to the commercial and industrial interests of the Neckar region, and he was one of the few members of the cabinet who did not come from the higher civil service or the nobility. He was raised to the personal nobility in 1865 through the award of the Order of the Württemberg Crown, after which he was entitled to use the style "von Strehlow", though he rarely did so.

= = = Financial policy = = =

As finance minister Strehlow pursued a policy of fiscal caution combined with continued investment in infrastructure. He consolidated a number of short-term railway loans into a long-term state loan in 1865, reducing the state's interest costs, and introduced reforms to the presentation of the state budget intended to make the accounts of the railway administration more transparent to the chamber. He also oversaw a revision of the land tax cadastre, which had not been comprehensively updated since the 1820s.

His most significant legislative achievement was the Trade and Business Tax Act of 1866, which reformed the taxation of commercial and industrial enterprises. The act replaced a complex system of fixed levies, many of them based on guild classifications, with a tax assessed on the estimated yield of each enterprise. The reform was opposed by many small craftsmen, who feared higher assessments, and by some members of the chamber who objected to the powers it gave to tax assessors. It was passed after lengthy negotiations and several amendments. Strehlow's attempt to introduce a general state income tax, however, failed in 1868 in the face of opposition from both the landed interest and the democrats.

Strehlow also played a role in the monetary negotiations among the south German states. He represented Württemberg at conferences in Munich in 1865 and 1867 that discussed the coordination of coinage and the regulation of paper money issued by private banks. He was a proponent of the gold standard at a time when most German states were on silver, and his memoranda on the subject were later cited in the debates that preceded the introduction of the German mark in 1873.

= = = War of 1866 and its aftermath = = =

The outbreak of war between Prussia and Austria in June 1866 placed Strehlow in a difficult position. Württemberg, under Varnbüler's direction, sided with Austria and the German Confederation, and Strehlow was responsible for raising the funds required for mobilisation. He did so through an emergency loan and the temporary suspension of certain public works, but he was known to disapprove privately of the war. According to the later recollections of a fellow minister, he offered his resignation in June 1866, which the king declined.

The rapid Prussian victory and the subsequent peace settlement, which required Württemberg to pay an indemnity of eight million guilders and to conclude a secret offensive and defensive alliance with Prussia, strengthened the position of those in Württemberg who favoured closer ties with the north. Strehlow negotiated the financial terms of the indemnity and arranged its payment through a combination of a state loan and the sale of certain state properties.

In the years 1867–1870 Strehlow was the member of the cabinet most closely associated with the pro-Prussian position. He supported the reorganisation of the Zollverein in 1867, which created a customs parliament with deputies from both the North German Confederation and the south German states. This brought him into conflict with the Württemberg People's Party, the democratic party led by figures hostile to Prussia, which won a majority of Württemberg's seats in the customs parliament elections of 1868. Strehlow's ministry was attacked in the chamber for the cost of military reforms modelled on the Prussian system, which he defended as unavoidable under the terms of the alliance.

= = = Resignation = = =

Strehlow resigned as Minister of Finance in March 1870, officially on grounds of ill health. Historians have generally attributed his resignation to disagreements within the cabinet over military expenditure and to the declining support for the government in the chamber. He was succeeded by a senior civil servant from the finance ministry. Strehlow retained his seat in the Chamber of Deputies.

= = Later career = =

Following the outbreak of the Franco-Prussian War in July 1870, Strehlow spoke in the Chamber of Deputies in support of the war credits and of Württemberg's participation in the war alongside Prussia. In the autumn of 1870 he was among the Württemberg politicians who urged the government to negotiate entry into the new German Empire, and he voted in December 1870 in favour of the treaties of accession concluded at Versailles.

Strehlow was elected to the first Reichstag of the German Empire in March 1871 as deputy for a Württemberg constituency, sitting with the German Party, the Württemberg affiliate of the National Liberal Party. He served in the Reichstag until 1874. His activity there was confined largely to committee work on the coinage laws of 1871 and 1873 and on the law establishing the Reichsbank, though he left the Reichstag before the latter was passed in 1875. He spoke rarely in plenary sessions and was regarded by colleagues as an expert on monetary questions rather than as a party politician.

He remained a member of the Württemberg Chamber of Deputies until 1876, when he declined to stand for re-election. In his final years he lived in Esslingen, where he served as chairman of the local savings bank and of a society for the support of the town's technical school. He suffered from a heart ailment from the mid-1870s and died at his home in Esslingen on 2 November 1879. He was buried in the Ebershaldenfriedhof in Esslingen.

= = Political views = =

Strehlow belonged to the generation of south German liberals whose political outlook was formed in the 1830s and 1840s and who combined a commitment to constitutional government with an emphasis on economic development. He held that political freedom depended on the growth of an independent commercial and industrial middle class and that the fragmentation of Germany into numerous small states was an obstacle to economic progress. His support for Prussian leadership in Germany derived principally from economic considerations, in particular his conviction that Württemberg's prosperity depended on its membership in the Zollverein.

On constitutional questions Strehlow was moderate. He favoured a franchise restricted by property qualifications, opposed universal manhood suffrage until the 1860s and accepted the introduction of universal suffrage for the Reichstag in 1871 with some reluctance. He supported freedom of the press and of association but was critical of what he regarded as the demagogic tendencies of the democratic movement. In religious matters he was a liberal Protestant and favoured the separation of church administration from the state, though he took little part in the conflicts between the state and the Catholic Church in the 1870s.

= = Legacy and assessment = =

Strehlow's reputation declined after his death, in part because he was overshadowed by more prominent figures of the period and in part because his association with the pro-Prussian cause was viewed critically in Württemberg, where particularist sentiment remained strong. Nineteenth-century accounts written from a national liberal perspective presented him as a far-sighted advocate of German unity, while democratic and Catholic writers portrayed him as an opportunist who had abandoned the principles of 1848.

Twentieth-century historians have generally assessed him as a competent and effective administrator whose principal achievements lay in financial and economic policy. His reforms of business taxation and his management of the state debt have been described as contributing to the stability of Württemberg's public finances in the period before unification. His writings on municipal finance and railway policy are regarded as useful sources for the economic history of Württemberg in the mid-nineteenth century.

A street in Esslingen, the Strehlowstraße, was named after him in 1891. His papers, including correspondence with several leading south German liberals, are held in the Württemberg State Archives in Stuttgart.

**River Merrow**

The **River Merrow** is a river in the north of England, approximately 74 kilometres (46 mi) long, which rises on the eastern slopes of Fell Harrow in the uplands and flows generally south-east before entering the North Sea through a tidal estuary at Saltmouth. Its catchment covers about 610 square kilometres (236 sq mi) and includes upland moorland, mixed farmland and several small industrial towns. The principal settlements on its course are Holmcote, Brackenford, Ashby Weir, Callington and Saltmouth.

The river was historically important for milling, wool processing and, in its lower reaches, coastal trade. Navigation improvements made in the late 18th century allowed barges to reach Callington, although commercial traffic ceased in the 1930s. The river is now managed primarily for flood control, water supply and recreation.

= = Etymology = =

The name *Merrow* is first recorded in a charter of 1086 as *Meruwe*, and appears as *Merewe* and *Merowe* in documents of the 13th and 14th centuries. Most place-name scholars derive it from a Brittonic root related to Welsh *mêr*, meaning "marrow" or "soft substance", possibly a reference to the soft peat-stained water of the upper river. An alternative interpretation, proposed in a 1927 county survey, connects the name with Old English *mere*, "pool" or "lake", referring to the marshy ground near its source, but this derivation is considered less likely because of the early spellings.

= = Course = =

The Merrow rises at an elevation of about 540 metres (1,770 ft) in a series of springs and peat seepages known collectively as Merrow Head, on the eastern flank of Fell Harrow. The upper river, locally called Merrow Beck, descends steeply through a narrow valley for about 9 kilometres, passing several waterfalls, the largest of which, Gill Force, has a drop of 11 metres (36 ft).

Below the hamlet of Low Scarth the valley widens and the river is joined from the north by the Tarn Beck. It then flows through the town of Holmcote, where it is crossed by a 15th-century packhorse bridge. Between Holmcote and Brackenford the river meanders across a broad floodplain of alluvial meadows, receiving the River Sallow from the west near the village of Thornby.

At Brackenford the river passes through a short gorge cut in sandstone, known as the Brackenford Narrows, before entering the lower valley. The village of Ashby Weir takes its name from the masonry weir constructed there in the 17th century, which marks the former upper limit of navigation for small craft. From Ashby Weir the river flows past the market town of Callington, below which it becomes tidal. The final 8 kilometres form the Merrow Estuary, which widens to almost 1.5 kilometres at its mouth between Saltmouth and the sand spit of Gull Ness.

= = = Tributaries = = =

The principal tributaries of the Merrow, from source to mouth, are:

* Tarn Beck (left bank), draining Scarth Tarn
* Hollin Gill (right bank)
* River Sallow (right bank), the largest tributary, approximately 21 kilometres long
* Witherdale Beck (left bank)
* Cotter Brook (right bank), joining at Callington
* Fleet Water (left bank), entering the estuary near Saltmouth

= = Geology = =

The upper catchment is underlain by Carboniferous gritstone and shale, overlain by blanket peat, which gives the upper river its characteristic brown colouring. Below Holmcote the river crosses a band of Carboniferous limestone, and several springs along this section contribute calcium-rich water that noticeably alters the river's chemistry. The Brackenford Narrows are cut through a resistant bed of Permian sandstone, while the lower valley is floored with glacial till and post-glacial alluvium. The estuary contains extensive intertidal mudflats and salt marsh.

During the last glaciation the lower Merrow valley was occupied by ice, and a moraine near Callington is thought to have temporarily impounded a lake, whose former shoreline can be traced as a terrace on the northern side of the valley.

= = History = =

= = = Prehistory and Roman period = = =

Flint scatters of Mesolithic date have been recorded on terraces above the river near Thornby, and a Bronze Age burial cairn stands on the ridge above Holmcote. A Roman road from the coast to the upland forts crossed the Merrow at a ford near the present site of Brackenford; excavations in 1962 uncovered timber piles thought to belong to a Roman bridge. Coins and pottery found in the area date from the 2nd to the 4th century AD.

= = = Medieval period = = =

By the 11th century there were at least six water mills on the Merrow and its tributaries, recorded in the survey of 1086. In the 12th century the Cistercian abbey of St Mary at Witherdale was granted fishing rights on a stretch of the river and constructed fish weirs and a corn mill. The abbey also developed sheep farming in the upper catchment, and by the 14th century fulling mills had been established at Holmcote and Brackenford to process locally produced wool.

Callington received a market charter in 1251 and grew as a trading centre at the lowest bridging point of the river. Saltmouth developed as a fishing and salt-making settlement, with salt pans recorded on the estuary margins in the 13th century.

= = = Navigation = = =

Attempts to improve navigation on the lower Merrow began in the 17th century, when the weir at Ashby was rebuilt and a flash lock was installed to allow keels to pass. The Merrow Navigation Act of 1774 authorised the construction of three pound locks and the dredging of shoals between Saltmouth and Callington. The works were completed in 1779 at a cost of about £14,000. Coal, lime, grain and timber were the main cargoes, and annual tonnage reached a peak of approximately 46,000 tons in the 1830s.

The opening of the Saltmouth and Callington Railway in 1847 drew away much of the river's traffic. The navigation company was purchased by the railway in 1853, and maintenance declined thereafter. The last recorded commercial cargo, a load of roadstone, reached Callington in 1934. Two of the three locks survive in a derelict condition; the lock at Ashby Weir was restored as a heritage structure in 1998.

= = = Industrial period = = =

In the late 18th and 19th centuries water power on the Merrow supported cotton spinning, paper making and bobbin turning. Holmcote had four mills by 1820, the largest of which, Low Mill, employed about 300 workers at its peak. A paper mill operated at Brackenford from 1792 until 1971. Industrial effluent and untreated sewage caused serious pollution in the middle reaches during the 19th century, and a report of 1868 described the river below Brackenford as "devoid of fish for several miles".

= = = Floods = = =

The Merrow has a history of rapid flooding owing to the steep, impermeable upper catchment. Notable floods occurred in 1771, 1852, 1903, 1947 and 2009. The flood of November 1852 destroyed the medieval bridge at Brackenford and drowned eleven people. The 2009 flood, following more than 200 millimetres of rain in 48 hours, inundated around 380 properties in Holmcote and Callington and led to the construction of a flood defence scheme completed in 2014.

= = Hydrology = =

The mean flow of the Merrow measured at the gauging station at Ashby Weir is 14.2 cubic metres per second (500 cu ft/s). Flows are highly variable, with a recorded maximum of 412 cubic metres per second during the 2009 flood. Water is abstracted at Thornby for public supply, and the Scarth Tarn reservoir, created in 1896 by damming Tarn Beck, regulates part of the upper flow.

= = Ecology = =

Water quality improved substantially after the closure of the Brackenford paper mill and the upgrading of sewage treatment works in the 1970s and 1980s. Atlantic salmon, which had been absent for over a century, were recorded again in 1987, and fish passes were added to the weirs at Ashby and Holmcote in 2002. The river also supports brown trout, sea trout, grayling and European eel.

The upper river supports populations of white-clawed crayfish and freshwater pearl mussel, both of which are of conservation concern. Otters returned to the catchment in the 1990s. The Merrow Estuary is designated as a site of special scientific interest for its wintering wading birds, including knot, dunlin, bar-tailed godwit and curlew, and its salt marsh vegetation.

= = Settlements = =

= = = Holmcote = = =

**Holmcote** is a small town in the upper Merrow valley, with a population of about 4,900 at the 2021 census. The name derives from Old Norse *holmr*, "island in a river" or "water meadow", and Old English *cot*, "cottage". The town grew around a river crossing and, from the 14th century, a fulling mill. Its packhorse bridge, of five arches, dates from about 1460 and is a listed structure.

Holmcote expanded rapidly during the cotton spinning boom of the early 19th century, and many rows of stone workers' cottages survive from this period. Low Mill closed in 1958 and has since been converted into apartments and a small museum of local industry. The town now serves as a centre for walkers visiting the surrounding fells, and tourism is a significant part of the local economy. Holmcote hosts an annual sheep show held on the riverside meadows each September.

= = = Brackenford = = =

**Brackenford** is a town of about 8,200 inhabitants situated at the head of the Brackenford Narrows. The town developed at the ford used by the Roman road and was a significant wool processing centre in the medieval period. The present bridge, a three-arched stone structure, replaced the bridge destroyed in the flood of 1852 and was completed in 1856.

The paper mill, established in 1792 by the Lister family, was for much of its existence the town's largest employer, producing writing paper and later packaging board. Its closure in 1971 led to economic decline, and part of the site was later redeveloped as a business park. The Church of St Wilfrid contains a carved Anglo-Scandinavian cross shaft, indicating a religious site of pre-Conquest origin. Brackenford railway station lies on the line between Saltmouth and the upland towns.

= = = Ashby Weir = = =

**Ashby Weir** is a village and civil parish with a population of approximately 1,100. Originally known simply as Ashby, the village acquired its present name in the 18th century in reference to the weir and lock that marked the limit of navigation for larger vessels. The village contains a former wharf, a lock keeper's cottage and a public house, the Navigation Inn, which dates from 1781. The restored lock and a short section of towpath form part of the Merrow Valley Way, a long-distance footpath following the river from Holmcote to Saltmouth.

= = = Callington = = =

**Callington** is a market town with a population of around 15,600, making it the largest settlement on the river apart from Saltmouth. It lies at the lowest traditional bridging point of the Merrow and at the head of the tidal reach. The town received its market charter in 1251, and a weekly market continues to be held in the Market Place each Thursday.

During the 18th and 19th centuries Callington prospered as the inland terminus of the Merrow Navigation, with warehouses, maltings and a basin that remained in use until the 1930s. Several warehouses on Wharf Street have been converted into housing and studios. The town's Bridge Street crossing of the Merrow incorporates the medieval core of a 14th-century bridge widened in 1790 and 1924. Callington Grammar School, founded in 1563, is one of the older schools in the region. Following the 2009 flood, flood walls and demountable barriers were installed along the riverside.

= = = Saltmouth = = =

**Saltmouth** is a coastal town and port at the mouth of the Merrow, with a population of about 27,000. The town originated as a fishing settlement and centre of salt production, the salt being made by evaporating seawater in shallow pans heated with peat and, later, coal. Salt making declined in the 17th century due to competition from rock salt, but the harbour grew in importance as an outlet for coal and agricultural produce carried down the river.

A stone pier was built in 1712 and extended in 1840, and the arrival of the railway in 1847 led to the construction of a wet dock. Saltmouth developed as a seaside resort in the late 19th century, with a promenade and bandstand along the North Beach. The fishing fleet declined during the 20th century, and the dock now handles mainly leisure craft and small coastal vessels. The Gull Ness spit, across the estuary from the town, is a nature reserve managed for its bird populations and sand dune habitats.

= = Bridges and crossings = =

The Merrow is crossed by numerous bridges. Notable crossings include:

* Gill Force footbridge, a timber footbridge on the fell path below Merrow Head
* Holmcote Packhorse Bridge (c. 1460)
* Thornby Viaduct, a 12-arch railway viaduct completed in 1863
* Brackenford Bridge (1856)
* Ashby Lock footbridge (restored 1998)
* Callington Bridge (14th century, widened 1790 and 1924)
* Merrow Estuary Bridge, a concrete road bridge carrying the coastal bypass, opened in 1976

= = Recreation = =

The Merrow Valley Way, opened in 1991, is a 68-kilometre waymarked footpath following the river from its upper valley to the sea. Angling is popular throughout the river, with fishing rights held by several local angling associations. Canoeing is permitted on the lower river between Ashby Weir and Callington, and the Callington Regatta, first held in 1868, takes place annually in July. The estuary is used for sailing and birdwatching.

= = In culture = =

The river features in the work of the 19th-century landscape painter Thomas Ridsdale, whose painting *Evening on the Merrow* (1838) hangs in the Saltmouth Art Gallery. The poet Margaret Elland, who lived in Holmcote from 1902 until her death in 1949, wrote a sequence of poems titled *Merrow Songs* (1921) describing life in the upper valley. A local folk song, "The Keelman of the Merrow", recounts the life of a barge worker on the navigation and was collected by folklorists in the early 20th century.

= Night Freight =

Night Freight is the debut studio album by American rock and roll group the Calloway Brothers, released in March 1958 by Meridian Records. The album was recorded over four sessions between October and December 1957 at Sun Valley Recording Service in Memphis, Tennessee, and was produced by Harlan Pruitt. It contains twelve songs, seven of which were written or co-written by the group's lead vocalist and rhythm guitarist, Earl Calloway.

The album reached number 14 on the Billboard Best-Selling Pop LPs chart and produced two singles, "Midnight on the Wabash" and "Kerosene Kiss". The former became the group's only top-ten hit on the Billboard Hot 100 precursor charts. Although the Calloway Brothers did not sustain their commercial success into the 1960s, Night Freight has since been reassessed by critics and music historians as an early example of a rock and roll album conceived as a unified work rather than a collection of singles.

= = Background = =

The Calloway Brothers were formed in 1954 in Evansville, Indiana, by brothers Earl (vocals, rhythm guitar) and Dale Calloway (lead guitar), together with bassist Ronnie Fitch and drummer Walter "Buzz" Kemper. The group initially performed country and western swing material at dances and roadhouses along the Ohio River, gradually incorporating rhythm and blues songs into their sets after hearing records broadcast from stations in Nashville and Memphis.

In 1956 the group self-financed a single, "Ohio Line" backed with "Pretty Peggy", which was pressed in a run of 500 copies by a local printing firm. A copy reached Harlan Pruitt, a former radio announcer who had recently been hired as an artists and repertoire representative for Meridian Records, a Chicago-based label known chiefly for gospel and polka recordings. Pruitt saw the group perform at the Riverside Pavilion in Owensboro, Kentucky, in May 1957 and signed them to a two-year contract the following month.

Meridian's executives initially intended the group to record only singles, as was customary for rock and roll acts of the period. According to a 1979 interview with Pruitt, Earl Calloway had written a number of songs dealing with railroads, river towns and nighttime travel, and Pruitt persuaded the label that these could be sequenced as a long-playing record with a common theme. The label agreed on the condition that the album include at least two songs suitable for release as singles.

= = Recording = =

Recording took place at Sun Valley Recording Service, a small independent studio on Union Avenue in Memphis operated by engineer Thomas Ledbetter. Pruitt chose the studio because of its reputation for a pronounced room echo, which Ledbetter achieved by routing signals through a converted concrete storage room behind the control booth.

The first session, held on October 14, 1957, produced usable takes of "Midnight on the Wabash" and "Switchman's Lament". The group returned on November 2 and November 3 to record six further songs. Session logs held by the Memphis and Shelby County Room of the Memphis Public Library indicate that "Kerosene Kiss" required twenty-two takes, largely because of disagreements over the tempo of the bridge section.

For the final session on December 9, Pruitt hired pianist Odell Vance and tenor saxophonist Cleophus Grant, both of whom were regular session players at the studio. Vance played on four tracks, including "Coal Town Saturday", while Grant contributed the saxophone solo on "Lantern Swing". Dale Calloway later stated that the addition of piano and saxophone was intended to broaden the album's appeal to rhythm and blues audiences.

The album was recorded on a monaural Ampex 350 tape machine. No stereo version was produced at the time; a simulated stereo edition issued by Meridian in 1966 was poorly received and has since been withdrawn.

= = Music and lyrics = =

Night Freight combines elements of rockabilly, rhythm and blues and country music. Critics have noted the prominence of Dale Calloway's lead guitar, which makes frequent use of a tremolo effect produced by a DeArmond unit attached to his Gretsch hollow-body guitar. Kemper's drumming relies heavily on brushes and a shuffle rhythm, giving several songs a pace that writers have compared to the motion of a train.

Lyrically, the album is organized loosely around a single night. The opening track, "Sundown Whistle", describes a rail worker finishing his shift, while the closing track, "Daybreak in Terre Haute", depicts the same character arriving home at dawn. Intervening songs concern dance halls, a failed romance, a mining town and a riverboat. In his liner notes for the 1994 reissue, music historian Gerald Ashby described the record as "a travelogue of the lower Midwest compressed into thirty-one minutes".

Not all of the songs fit the narrative. "Kerosene Kiss" and "Tell Me Twice" were written separately as potential singles, and Earl Calloway acknowledged in later years that they were included at the label's insistence.

= = Release = =

Meridian released Night Freight on March 17, 1958, under catalogue number MLP-1104. The front cover featured a photograph by staff photographer Irene Szabo depicting the four members standing beside a Pennsylvania Railroad boxcar at a freight yard in Gary, Indiana. The back cover carried liner notes by Chicago disc jockey Sam "Daddy Sam" Holloway.

"Midnight on the Wabash" was issued as a single in January 1958, two months ahead of the album, and reached number 8 on the Billboard chart in March. "Kerosene Kiss" followed in May and peaked at number 31. The group promoted the album with a tour of the Midwest and South and appeared on two regional television programs, as well as one national broadcast, an appearance on a Saturday evening variety show in April 1958.

The album sold an estimated 180,000 copies in its first year, a substantial figure for a rock and roll LP at the time. It was deleted from the Meridian catalogue in 1963 after the label was acquired by a larger company.

= = Reception = =

Contemporary reviews were generally favorable. Billboard listed the album among its "Spotlight" selections, describing it as "a strong package with plenty of jukebox potential". Cash Box praised the guitar work but suggested that "the slower numbers could have been trimmed in favor of more of the rocking sides". A review in a Chicago daily newspaper called it "an unusually thoughtful record from a field not known for thought".

Later assessments have been more enthusiastic. In a 1987 survey of early rock albums, critic Marian Wexler wrote that Night Freight "anticipates by nearly a decade the idea that a rock record could tell a story from start to finish". A 2003 reference guide to American popular music awarded the album four and a half stars out of five, citing "Switchman's Lament" and "Coal Town Saturday" as highlights.

= = Legacy = =

The Calloway Brothers released a second album, Riverboat Ballroom, in 1959, which failed to chart. Dale Calloway left the group in 1960 to work as a session guitarist in Nashville, and the band dissolved in 1961. Earl Calloway later worked as a radio announcer in Indiana and occasionally performed with reconstituted versions of the group at revival concerts during the 1970s and 1980s.

Interest in Night Freight increased after several British musicians cited it as an influence in the late 1960s. The album was reissued on compact disc in 1994 by an archival label, with eight bonus tracks including alternate takes and both sides of the 1956 "Ohio Line" single. A remastered vinyl edition was released in 2018 to mark the album's sixtieth anniversary.

In 2009 the Indiana Historical Bureau placed a marker outside the former Riverside Pavilion site acknowledging the group's early performances there.

= Midnight on the Wabash =

"Midnight on the Wabash" is a song by American rock and roll group the Calloway Brothers, written by brothers Earl and Dale Calloway. It was released as a single by Meridian Records in January 1958 and later included as the second track on the group's debut album, Night Freight. The song reached number 8 on the Billboard chart and remains the group's best-known recording.

= = Composition = =

Earl Calloway wrote the song's lyrics in the summer of 1956 while working nights at a grain elevator near the Wabash River in southern Indiana. In a 1982 interview, he recalled watching freight trains cross a trestle bridge during his breaks and imagining "where everybody on board was headed". The lyrics describe a narrator waiting by the river for a lover who has promised to return on a late train.

Dale Calloway composed the song's guitar introduction, a descending figure played with heavy tremolo that recurs between verses. The song is in the key of E major and is performed at approximately 132 beats per minute. Its structure follows a verse–chorus pattern with an eight-bar instrumental break in place of a bridge.

= = Recording = =

The song was recorded on October 14, 1957, at Sun Valley Recording Service in Memphis. According to engineer Thomas Ledbetter, the released version was the ninth take. The handclaps heard during the instrumental break were added by producer Harlan Pruitt, Ledbetter and the studio's receptionist, a detail confirmed by Pruitt in later interviews.

The B-side of the single was "Hollow Rails", the group's arrangement of a traditional railroad ballad.

= = Chart performance = =

"Midnight on the Wabash" entered the Billboard chart in February 1958 and peaked at number 8 in the week of March 24, remaining on the chart for fourteen weeks. It also reached number 11 on the Cash Box chart and was a regional number one in several Midwestern markets, including Indianapolis and Louisville. The single sold an estimated 600,000 copies in the United States.

= = Cover versions = =

The song has been recorded by a number of artists. A country version by singer Loretta Banks reached the lower half of the Billboard country chart in 1964. A British beat group released a faster arrangement in 1965, and a slowed, acoustic interpretation appeared on an album by an American folk-rock duo in 1972. In 2011 the song was used in the soundtrack of an independent film set in rural Indiana.

= Switchman's Lament =

"Switchman's Lament" is a song written by Earl Calloway and recorded by the Calloway Brothers for their 1958 album Night Freight, on which it closes the first side. Although it was never issued as a single, it has come to be regarded by critics as one of the strongest compositions on the album.

= = Background and lyrics = =

The song is narrated by an aging railroad switchman reflecting on decades of routing trains he never rode. Calloway stated that the character was based on his maternal uncle, who worked in a rail yard in Vincennes, Indiana, for more than thirty years. The lyrics contrast the narrator's stationary life with the trains passing through, ending with the line "I sent ten thousand on their way / and never once went home".

Musically, the song is slower than most of the album, set in a 6/8 time signature with a waltz-like feel. Dale Calloway plays the lead guitar part largely on the lower strings, and Ronnie Fitch's bowed double bass appears in the final verse, an unusual feature for a rock and roll recording of the period.

= = Recording = =

"Switchman's Lament" was recorded during the first Night Freight session on October 14, 1957. It was completed in three takes, the fewest of any song on the album. Producer Harlan Pruitt reportedly considered omitting it as too slow for the group's audience but was persuaded by Earl Calloway to retain it as the centerpiece of the album's narrative.

= = Reception and legacy = =

Early reviews of Night Freight seldom mentioned the song, and Cash Box singled out the album's slower material as a weakness. Later critics have been more appreciative. Marian Wexler described it in 1987 as "the emotional hinge of the record", and Gerald Ashby's 1994 liner notes called it "one of the first rock and roll songs to treat working life with real tenderness".

The song has been performed by several roots and Americana musicians since the 1990s. It was included on a 2006 compilation of railroad songs issued by a folk music archive, and a recording of Earl Calloway performing it alone on acoustic guitar, made at a 1984 revival concert, was released posthumously in 2015.
Harrowfield Athletic Football Club is an English association football club based in the town of Harrowfield, West Yorkshire. Founded in 1887, the club currently competes in the National League North, the sixth tier of the English football league system. Nicknamed "the Weavers" in reference to the town's historic textile industry, Harrowfield Athletic have played their home matches at Millbeck Lane since 1903. The club spent 41 seasons in the Football League between 1921 and 1968, reaching the Third Division North on several occasions and recording their highest league finish of fourth in the 1935–36 season.

The club's traditional colours are amber and black, adopted in 1894. Their principal rivals are Calderbridge Town, with whom they contest the Calder Valley derby, and to a lesser extent Ossett Vale.

= = History = =

= = = Formation and early years (1887–1903) = = =

Harrowfield Athletic was formed in October 1887 at a meeting held in the upstairs room of the Golden Shuttle public house on Cross Street. The founding members were drawn largely from the Harrowfield Cricket Club and from employees of Thornley & Sons, the town's largest worsted mill, which provided the club with its first pitch on a field adjoining the mill's dye works. The club's first recorded match was a 3–1 defeat to a works side from Brighouse on 5 November 1887.

For its first seasons the club played only friendly matches and local cup competitions, winning the Heavy Woollen District Challenge Cup in 1891 and 1893. In 1894 Harrowfield joined the West Yorkshire League and adopted amber and black striped shirts, reportedly because a consignment of dyed cloth in those colours was available cheaply from the mill. The club finished as league runners-up in 1897–98 and won the title in 1899–1900, losing only two of its 22 matches.

The relationship with Thornley & Sons ended in 1902 when the mill reclaimed the field for an extension of its premises. After a season sharing a ground with the cricket club, Athletic purchased a plot of former allotment land on Millbeck Lane for £640, funded by a share issue and a public subscription. The new ground opened on 5 September 1903 with a friendly against Bradford City reserves, which was watched by a crowd of approximately 3,000.

= = = Midland League and election to the Football League (1903–1921) = = =

Harrowfield joined the Midland League in 1905, then one of the strongest regional competitions outside the Football League. The club's early seasons in the competition were modest, but the appointment of the former Sheffield United half-back Arthur Penrose as secretary-manager in 1910 brought a marked improvement. Under Penrose the club finished third in 1911–12 and won the Midland League championship in 1913–14, finishing two points ahead of Rotherham County. Forward Herbert Lister scored 34 league goals that season, a club record which stood until 1932.

Competitive football was suspended during the First World War, although the club continued to arrange friendly and regional matches until 1916. At least eleven players who had represented the club before the war were killed in service; a memorial plaque bearing their names was unveiled at Millbeck Lane in 1921 and remains in the main stand.

On the expansion of the Football League in 1921, Harrowfield applied for membership of the newly created Third Division North. The application was supported by the strength of the club's pre-war record and by improvements to Millbeck Lane, including a covered stand seating 1,800. The club was elected at the League's annual meeting in June 1921.

= = = Football League years (1921–1968) = = =

Harrowfield's first Football League match was a 2–2 draw away to Hartlepools United on 27 August 1921. The club finished its debut season in 14th place, and spent most of the 1920s in the lower half of the division, applying for re-election in 1925–26 after finishing second from bottom. The application was successful, with the club receiving 38 votes.

The appointment of Walter Grieve as manager in 1930 began the most successful period in the club's history. Grieve, a Scot who had previously coached in the Netherlands, introduced a more structured training regime and recruited heavily from Scottish junior football. Centre-forward Tommy Ainslie, signed from Bo'ness in 1931, scored 39 league goals in 1932–33, which remains the club's record for a single season. In 1935–36 Harrowfield finished fourth in the Third Division North, eight points behind the champions Chesterfield. The club's record attendance of 21,415 was set on 18 January 1936, for an FA Cup third round tie against Sheffield Wednesday, which Harrowfield lost 1–0.

Grieve left for Hull City in 1937 and the club's form declined. Following the resumption of league football after the Second World War, Harrowfield were a consistently mid-table side. The 1950s were notable chiefly for the club's FA Cup run of 1954–55, in which it defeated Accrington Stanley and Workington before losing 3–2 to Preston North End in the fourth round in front of 19,800 spectators at Millbeck Lane.

The reorganisation of the regional third divisions in 1958 placed Harrowfield in the new Fourth Division. The club struggled financially throughout the 1960s, with attendances falling below 3,000 by 1964 as the local textile industry contracted. Floodlights were installed in 1961 at a cost of £14,000, largely funded by a supporters' club lottery. Harrowfield finished in the bottom four of the Fourth Division in 1964–65, 1966–67 and 1967–68. On the third occasion the club's re-election application failed; it received 17 votes and was replaced in the League by Cambridge United's rivals for the place, with the vacancy ultimately filled from the Southern League.

= = = Non-League football (1968–1999) = = =

Following the loss of League status Harrowfield joined the Northern Premier League, which had been founded the same year. The transition proved difficult: the club released almost its entire professional squad and briefly entered administration in 1970, before a consortium of local businessmen led by the haulage contractor Dennis Firth acquired a controlling interest.

Under Firth's chairmanship the club stabilised and achieved a fifth-place finish in 1973–74. Harrowfield reached the semi-finals of the FA Trophy in 1977–78, losing 3–1 on aggregate to Altrincham. Applications to rejoin the Football League were made in 1971, 1974 and 1977, but none attracted significant support.

The 1980s were a period of decline. The club was relegated from the Northern Premier League for the first time in 1987–88, the season of its centenary, and spent eight seasons in the Northern Counties East League. Millbeck Lane's Kop terrace was closed in 1989 following a safety inspection, reducing the ground's capacity to 4,200. The club returned to the Northern Premier League as champions of its First Division in 1995–96, with manager Keith Rowbotham's side scoring 102 league goals.

= = = Recent history (1999–present) = = =

In 1999 Firth sold his shareholding to the Harrowfield Athletic Supporters' Trust, making the club one of the earliest in English football to come under majority supporter ownership. The trust financed a rebuilding of the Kop end, which reopened as a covered terrace in 2004.

Harrowfield won the Northern Premier League Premier Division title in 2007–08 under manager Gary Holroyd, securing promotion to the Conference North. The club reached the play-off final in 2012–13, losing 2–1 after extra time to Gainsborough-based opponents. Holroyd departed in 2014, and the club was relegated in 2016–17 after a season in which it used five different managers.

The club regained its place in the National League North at the first attempt, winning the 2017–18 Northern Premier League play-off final 1–0 against Ashton United. The 2019–20 and 2020–21 seasons were curtailed because of the COVID-19 pandemic; Harrowfield were in ninth and twelfth place respectively when play was suspended. In 2023–24 the club reached the first round proper of the FA Cup for the first time in sixteen years, losing 2–0 at home to Accrington Stanley in a televised match.

= = Stadium = =

Millbeck Lane has been the club's home since 1903. The ground lies approximately half a mile south of Harrowfield town centre, bordered by the Millbeck stream on its western side. Its current capacity is 5,100, of which 1,850 is seated.

The Main Stand, on the north side, dates in its present form from 1936 and was built with the proceeds of the FA Cup tie against Sheffield Wednesday. It is the oldest surviving structure at the ground and retains its original timber roof trusses. Opposite stands the Riverside Terrace, an open terrace partially covered in 1978. The Kop, rebuilt in 2004 and formally named the Dennis Firth Stand, accommodates around 1,600 standing spectators. The southern end, known as the Allotment End, contains a small seated stand opened in 2011, together with the club's offices and community facilities.

An artificial playing surface was proposed in 2019 but was rejected by a vote of trust members, and the pitch remains natural grass.

= = Colours and crest = =

Harrowfield have worn amber and black since 1894. Vertical stripes were used until 1931, when Walter Grieve introduced plain amber shirts with black collars and cuffs; these remained the standard design until the 1990s. Since 2008 the club has alternated between plain and striped designs. Away kits have most commonly been white or sky blue.

The club crest depicts a shuttle crossed with a bale hook above the town's motto, ''Labore et Fide'' ("By work and faith"), adapted from the arms of the former Harrowfield Borough Council. A simplified version without the motto was used from 1974 to 1999.

= = Rivalries = =

The club's principal rivalry is with Calderbridge Town, located six miles to the east. The two sides first met in 1890, and the fixture, known as the Calder Valley derby, was a regular Football League match between 1921 and 1950. Since 1968 the clubs have frequently competed at different levels, and meetings have been irregular. The highest attendance for the derby in non-League football was 3,940 at Millbeck Lane on Boxing Day 2009.

A secondary rivalry exists with Ossett Vale, dating principally from the clubs' shared seasons in the Northern Counties East League in the late 1980s and early 1990s.

= = Ownership = =

Since 1999 the club has been majority owned by the Harrowfield Athletic Supporters' Trust, an industrial and provident society with approximately 1,400 members as of 2025. The trust elects a board of seven directors, and major decisions, including changes to the club's name, colours or ground, require approval by a two-thirds majority of members. The arrangement has been cited in several reports on supporter ownership in English football as an example of long-term financial stability at semi-professional level.

The ochre-throated fen warbler ( Calamodytes ochrogularis ) is a small passerine bird in the family Acrocephalidae. It is endemic to a few high-altitude sedge marshes on the Halewa Plateau in the southern highlands of Ethiopia. It was first described in 1931 and is one of the least studied warblers in Africa. The species depends on stands of tall sedge and tussock grass that are waterlogged throughout the year. Since the 1970s, drainage, grazing and peat cutting have reduced these habitats, and the International Union for Conservation of Nature ( IUCN ) currently lists the bird as Endangered.

The species is about 13 centimetres ( 5 @.@ 1 in ) long. Its upperparts are warm brown with dark streaks. Its most obvious field mark is a rich ochre patch on the throat and upper breast, which is brighter in the breeding season. Its song is a long, dry, rattling sequence mixed with clear whistled notes. It is most often heard at dawn and dusk. Local Oromo-speaking herders call the bird gurra boollaa , which roughly means " ear of the swamp ". The name refers to the bird's habit of calling from deep cover, where it is rarely seen.

= = Taxonomy = =

The ochre-throated fen warbler was formally described in 1931 by the German ornithologist Ernst Walther Kestermann. His description was based on four specimens collected the previous year near the village of Dida Halewa during an expedition funded by the Senckenberg Museum in Frankfurt. Kestermann placed the species in the genus Acrocephalus and named it Acrocephalus ochrogularis . The specific epithet combines the Greek ōchros , " pale yellow ", with the Latin gula , " throat ".

In 1958 the British taxonomist Margaret Ellery moved the species to a new genus, Calamodytes . She based this on its unusually rounded wings, its graduated tail and the structure of its nest, which differed from those of typical reed warblers. Many later authors rejected the genus and kept the bird in Acrocephalus or placed it in Iduna . A molecular phylogenetic study published in 2014 analysed mitochondrial and nuclear DNA from museum specimens and two recently collected birds. It found that the ochre-throated fen warbler forms a distinct lineage sister to a clade that contains several African swamp warblers. The authors estimated that the lineage diverged about 3 @.@ 2 million years ago. That date coincides with the uplift and cooling of the Ethiopian highlands. Following this study, most major checklists restored Calamodytes as a monotypic genus.

No subspecies are currently recognised. A population on the Gara Muleta escarpment, about 60 kilometres ( 37 mi ) north-east of the main range, was described in 1967 as C. o. muletae . It was said to have a paler throat and a slightly longer bill. Later examination of a larger series of specimens showed that these differences fall within the normal range of variation of the nominate population. The name is now treated as a synonym.

= = Description = =

The ochre-throated fen warbler is a medium-sized warbler with a rounded tail and a fairly long, slender bill. Adults measure 12 @.@ 5 to 13 @.@ 5 centimetres ( 4 @.@ 9 to 5 @.@ 3 in ) in length. They weigh 11 to 15 grams ( 0 @.@ 39 to 0 @.@ 53 oz ). Males average slightly larger than females, but the sexes are alike in plumage.

The crown, mantle and back are warm russet-brown with fine blackish streaks, which are broadest on the mantle. The rump is plainer and more rufous. The wings are dark brown with buff-edged coverts and tertials. The tail feathers are graduated, so the outermost pair is about 15 millimetres ( 0 @.@ 59 in ) shorter than the central pair. The tail feathers also have faintly paler tips. A narrow, pale buff supercilium runs from the base of the bill to just behind the eye. It is bordered below by a dusky eye stripe.

The chin is whitish. It grades into a broad ochre-yellow patch that covers the throat and upper breast. During the breeding season this patch becomes a deep saffron colour. Outside the breeding season it fades to a dull buff-yellow. The lower breast and belly are off-white, and the flanks and undertail coverts are washed tawny. The upper mandible is dark horn and the lower mandible is pinkish. The legs are pale brownish-pink and the iris is dark brown.

Juveniles are duller than adults. Their streaking is less distinct, and the throat is pale buff with no clear ochre patch. They acquire adult plumage after a partial moult in their first year.

The species is unlikely to be confused with any other bird within its small range. The African reed warbler ( Acrocephalus baeticatus ) and the little rush warbler ( Bradypterus baboecala ) sometimes occur at lower altitudes nearby. Neither has the ochre throat. The little rush warbler is also darker and plainer above.

= = = Vocalisations = = =

Males give the song from a concealed perch within dense sedge, usually 30 to 80 centimetres ( 12 to 31 in ) above the water or ground. A typical song lasts between 8 and 20 seconds. It begins with a series of dry, ticking notes that speed up into a rattling trill. The trill is broken at irregular intervals by two to four clear, descending whistles. Recordings made in 2009 showed that individual males have a repertoire of three to six whistle types. Neighbouring males often share some of these whistle types. Song is most intense in the hour before sunrise and again in the late afternoon. Males also sing on overcast or misty days, which are frequent on the plateau during the rains.

The usual contact call is a soft, nasal tchep . Both sexes give a harsh, scolding churr when disturbed near the nest.

= = Distribution and habitat = =

The ochre-throated fen warbler is restricted to the Halewa Plateau and adjoining highland areas in the Oromia Region of southern Ethiopia. It occurs at altitudes between 2 @,@ 600 and 3 @,@ 300 metres ( 8 @,@ 500 and 10 @,@ 800 ft ). Its total extent of occurrence is estimated at less than 900 square kilometres ( 350 sq mi ). The area actually occupied is much smaller, probably under 40 square kilometres ( 15 sq mi ). The species is known from eleven sites. Four of these sites hold most of the population: the Dida Halewa marshes, the Sorbo Fen, the upper Gelana Valley swamps, and the wetlands surrounding Lake Haro Qillee.

= = = The Halewa Plateau wetlands = = =

The Halewa Plateau is a basalt upland formed by lava flows of Oligocene and Miocene age. Its gently undulating surface is cut by shallow valleys. Poor drainage and an annual rainfall of 1 @,@ 400 to 1 @,@ 800 millimetres ( 55 to 71 in ) have produced a network of permanent marshes, seasonal pools and peat-forming fens. Most rain falls in two seasons: the main rains from June to September and shorter rains from March to May. Night frosts are common between November and February. Daytime temperatures seldom exceed 20 ° C ( 68 ° F ).

The largest wetlands lie in broad, flat-bottomed valleys where springs emerge at the base of old lava flows. These springs feed the marshes with a steady supply of cool water even in the dry season. This year-round supply is thought to be critical for the warbler, because the species is absent from wetlands that dry out for more than a few weeks each year. Peat deposits in the Sorbo Fen reach a depth of 4 @.@ 5 metres ( 15 ft ). Radiocarbon dates from the base of the peat show that the fen has existed for at least 9 @,@ 000 years.

The vegetation of the occupied wetlands is dominated by tall sedges, especially Carex monostachya and an endemic Cyperus species. Large tussock grasses such as Festuca and Pennisetum species are also common. Mixed stands of the giant lobelia Lobelia rhynchopetalum grow at the margins of several fens. Sphagnum moss and the creeping herb Alchemilla form a dense mat between the tussocks. Open pools hold sparse emergent vegetation, including Juncus rushes. The warbler prefers the densest parts of the marsh, where sedge stands reach 1 to 1 @.@ 6 metres ( 3 @.@ 3 to 5 @.@ 2 ft ) in height. It is generally absent where vegetation is shorter than about 50 centimetres ( 20 in ). Grazed or burned margins are therefore usually unoccupied.

The wetlands of the plateau support several other notable species. These include the endemic Abyssinian longclaw ( Macronyx flavicollis ), the wattled crane ( Grus carunculata ) and the rouget's rail ( Rougetius rougetii ). The marshes are also an important dry-season refuge for wintering Palearctic migrants such as the common snipe and the sedge warbler. Several endemic frogs of the genus Ptychadena breed in the shallow pools. In 2011 the plateau's core wetlands were identified as an Important Bird and Biodiversity Area.

= = Behaviour and ecology = =

The ochre-throated fen warbler is secretive and spends most of its time low in dense vegetation. It moves through the sedge by clambering between stems, often holding two stems at once with its legs spread wide. It flies only short distances, usually low over the vegetation with rapid wingbeats and the tail slightly spread, before dropping back into cover. It is sedentary. Ringing studies have found adults in the same territories over several years, and no seasonal movements have been recorded.

= = = Diet = = =

The species feeds mainly on small invertebrates. It gleans these from sedge stems, leaves and the surface of the moss layer. Faecal samples collected between 2008 and 2010 contained the remains of beetles, true bugs, spiders, caterpillars, crane flies and non-biting midges. Aquatic insect larvae taken from the water's edge were also present. Chironomid midges were especially abundant in samples from the rainy season. Birds have occasionally been seen taking small snails and seeds of Carex , but plant material appears to form only a minor part of the diet. Foraging activity drops sharply on cold mornings until the sun has warmed the vegetation.

= = = Breeding = = =

Breeding takes place mainly during and just after the main rains. Most clutches are laid between July and October. A smaller number of breeding attempts have been recorded during the short rains in April and May. The species is socially monogamous, and pairs appear to remain together across seasons. Territories are defended by both sexes. In optimal habitat at the Sorbo Fen they average 0 @.@ 3 hectares ( 0 @.@ 74 acres ) in area.

The nest is a deep cup woven from strips of sedge leaf and grass. It is lined with fine rootlets, moss and plant down. Unlike the nests of most reed warblers, it has a partial dome of loosely woven sedge over the rim, which leaves a side entrance. Ellery used this feature as part of her justification for erecting a separate genus. The nest is attached to three to six upright stems, usually 20 to 50 centimetres ( 7 @.@ 9 to 19 @.@ 7 in ) above water or wet ground. The female builds the nest over five to eight days. The male sometimes brings material.

The clutch is two or, rarely, three eggs. The eggs are pale greenish-white with fine olive-brown speckles concentrated at the broad end. Only the female incubates, for 14 to 16 days. The male feeds her at the nest during this period. Both parents feed the nestlings, which fledge at 13 to 15 days old. The young remain dependent on their parents for a further three to four weeks. Nesting success at monitored sites between 2008 and 2012 was about 41 percent. Most failures were caused by predation, while flooding of nests during heavy storms caused a smaller share. Recorded or suspected predators include the Ethiopian highland grass snake, the African marsh harrier ( Circus ranivorus ) and small mammals such as the Ethiopian vlei rat. One pair may raise two broods in a favourable year.

= = = Survival = = =

Ringing data from the Sorbo Fen suggest an annual adult survival rate of about 68 percent. The oldest known individual was a female ringed as an adult in 2007 and last recaptured in 2015. She was therefore at least nine years old.

= = Status and conservation = =

The ochre-throated fen warbler was first assessed by the IUCN as Vulnerable in 1994. It was uplisted to Endangered in 2008 after surveys showed continued loss of habitat and a decline in numbers. A plateau-wide census in 2010 and 2011 estimated the total population at 1 @,@ 200 to 1 @,@ 900 mature individuals. More than 60 percent of these were found at the Sorbo Fen and Dida Halewa marshes. A partial repeat survey in 2019 found that numbers at those two sites were broadly stable. Several smaller sites had lost their populations entirely.

= = = Threats = = =

The main threat to the species is the loss and degradation of its wetland habitat. Since the 1970s, small drainage channels have been cut into the margins of many marshes to create land for barley cultivation and improved pasture. A study of aerial photographs and satellite images estimated that the total area of tall sedge marsh on the plateau fell by about 35 percent between 1972 and 2012.

Heavy grazing by cattle, sheep and horses is another important pressure, especially during the dry season when other forage is scarce. Livestock trample the tussocks and graze the sedge down to a height that the warbler cannot use. Herders also burn the marsh margins to stimulate fresh growth, and such fires occasionally spread into the core of the fens.

Peat is cut on a small scale for fuel at the Lake Haro Qillee wetlands and the upper Gelana Valley. This activity has increased with population growth and the scarcity of firewood on the treeless plateau. Peat cutting removes the vegetation completely, and cut areas take many years to recover.

Climate change may pose a further long-term threat. Regional models predict warmer temperatures and greater variability in rainfall for the Ethiopian highlands. Either change could reduce the flow of the springs that keep the marshes wet year-round. Because the species is already confined to the highest wetlands on the plateau, it has little scope to shift its range upward.

= = = Conservation measures = = =

The Sorbo Fen and part of the Dida Halewa marshes are included in the Halewa Community Conservation Area. The area was established in 2013 through an agreement between regional authorities, local kebele administrations and the Ethiopian Wildlife and Natural History Society. Under the agreement, communities have set aside core zones of the wetlands where grazing and burning are excluded. In return they receive support for alternative fodder production and fuel-efficient stoves. Monitoring since 2014 has shown recovery of sedge height in the fenced core zones. Warbler territories have increased by an estimated 20 percent at the Sorbo Fen.

Several drainage channels at the Dida Halewa marshes were blocked with earth and stone dams between 2016 and 2018. These dams raised the water table over about 45 hectares ( 110 acres ). A long-term monitoring programme has been run jointly by Addis Ababa University and international partners since 2007. It includes annual counts of singing males at fixed points, colour-ringing of adults and nest monitoring.

Proposed conservation actions include the following:

legal protection of the remaining unprotected sites, especially the Lake Haro Qillee wetlands;

restriction of peat extraction;

restoration of drained marsh margins;

further surveys of poorly known wetlands on the Gara Muleta escarpment, where small numbers of birds may persist.

The species has also been suggested as a flagship for the conservation of the Halewa Plateau as a whole, because its presence indicates healthy, permanently wet sedge marsh.

= = History of study = =

For several decades after its description, the ochre-throated fen warbler was known only from the four type specimens and a handful of sight records. The British ornithologist Leslie Brown visited the plateau in 1963 and reported hearing a " rattling and whistling warbler " in the Dida Halewa marshes. However, he did not see the bird well enough to confirm its identity. The first detailed field observations were made in 1967 by the Ethiopian naturalist Tadesse Woldemariam and the American zoologist Robert Kittredge. They collected the specimens later assigned to the subspecies muletae . They also described the domed nest for the first time.

Political instability limited ornithological work in southern Ethiopia from the mid-1970s until the early 1990s, and the species went largely unrecorded during this period. It was rediscovered in 1996 by a team of the Ethiopian Wildlife and Natural History Society. The team found singing males at the Sorbo Fen and recorded the song for the first time. Much of the current knowledge of the species' ecology and breeding biology comes from studies begun at the Sorbo Fen in 2007, led by the ecologist Hirut Abebe.

= = In culture = =

Among the Oromo herders of the Halewa Plateau, the bird's song has traditionally been regarded as a sign that the marshes hold water. Some older herders say that a marsh where the gurra boollaa no longer sings is " tired ". In such cases, livestock should be moved elsewhere to let the marsh rest. Conservation workers have used this belief in community education programmes since 2013.

The species appeared on a 2 birr postage stamp issued by Ethiopian Postal Service in 2017 as part of a series on endemic birds. The stamp depicts a singing male perched among Carex stems.
= = Battle of the Tavuni Passage = =

The Battle of the Tavuni Passage was a night naval engagement fought on 17–18 September 1943 between a United States Navy task group and a Japanese destroyer transport force. It took place in the narrow waters between Tavuni Island and the northern coast of Malakua, in the central Solomon Sea. The Japanese force was attempting to evacuate about 1,900 troops from the garrison at Koromu Point. The American force had been sent to intercept it. The battle was one of several destroyer actions fought during the later stages of the campaign in the central Solomon Islands. It is often cited as an early example of radar-directed torpedo tactics used by American destroyer divisions.

The engagement lasted about two hours and ten minutes. It ended with the loss of two Japanese destroyers and one American destroyer, and serious damage to a second American ship. Most of the Koromu garrison was nevertheless evacuated. Historians have therefore described the outcome as a tactical victory for the United States but an operational success for Japan.

= = Background = =

By the middle of 1943, Allied forces had begun a series of advances through the central Solomon Islands. Malakua, a mountainous island of about 1,400 square kilometres, had been occupied by the Imperial Japanese Army in late 1942. The Japanese built a small airstrip near the village of Hanaro and set up a supply depot at Koromu Point on the island's northwestern tip. The garrison was drawn mainly from the 2nd Battalion of the 229th Infantry Regiment, with naval guard units and construction troops attached.

American and New Zealand forces landed on the southern coast of Malakua on 4 August 1943. They advanced slowly through dense jungle against determined resistance. By early September the Hanaro airstrip had been captured, and the remaining Japanese units had fallen back toward Koromu Point. Their position there was isolated. Supply by barge from the Japanese bases further north had become very dangerous because American motor torpedo boats were operating at night and aircraft were attacking by day.

On 9 September the Japanese Eighth Fleet headquarters at Rabaul decided to withdraw the garrison rather than reinforce it. The task was given to Rear Admiral Ōmura Kiyoshi, who had commanded several earlier supply runs in the region. Ōmura planned to use a mixed force of destroyers and landing barges. The destroyers would escort the barges and carry troops themselves, and would cover the barges against any American surface interference.

= = Opposing forces = =

= = = Japanese forces = = =

Ōmura's force was organised into three groups. The screening group consisted of the destroyers ''Asagiri'', ''Hatsuyuki'' and ''Kazegumo''. It was to engage any American warships that approached. The transport group consisted of the destroyers ''Mikazuki'', ''Isonami'' and ''Yūnagi''. These ships had had some of their torpedo reloads removed to make room for troops. The barge group was made up of about twenty-two Daihatsu landing craft and several smaller vessels. Two submarine chasers, ''Ch-14'' and ''Ch-31'', escorted the barges. Ōmura flew his flag in ''Asagiri''.

The Japanese destroyers carried the Type 93 torpedo. This weapon had a long range and a heavy warhead, and it had caused severe losses to Allied ships earlier in the campaign. However, only ''Asagiri'' and ''Kazegumo'' were fitted with early-model surface search radar, and their crews had little experience with it. The Japanese continued to rely mainly on trained night lookouts using high-powered binoculars.

= = = United States forces = = =

The American force was Task Group 39.4, under Captain Walter H. Brennecke. It consisted of six destroyers organised into two divisions. Division 41, led directly by Brennecke, included USS ''Halloran'', USS ''Pell'' and USS ''Stanbury''. Division 42, under Commander Robert A. Tilson, included USS ''Corwin'', USS ''Mayhew'' and USS ''Granger''. All six ships carried SG surface search radar. All of them except ''Granger'' had combat information centres that had been set up only in the previous few months.

Brennecke had studied earlier night actions in the Solomons. He had concluded that American destroyers had often been tied too closely to cruiser formations, and that they had opened fire with guns too early. Gun flashes revealed their positions and allowed Japanese torpedo attacks to be aimed at them. He had trained his divisions to operate independently. One division would make a torpedo attack using radar while the other stayed in a supporting position, ready to attack from a different bearing. Gunfire was to be held back until the torpedoes had reached their targets.

No cruisers were available to support Task Group 39.4. Several American motor torpedo boats from the base at Rendova Lagoon were ordered to patrol the approaches to Koromu Point on the same night. Their operations were not closely coordinated with the destroyers.

= = Prelude = =

American code breakers and coastwatchers gave the Allies some warning of the Japanese plans. On 15 September a coastwatcher on Tavuni Island reported a sharp rise in barge traffic along the northern coast of Malakua. On 16 September Allied radio intelligence identified signals suggesting a destroyer operation would take place within two days. On the afternoon of 17 September a Royal New Zealand Air Force Hudson patrol aircraft sighted six destroyers about 150 nautical miles north of Malakua. They were heading south at high speed.

Rear Admiral Theodore S. Ashworth, the commander of the area's surface forces, ordered Task Group 39.4 to sail from its anchorage at Purvis Bay. The group left at 13:40. Brennecke's orders were to prevent the evacuation, and to sink as many Japanese vessels as possible. The ships steamed northwest at 30 knots. They entered the Tavuni Passage from the east shortly before 22:00.

The weather that night was overcast, with occasional rain squalls and almost no moonlight. Visibility was estimated at between 2,000 and 4,000 yards (1,800 and 3,700 m) for much of the battle. The sea was calm. The passage itself is about 9 nautical miles (17 km) wide at its narrowest point. Several small islets and reefs lie off the Malakuan coast, and these complicated radar interpretation.

= = Battle = =

= = = Initial contact = = =

At 22:31 the radar operators on ''Halloran'' detected several contacts at a range of about 21,000 yards (19,000 m) to the northwest. At first these were thought to be islets. By 22:36 they were clearly moving, and their plot showed a group of at least three ships heading southeast at about 26 knots. This was Ōmura's screening group. The transport group was about 6 nautical miles behind it and further to the west, closer to the coast. The barges had already begun gathering off Koromu Point.

Brennecke ordered Division 42 to turn away to the south and take up a position from which it could attack the Japanese ships' flank. Division 41 continued northwest in line ahead, closing the range. At 22:44 ''Kazegumo'''s radar detected the American ships. Its operators reported the contact to ''Asagiri'', but the report was confused and Ōmura did not act on it at once. Japanese lookouts did not sight the American destroyers until several minutes later.

= = = First torpedo attack = = =

At 22:49, with the range at about 6,300 yards (5,800 m), Division 41 turned to port and fired torpedoes. ''Halloran'' fired eight, ''Pell'' eight and ''Stanbury'' six, for a total of twenty-two. The ships then turned away sharply to the south to avoid any return fire. Brennecke ordered that no guns be fired.

Japanese lookouts on ''Hatsuyuki'' sighted the American destroyers during their turn. Ōmura ordered his screening group to fire torpedoes and turn away. The Japanese launched about eighteen torpedoes, but the American ships were already turning out of their path, and all of them missed.

The American torpedoes arrived at about 22:54. At least two struck ''Hatsuyuki''. One hit forward of the bridge and one hit the after engine room. The ship lost power immediately and began to settle by the stern. A third torpedo may have struck ''Kazegumo'', but if so it failed to explode. Several other explosions were observed, which American officers believed to be hits. Postwar analysis suggests most of these were torpedoes detonating at the end of their runs or striking reefs near the Malakuan shore.

''Hatsuyuki'' sank at about 23:20. Of its crew of about 230 men, 61 were later rescued by barges from Koromu Point. Others reached the shore by swimming.

= = = Engagement with the transport group = = =

After the first torpedo attack, Ōmura ordered the transport group to continue toward Koromu Point. He took ''Asagiri'' and ''Kazegumo'' north to draw the Americans away from the evacuation area. Brennecke did not follow at once. His radar showed a second group of contacts to the west, and he correctly judged that this was the main body of transports.

Division 42 was by this time in position south of the transport group. At 23:07 Tilson ordered his ships to fire torpedoes at a range of about 5,500 yards (5,000 m). ''Corwin'' and ''Mayhew'' each fired eight. ''Granger'', whose fire control radar had failed, fired only four, and these were aimed largely by visual estimate.

The Japanese transport destroyers were in a loose formation and were heavily loaded. They had little room to manoeuvre. At 23:12 a torpedo struck ''Isonami'' amidships. Its magazine exploded, and the ship broke in two and sank within about three minutes. Only nineteen of the crew survived. ''Mikazuki'' and ''Yūnagi'' turned toward the coast and escaped undamaged.

Division 42 then opened fire with guns on ''Yūnagi'', which had become visible against the glow of the burning wreckage. ''Yūnagi'' was hit several times by 5-inch shells and caught fire. Its crew brought the fires under control, and it continued toward Koromu Point.

= = = Japanese counterattack = = =

The use of gunfire by Division 42 revealed its position. At 23:21 ''Asagiri'' and ''Kazegumo'' returned from the north at high speed. They had reloaded their torpedo tubes. At 23:26 they fired a combined spread of sixteen torpedoes at Division 42 from a range of about 7,000 yards (6,400 m).

The American ships did not detect the attack. At 23:32 a torpedo struck ''Mayhew'' on the starboard side below the forward gun mounts. The explosion destroyed the bow section as far back as the bridge. A second torpedo struck ''Granger'' near the stern, wrecking its propellers and rudder and leaving it dead in the water.

''Corwin'' was not hit. Tilson ordered it to lay smoke and stand by the damaged ships. Division 41 turned north and fired its remaining torpedoes at ''Asagiri'' and ''Kazegumo'' at about 23:40, but these missed. Division 41 then opened fire with guns. ''Kazegumo'' was hit by at least four shells, one of which damaged its forward boiler room. Both Japanese ships withdrew to the northwest under cover of a rain squall.

= = = Evacuation and withdrawal = = =

While the destroyers were fighting, the barges and the remaining transport destroyers carried out the evacuation. ''Mikazuki'' and ''Yūnagi'' anchored off Koromu Point at about 23:35. Barges carried troops out to them in a continuous shuttle. By 00:40 on 18 September about 1,150 troops had been taken aboard the two destroyers. The rest of the garrison, around 600 men, remained in the barges. They set out northwest along the coast toward the island of Rekata, about 40 nautical miles away.

Two American motor torpedo boats, PT-188 and PT-204, attacked the barge column at about 01:15. They sank two barges and damaged a third. Japanese gunfire from the submarine chaser ''Ch-31'' damaged PT-204 and killed two of its crew. The motor torpedo boats then withdrew.

''Mayhew'' sank at about 00:50. ''Corwin'' rescued 187 of its crew. Fifty-eight men were killed or missing. ''Granger'' was taken in tow by ''Stanbury'' at about 01:30 and began a slow return to Purvis Bay. It arrived there on the evening of 19 September.

= = Aftermath = =

= = = Casualties = = =

Japanese losses were the destroyers ''Hatsuyuki'' and ''Isonami'', and at least two landing barges. Total Japanese naval casualties were estimated at about 410 killed. The number of army personnel killed aboard the barges is not known precisely, but is generally put at between 40 and 90. ''Kazegumo'' and ''Yūnagi'' were damaged. Both were repaired at Rabaul within two months.

American losses were the destroyer ''Mayhew'' and 58 men killed or missing. A further 41 men were wounded on ''Mayhew'' and ''Granger''. ''Granger'' was out of service for about seven months while it was repaired at Mare Island Naval Shipyard. Two sailors were killed on PT-204.

About 1,750 Japanese troops reached safety at Rekata or at bases further north. Some 150 men who could not be evacuated were left behind at Koromu Point. Most of them were killed in fighting with American troops over the following week. Twelve were captured.

= = = Assessment = =

American commanders at first believed that the battle had been a much larger success than it was. Brennecke's report claimed four Japanese destroyers sunk and two damaged, along with a large number of barges. These claims were based mainly on observed explosions and the disappearance of radar contacts. They were reduced after the war when Japanese records became available.

The United States Navy regarded the battle as confirming the value of independent destroyer tactics and radar-guided torpedo attacks. The first attack by Division 41 was studied at the Naval War College and in the training of new destroyer squadrons. Analysts noted that it had been made without the Japanese realising that American ships were present, and that no American gun had fired until after the torpedoes had arrived.

The loss of ''Mayhew'' and the damage to ''Granger'' drew attention to the weaknesses of the tactics as well. A report by the staff of the South Pacific Force in October 1943 concluded that Division 42 had opened gunfire too soon and had stayed too long in the same area afterwards. The report recommended that destroyer divisions change course and speed immediately after any gunfire. It also recommended closer coordination between divisions, so that a division not engaged could cover the other against counterattacks.

From the Japanese point of view, the battle was regarded as costly but acceptable. Ōmura was praised by Eighth Fleet headquarters for having saved most of the Koromu garrison. Japanese naval staff officers noted that American radar had given the enemy a clear advantage in the first part of the action. They recommended faster installation of radar on destroyers and better training of radar operators. Few of these improvements were made before Japanese forces left the central Solomons later that year.

= = = Historical interpretation = = =

Historians have generally agreed that the battle was a tactical success for the United States, because the Japanese lost more ships and men. Its strategic result is seen as more mixed. The main aim of the American force had been to stop the evacuation, and in this it failed. The historian Martin R. Okafor has argued that Brennecke concentrated too heavily on the Japanese destroyers and too little on the barge column, which carried a third of the garrison. Others have pointed out that the barges were very difficult to detect by radar near the coast, and that the destroyers had few practical means of attacking them in the dark.

The battle is also noted for the performance of the Type 93 torpedo in the Japanese counterattack. Although the Japanese had been surprised at the start of the action, their ability to reload quickly and return to attack from long range allowed them to inflict serious losses. Some writers have described this as one of the last successful Japanese torpedo attacks of its kind in the Solomon Islands campaign.

= = Legacy = =

The wreck of ''Mayhew'' lies at a depth of about 260 metres (850 ft) in the Tavuni Passage. It was located in 2009 by a survey team using a remotely operated vehicle. The wreck of ''Isonami'' was found during the same survey, in two sections lying about 400 metres (1,300 ft) apart. Both sites are regarded as war graves. Local authorities in Malakua have requested that they not be disturbed.

A memorial to the crew of ''Mayhew'' was dedicated in 1951 at the Naval Station in Norfolk, Virginia. In 1993 a memorial stone was placed at Koromu Point by a delegation of Japanese veterans and families of the men who died there, in a ceremony attended by local residents and representatives of the United States.

Captain Brennecke was awarded the Navy Cross for his command of Task Group 39.4. Commander Tilson received the Silver Star. Rear Admiral Ōmura was killed in action in February 1944 when his flagship was sunk by aircraft near Truk.

Lanternfall is a 2006 action-adventure video game developed by Halvorsen Interactive and published by Corvane Entertainment for the PlayStation 2, Xbox, and Microsoft Windows. The game follows Edda Marrow, a lighthouse keeper's daughter on a fictional North Atlantic archipelago, who searches for her missing father after the island's lights go dark during a prolonged winter storm. The game combines exploration, environmental puzzles, and light-based combat, in which the player uses a modified oil lantern to repel hostile creatures and reveal hidden pathways.

Lanternfall was Halvorsen Interactive's second commercial release, following the 2003 racing game Fjordline. Development began in 2004 with a team of about 40 people based in Bergen, Norway, and later expanded to about 65. The game was released in North America on October 17, 2006, and in Europe on October 27, 2006. Critics praised its atmosphere, art direction, and sound design, while its combat system, camera controls, and pacing in the final third drew criticism. It sold about 410,000 copies across all platforms by the end of 2007. Commercially it fell short of the publisher's expectations, but it later gained a cult following and influenced several independent games of the following decade.

= = Gameplay = =

Lanternfall is played from a third-person perspective. The player controls Edda Marrow as she travels across the islands of the Skerrow archipelago, a chain of six main islands and several smaller outcrops. The game is broadly linear. Each island forms a self-contained area that the player can explore freely before moving on, and the player can return to previously visited islands by small boat once certain story events have taken place.

The central mechanic is Edda's lantern. It can be adjusted between three settings: a dim glow that saves fuel, a standard beam that lights the area ahead, and a concentrated flare that damages enemies and triggers certain environmental objects. Fuel is limited. The player collects whale oil, paraffin, and salvaged lamp oil scattered throughout the islands, and running out of fuel leaves Edda vulnerable in darkness. A gauge on the brass housing of the lantern itself shows the current fuel level, rather than an on-screen display. The developers said this choice was meant to keep the player's attention within the game world.

Many puzzles involve directing light. The player can rotate mirrors, open shutters, reposition lenses salvaged from lighthouses, and light signal fires to cast beams onto distant surfaces. Some of these surfaces show messages or symbols visible only under particular kinds of light. In later sections, the player gains coloured glass filters for the lantern. These reveal different layers of the environment, such as old footprints, faded paint markings, or the outlines of structures that no longer exist.

Combat in Lanternfall is mostly defensive. The main enemies, called "the Drowned" in the game, are humanoid figures that appear in darkness and retreat from strong light. Edda cannot kill most of them outright. She must drive them back with her lantern, trap them in lit areas, or avoid them through stealth. A small number of larger enemies, which the game calls "Tidewardens," serve as boss encounters. Defeating each one requires lighting a specific sequence of beacons within its arena. Edda also carries a boathook, which she can use for close-range attacks, though it does little damage.

The game has no traditional experience or levelling system. Edda's abilities grow as she acquires new tools, such as a grappling line, a hand-cranked signal horn that stuns nearby enemies, and an improved lantern reflector that widens the beam. Collectable items include pages from her father's logbook, which add to the backstory, and carved driftwood figures. Finding all 48 figures unlocks concept art and an alternate costume.

= = Plot = =

The game is set in 1911 on the fictional Skerrow Islands, said to lie between the Faroe Islands and Iceland. Edda Marrow, aged nineteen, lives with her father, Aldous, at the lighthouse on Inner Skerrow, the largest of the islands. During a severe storm in early December, every lighthouse in the archipelago goes dark on the same night. Aldous leaves to investigate and does not return.

After the storm passes, Edda finds the island strangely empty and wrapped in persistent fog. The other residents have vanished, and their homes have been left as if abandoned in a hurry. She takes her father's old lantern and sets out to relight the lighthouses one at a time, believing the lights may guide her father home. As she moves from island to island, she meets the Drowned and begins finding pages of her father's logbook. The pages show that Aldous had spent years studying records of an older lighthouse that once stood on the outermost island, Farrow's Teeth, and had been destroyed in an accident in 1847.

Edda meets few other living characters. Among them is Peder Halse, an elderly fisherman who has sheltered in a sea cave on the island of Gullhamn and who knows fragments of local folklore. Another is a mute girl named Sigrun, who appears several times and leaves objects to guide Edda without speaking. Through Peder, Edda learns of a local legend that the original keeper of Farrow's Teeth deliberately put out his light to wreck a ship, and that the drowned crew had sworn to bring darkness back to the islands.

In the final act, Edda reaches Farrow's Teeth and climbs the ruins of the old lighthouse. There she finds her father, who has been trying to rebuild the lamp in the belief that relighting it will lay the drowned crew to rest. The logbook pages reveal that the original keeper was Aldous's great-grandfather. Edda and Aldous relight the beacon together while fending off the last of the Drowned. The game then cuts to a scene of the islands at dawn, with the fog lifting and the lighthouses burning.

A short epilogue unlocked by collecting all logbook pages shows that Sigrun was a passenger on the wrecked ship of 1847, implying that she had been guiding Edda toward resolving the curse. Critics noted the ending's ambiguity, and the developers declined for several years to say whether the supernatural events were meant to be read literally.

= = Development = =

Halvorsen Interactive was founded in 2000 by Tore Halvorsen and Kjersti Lunde, both former employees of a Bergen-based multimedia company. After the moderate success of Fjordline, the studio wanted to make a game that drew on Norwegian coastal culture and the history of lighthouse keeping. Lunde, who served as the game's creative director, said in later interviews that the idea came from a visit to a decommissioned lighthouse near Stavanger. She said the experience of standing in the lamp room in total darkness while the wind battered the building gave her the core image of "light as the only thing between a person and the sea."

Pre-production began in early 2004. The original concept, titled Keeper, was a slower-paced exploration game with almost no combat. Corvane Entertainment signed the project in mid-2004 and asked for more action elements so the game would appeal to a wider audience. This led to the introduction of the Drowned and the lantern combat system. Several team members later said the change made development harder. In a 2011 retrospective interview, lead designer Magnus Eide said that the combat "was always something we were adding on to a game that had been designed without it," and that the team never fully reconciled the two halves.

The game was built on a modified version of the engine Halvorsen had developed for Fjordline, extended to support dynamic lighting and volumetric fog. Lighting was a major technical challenge on the PlayStation 2, whose hardware limited the number of real-time light sources. Lead programmer Anders Rønning devised a system that combined pre-calculated lighting for static scenery with a small number of dynamic lights for the lantern and key puzzle objects. The fog effects, which hide the game's draw distance and add to its atmosphere, were created partly to keep the frame rate stable on the console.

The art team, led by Ingrid Solheim, studied photographs of Norwegian and Scottish lighthouses from the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, along with paintings by artists such as Peder Balke. Solheim described the intended visual style as "muted and wet," with a restricted palette of greys, blues, and ochres, broken by the warm light of the lantern. Character models were kept deliberately simple. The faces of the Drowned were left mostly undefined to give them an unsettling quality.

The music was composed by Even Strand, who had no previous experience in video games. Strand recorded much of the score using a small string ensemble, a Hardanger fiddle, and recordings of wind and water made on the Norwegian coast. The soundtrack contains long stretches of near silence, and music plays mainly during story scenes and boss encounters. The sound design, by Lise Hauge, made heavy use of positional audio. The approach of the Drowned is signalled by the sound of dripping water, which helps the player locate them in darkness.

Voice recording took place in London in early 2006. Edda was voiced by the British actress Clare Whitton, and Aldous by Norwegian actor Henrik Vold. Corvane initially asked for the dialogue to be recorded in American English, but the developers argued for British and Scandinavian accents. A compromise allowed most characters to keep regional accents.

Development ran about six months behind schedule. The release window moved from spring 2006 to autumn 2006, partly because of difficulties with the Xbox version's lighting and partly because of a late redesign of the final island. A planned seventh island, provisionally called Ravnsøy, was cut during this period. Some of its content, including a puzzle involving a flooded church, was moved to other areas.

= = Release = =

Corvane announced Lanternfall at the Electronic Entertainment Expo in May 2005 with a short trailer showing Edda walking through a dark village. A playable demonstration was shown at E3 2006 and at the Games Convention in Leipzig in August 2006. A demo featuring the opening section on Inner Skerrow was released on the Official Xbox Magazine demo disc in September 2006 and as a download for Windows.

The game was released in North America on October 17, 2006, and in Europe on October 27, 2006. A limited edition, sold only in Europe, came with a small replica brass lantern keychain, a soundtrack CD containing 14 tracks, and a printed booklet styled as Aldous Marrow's logbook. The Windows version supported higher resolutions and included an optional setting that improved fog and shadow quality.

Marketing for the game was modest. Halvorsen staff later said they believed the game suffered from a crowded autumn release schedule and from being released late in the PlayStation 2 and Xbox generation, when attention was shifting to the Xbox 360 and the upcoming PlayStation 3. A planned Xbox 360 version was announced in early 2007 but was cancelled later that year.

= = Reception = =

Lanternfall received "generally favourable" reviews according to the review aggregator Metacritic, with scores of 77 out of 100 for the PlayStation 2 version, 78 for the Xbox version, and 75 for the Windows version.

Reviewers widely praised the game's atmosphere and presentation. GameSpot gave the Xbox version a score of 8.0 out of 10 and called the islands "one of the most quietly menacing settings of the console generation." The reviewer singled out the use of fog and limited light for building tension without relying on frequent combat. IGN rated the PlayStation 2 version 7.9 out of 10 and described the lantern puzzles as "clever and well integrated." It criticised the camera, which it said often became stuck against walls in tight interiors. Edge magazine gave the game 7 out of 10 and praised its "restraint," but argued that the combat sections "dilute what is otherwise a confident and unusual game."

The sound design and music received particular praise. Several publications named Strand's score among the best of the year, and the game's audio was nominated at the 2007 Game Audio Network Guild Awards. Eurogamer described the dripping-water cue for the Drowned as "a simple idea used to devastating effect."

The game's pacing and final third drew more mixed responses. Several reviewers felt that the later islands relied too heavily on repeated enemy encounters and backtracking. GamePro stated that "the last few hours feel stretched, as if the game is reluctant to end." The fuel system also divided critics. Some found that it added tension, while others found it frustrating, especially in the later stages, where fuel became scarce.

Critics generally praised the story for its restraint and its use of the logbook pages, though some found the ending abrupt. The Edda character was frequently noted as an unusual protagonist for an action game of the period. One reviewer wrote that she was "neither a soldier nor a superhero, but a practical young woman who fixes things."

= = = Sales = = =

Lanternfall sold about 140,000 copies in its first month across all platforms. Sales were strongest in Europe, particularly in the United Kingdom, Germany, and the Nordic countries. By December 2007 total sales had reached about 410,000 copies. Corvane had projected sales of about 750,000. In a 2008 financial report, the publisher described the title's performance as "below expectations," and it did not commission a sequel.

= = = Accolades = = =

The game won the award for Best Art Direction at the 2006 Nordic Game Awards and was nominated for Best Original Score. It also received a nomination for Outstanding Achievement in Sound Design at the 2007 Interactive Achievement Awards. Several publications, including the Norwegian newspaper Bergens Tidende, listed it among the best games of 2006.

= = Legacy = =

In the years after its release, Lanternfall developed a cult following, partly through word of mouth and partly through discount sales of the Windows version. Retrospective articles in the early 2010s cited it as an early example of a mainstream-published game built around atmosphere and environmental storytelling rather than combat. Several independent developers have named it as an influence. They include the creators of the 2014 exploration game Saltmarsh, who described Lanternfall as "the game that showed us darkness could be a mechanic."

Halvorsen Interactive released one more game, the 2009 puzzle title Tidewright for the Nintendo DS, before closing in 2010 after failing to secure funding for a new project. Many former staff joined other Scandinavian studios. Kjersti Lunde later co-founded an independent studio in Oslo, and Even Strand went on to compose music for film and television.

In 2015 the Windows version was re-released on digital distribution platforms with updated compatibility for modern operating systems and widescreen support, after the rights were acquired from Corvane's successor company. The re-release received positive user reviews and renewed critical interest. Some writers argued that the game's design ideas had become more widely accepted in the years since its release. A fan community has worked to restore cut content from the game files, including partial assets from the abandoned island of Ravnsøy.

In a 2016 interview marking the game's tenth anniversary, Lunde said that she remained proud of the game's atmosphere but regretted the compromises over combat. She stated that if the game were remade, she would "turn the lights down further and give the player fewer ways to fight." No remake or sequel has been announced.
= Sedgecombe =

Sedgecombe is a market town and civil parish in the Mendip district of Somerset, England. It lies on the northern edge of the Somerset Levels, about 9 miles (14 km) south-west of Wells and 4 miles (6 km) from the nearest point of the River Brue. At the 2021 census the parish had a population of 4,862. The town grew around a medieval market granted in 1268 and became a modest centre of the cloth and leather trades before declining in the 19th century. Its best-known landmarks are the Church of St Aldhelm, the Market Cross, and the Sedgecombe Tithe Barn. The town is the start of the long-distance footpath known as the Levels Way.

= = History = =

= = = Early history = = =

Worked flints of Mesolithic date have been recovered from the low ridge of Lias limestone on which the town stands, and a Bronze Age trackway of split oak planks was found in peat diggings at Hawkmoor, south of the town, in 1937. A small Romano-British settlement occupied the site of the present Church Street. Excavations in 1974, before the construction of a car park, uncovered the footings of a stone building with a hypocaust and coins dating from the reign of Constantine I to that of Honorius.

The name Sedgecombe is first recorded as Secgcumb in a charter of 973, in which King Edgar granted land in the area to the abbey of Glastonbury. The name is generally interpreted as Old English for "valley where sedge grows", a reference to the marshy hollow below the ridge. In the Domesday Book of 1086 the manor of Seccumbe was held by the abbey and was assessed at five hides, with land for eight ploughs, two mills, and 30 acres of meadow. The survey recorded 14 villeins, 9 bordars, and 3 slaves.

= = = Medieval market town = = =

In 1268 Henry III granted the abbot of Glastonbury a charter for a weekly market on Thursdays and an annual fair on the feast of St Aldhelm. The abbey laid out a broad market place along the ridge, with burgage plots running back to lanes on either side, and the pattern of these long, narrow plots remains visible in the modern street plan. By 1340 the town had 62 burgesses and a guildhall, and the lay subsidy of 1327 lists a dyer, two tanners and a fuller among its taxpayers.

The town's prosperity in the later Middle Ages rested on wool from the surrounding uplands and hides from cattle grazed on the Levels. A fulling mill on the Combe Brook is recorded in 1391. Much of the present fabric of the parish church dates from rebuilding funded by local clothiers in the 15th century.

= = = Dissolution and early modern period = = =

Following the dissolution of Glastonbury Abbey in 1539, the manor passed to the Crown and was sold in 1544 to Sir Thomas Wroxall, a London mercer. The Wroxall family held it until 1702, when it was sold to the Pennard family of Bath. The Wroxalls built Combe Court, a manor house north of the church, which was largely demolished in 1811; one wing survives as a private residence.

During the Monmouth Rebellion of 1685, a number of men from Sedgecombe joined the Duke of Monmouth's forces. Following the Battle of Sedgemoor, three were tried at the Bloody Assizes and hanged at Wells, and a further eleven were transported to Barbados. A plaque recording their names was placed in the market place in 1985.

The cloth trade declined in the 18th century as production shifted to the larger towns of Wiltshire and the north of England, but tanning continued and grew. By 1790 there were four tanyards in the town, the largest belonging to the Hembury family, which also operated a glove factory on Mill Lane from 1823.

= = = 19th and 20th centuries = = =

The Somerset and Dorset Joint Railway opened a branch line through Sedgecombe in 1862, with a station on the eastern edge of the town. The railway encouraged the growth of a cheese and butter trade, and the Sedgecombe Dairy Company opened a creamery near the station in 1889. The population, which had been 2,104 in 1801, peaked at 3,215 in 1861 before falling to 2,730 by 1901 as agricultural employment declined.

The Hembury tannery closed in 1931 and the glove factory in 1958. The railway branch closed to passengers in 1952 and to goods in 1963, following the Beeching report. The station building survives as offices. From the 1960s the town expanded with housing estates to the north and west, and it became increasingly a commuter settlement for Wells, Glastonbury and Bristol. A bypass carrying the A371 opened in 1994, removing through traffic from the market place.

= = Governance = =

Sedgecombe Town Council has fifteen councillors elected from two wards, North and South. The council meets in the Guildhall and is responsible for the town's allotments, cemetery, public conveniences and several open spaces, including the Recreation Ground and Hawkmoor Common. The town lies within the Somerset Council unitary authority area, which replaced Somerset County Council and Mendip District Council in 2023. For elections to the House of Commons, it is part of the Wells and Mendip Hills constituency.

From 1894 until 1974 the town was administered by Sedgecombe Urban District Council, which was abolished under the Local Government Act 1972.

= = Geography = =

The historic core of the town occupies a ridge of Blue Lias limestone rising to about 40 metres (130 ft) above sea level, overlooking the flat peat and clay moors of the Levels to the south. The Combe Brook, a tributary of the Brue, flows in a shallow valley along the eastern side of the town. Hawkmoor, an area of former peat workings south of the bypass, is now partly managed as a nature reserve by the Somerset Wildlife Trust and supports breeding snipe, reed warblers and a winter population of starlings that forms large roosting flocks.

The parish contains the hamlets of Lower Combe, Withy End and Kingsbridge. Flooding of the moors is frequent in winter; the most severe recent event, in February 2014, cut the road to Kingsbridge for eleven days.

= = Economy = =

The weekly Thursday market continues, although on a reduced scale, and a farmers' market is held in the market place on the first Saturday of each month. The main employers in the town are the Hawkmoor Business Park, established in 1998 on the site of the former creamery, which houses around thirty small firms; the secondary school; and a supermarket opened in 2006. A number of independent shops, cafés and public houses occupy the historic buildings of the market place and High Street. Tourism, centred on walking and the town's historic buildings, makes a modest contribution to the local economy.

= = Landmarks = =

The town contains 87 listed buildings, of which one, the Church of St Aldhelm, is listed at Grade I and three, including the Tithe Barn and Market Cross, at Grade II*. The centre of the town was designated a conservation area in 1971.

Other notable buildings include the Guildhall, a two-storey building of 1612 with an open ground-floor arcade, which was enclosed in 1834 and now houses the town council and a small museum; the Bell Inn, a coaching inn with a 17th-century façade and earlier timber framing; and the Hembury Almshouses, a range of six cottages built in 1847 under the will of Josiah Hembury.

= = Education = =

Sedgecombe has two primary schools, St Aldhelm's Church of England Primary School and Combe Brook Primary School, and one secondary school, the Wroxall Academy. The academy traces its origins to a grammar school founded in 1587 under the will of Sir Thomas Wroxall's son, Richard. It became a comprehensive school in 1973 and an academy in 2012, and has around 900 pupils.

= = Transport = =

The A371 between Wells and Castle Cary passes to the south of the town on the bypass. Bus services connect Sedgecombe with Wells, Glastonbury and Street. The nearest railway station is Castle Cary, about 11 miles (18 km) away. The trackbed of the former railway has been partly converted into a cycle path, the Brue Line, which runs for 3 miles (5 km) eastward from the old station.

= = Culture and community = =

Sedgecombe Fair, a descendant of the medieval St Aldhelm's fair, is held on the last weekend of May and includes a procession, a cattle show and a pageant staged by local schools. The town has a carnival in November as part of the Somerset carnival circuit, with illuminated floats built by local clubs.

The Sedgecombe and District Museum, in the upper room of the Guildhall, displays finds from the Roman site, items from the glove and tanning industries, and a model of the town as it appeared in about 1800. Sedgecombe Town Football Club plays in the Somerset County League, and the town also has cricket, bowls and rugby clubs.

= = Notable people = =

* Hannah Pennard (1748–1819), diarist, whose journals provide a detailed account of rural Somerset society in the late 18th century
* Josiah Hembury (1771–1846), tanner and philanthropist
* Walter Crane Ellison (1880–1952), botanist who made a study of the flora of the Somerset moors
* Margaret Ivey (1921–2010), potter, who established a workshop in Lower Combe in 1956

= Church of St Aldhelm, Sedgecombe =

The Church of St Aldhelm is the Church of England parish church of Sedgecombe, Somerset. It is a Grade I listed building and is notable for its 15th-century west tower, one of the group of elaborate Perpendicular towers characteristic of Somerset.

= = History = =

A church at Sedgecombe is implied by the Domesday survey, and fragments of a 12th-century building, including a carved tympanum showing the Agnus Dei, survive reset above the north door. The chancel was rebuilt in the 13th century and retains a group of three lancet windows in its east wall.

The church was substantially rebuilt between about 1440 and 1495. The nave, aisles and west tower date from this period. The tower is recorded in the will of John Bealde, clothier, who in 1463 left 40 shillings "to the werke of the new tour". The south porch, with a parvise chamber above, was added around 1500.

The church was restored in 1868–70 by the architect John Norton, who removed galleries, renewed the roof of the north aisle and installed the present pews. A further restoration of the tower stonework was carried out between 2003 and 2007 with funding from the Heritage Lottery Fund.

= = Architecture = =

The church is built of local Lias stone with dressings of Doulting stone. It consists of a chancel, a nave of five bays with north and south aisles, a south porch and a west tower.

The tower rises in four stages to a height of 31 metres (102 ft). It has set-back buttresses terminating in crocketed pinnacles, a pierced parapet, and a stair turret at the north-east corner. The belfry stage has paired windows filled with traceried stone panels known locally as "Somerset tracery". Niches on the west face formerly held statues, of which only a damaged figure of St Aldhelm survives.

Inside, the nave has a wagon roof with carved bosses, including figures of a green man and a pelican in her piety. A carved oak rood screen spans the nave and both aisles; it was restored in 1912 but retains much original 15th-century work. The church contains a font of the 12th century with a cable moulding, and a Jacobean pulpit dated 1631.

The north aisle contains the Wroxall monuments, the most elaborate of which is a canopied tomb chest with effigies of Sir Thomas Wroxall (d. 1561) and his wife Joan. Stained glass in the east window was made by the firm of Clayton and Bell in 1871.

= = Bells = =

The tower contains a ring of eight bells. The oldest, the fifth, was cast in about 1450 by a Bristol foundry and bears the inscription "Sancte Aldelme ora pro nobis". The ring was augmented from six to eight in 1924.

= Sedgecombe Market Cross =

Sedgecombe Market Cross is a 15th-century market cross standing at the western end of the market place in Sedgecombe, Somerset. It is a Grade II* listed building and a scheduled monument.

= = Description = =

The cross is a hexagonal structure built of Doulting stone, consisting of a central pillar surrounded by six arches supporting a vaulted roof. The roof is surmounted by a slender shaft and a carved head, which was replaced in 1897. Around the base is a stepped platform on which traders sold produce, and the interior space provided shelter for market sellers. The vault is ribbed and has a central boss carved with the crossed keys associated with Glastonbury Abbey.

= = History = =

The cross is thought to have been built around 1480 by the abbey, which held the market rights. It was used as a shelter for the butter and poultry market until the late 19th century. The original cross head was removed in 1645, reportedly by Parliamentarian soldiers quartered in the town, and the shaft stood bare until 1897, when a new head carved by a Wells mason was installed to mark the Diamond Jubilee of Queen Victoria.

In 1951 the cross was damaged when a lorry struck one of its piers. The pier was rebuilt using matching stone in the following year. The cross was cleaned and its masonry consolidated in 2018. It continues to serve as a focal point of Sedgecombe Fair, during which the proclamation of the fair is read from its steps.

= Sedgecombe Tithe Barn =

Sedgecombe Tithe Barn is a medieval barn located off Barn Lane in Sedgecombe, Somerset. Built in the 14th century for Glastonbury Abbey, it was used to store produce collected as tithes from the abbey's estates in the area. It is a Grade II* listed building and is owned and managed by a local charitable trust.

= = History = =

Dendrochronological analysis of the roof timbers, carried out in 1996, gave felling dates between 1338 and 1342, indicating that the barn was built shortly afterwards. After the Dissolution, the barn passed with the manor to the Wroxall family and was used as an ordinary farm barn. It remained in agricultural use until 1962.

By the 1970s the barn was in poor condition, and part of the roof had collapsed in a storm in 1976. The Sedgecombe Barn Trust was formed in 1979 to acquire and repair the building. Restoration, completed in 1988, involved re-roofing with stone tiles salvaged from demolished buildings in the area and the repair of the cruck trusses.

= = Architecture = =

The barn is built of coursed Lias rubble and is 33 metres (108 ft) long and 9 metres (30 ft) wide. It is cruciform in plan, with two opposed gabled porches on its long sides, which allowed loaded carts to enter and unload. The gable ends carry carved stone figures of the symbols of the Four Evangelists, of which those of St Mark and St Luke remain in good condition. The roof is supported by seven raised cruck trusses with two tiers of wind braces.

= = Present use = =

The barn is open to visitors from April to October and is used for concerts, exhibitions, weddings and the annual Sedgecombe Craft Fair. A small exhibition inside describes the tithe system and the history of the abbey's estates on the Levels.

= Levels Way =

The Levels Way is a waymarked long-distance footpath in Somerset, England, running 34 miles (55 km) from Sedgecombe to Langport. It was established in 2001 by a partnership of local authorities and walking groups.

= = Route = =

The path begins at the Market Cross in Sedgecombe and descends southward across Hawkmoor before following the banks of the River Brue and a series of drainage channels, known locally as rhynes. It passes through the villages of Kingsbridge, Meare Green and Westholme, and skirts the edge of several nature reserves, before reaching the River Parrett and following it to Langport. Most of the route is level, making it popular with walkers of all abilities, although sections near the rivers may be impassable after heavy rainfall.

= = Features = =

Points of interest along the route include the site of the Hawkmoor trackway, a restored pumping station at Westholme dating from 1867, and several wooden bird hides. Information boards along the path describe the history of drainage on the Levels, the peat industry, and the growing of willow for basket-making.
