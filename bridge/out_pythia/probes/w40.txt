Halloran College, Aldmere

Halloran College is one of the constituent colleges of the University of Aldmere, a collegiate university in the cathedral city of Aldmere in the north of England. Founded in 1847 as a theological hall for training non-conformist ministers, it was incorporated into the university in 1903 and became a fully mixed undergraduate and graduate college in 1971. The college occupies a site of roughly 4.2 hectares on the eastern bank of the River Wenn, bounded by Sexton Lane to the north and the former Corn Exchange to the south. As of the 2024–25 academic year, it had approximately 640 undergraduate and 310 postgraduate students, along with 74 fellows.

The college takes its name from Josiah Halloran (1789–1858), a Leeds wool merchant and Congregationalist lay preacher whose bequest funded the original buildings. Its motto is *Lux in tenebris quaerenda*, "Light is to be sought in darkness." Its colours are slate grey and russet.

= = History = =

= = = Foundation and early years = = =

Halloran Hall, as it was originally known, opened in October 1847 with eleven students and two tutors. It occupied a converted malt house on Sexton Lane, which had been acquired by Josiah Halloran in 1844 with the intention of establishing a seminary for dissenting ministers who were then excluded from the ancient universities. The first principal was the Reverend Ebenezer Thwaite, a Glasgow-educated Hebraist, who remained in post until 1869.

The early curriculum was dominated by theology, biblical languages and moral philosophy, though Thwaite introduced lectures in natural philosophy and political economy in the 1850s, which attracted a small number of non-theological students from the town. Following Halloran's death in 1858, a dispute arose among his heirs over the terms of the bequest, and the hall was briefly closed between 1860 and 1861 while the matter was resolved in the Court of Chancery. The settlement, known locally as the Halloran Deed, placed the hall under a board of nine trustees and permitted the admission of students not intending to enter the ministry.

= = = Expansion and university incorporation = = =

The 1870s and 1880s saw considerable growth. Under the principalship of the Reverend Walter Ingleby (1869–1895), the hall expanded to around sixty students and commissioned its first purpose-built accommodation, the North Range, designed by the Bradford architect Samuel Purnell and completed in 1878. The range, built of local gritstone in a restrained Gothic Revival style, remains the oldest residential building in the college and is Grade II listed.

When the University of Aldmere received its royal charter in 1899, Halloran Hall was initially excluded on account of its denominational character. Negotiations conducted by Ingleby's successor, Arthur Fenwick, led to the hall's incorporation as a recognised college of the university in 1903, on condition that religious tests for admission and fellowship be abolished. The theological faculty was subsequently transferred to the university, though the college retained a chaplaincy and continues to hold a Sunday evening service in its chapel during term.

= = = Twentieth century = = =

During the First World War, the college's numbers fell sharply, and the North Range was requisitioned as a convalescent hospital for officers between 1916 and 1919. A memorial tablet in the chapel records the names of forty-three members of the college who died in the war. A similar tablet, unveiled in 1949, lists twenty-eight members who died in the Second World War.

In the interwar period, the college developed a reputation for the study of geology and agricultural science, largely owing to the influence of Professor Ronald Eskdale, a fellow from 1921 to 1954, who established a small field station at Grisedale Moor. The college library, completed in 1934 to designs by Helena Marsh, one of the first women to be elected an associate of the Royal Institute of British Architects in the north of England, is regarded as a notable example of interwar civic architecture.

Halloran admitted women to postgraduate study from 1958 and to undergraduate courses from 1971, making it the third college in the university to become fully co-educational. The first female fellow, the historian Dr Margaret Oakshott, was elected in 1962. The college's first female principal, Professor Anne Ridley-Carr, served from 1989 to 2001.

= = = Recent history = = =

In 2006 the college completed the Wennside Building, a residential and teaching block of 180 rooms on the riverside, designed by the practice Lowther Abbott Architects. The building received a regional award from the Royal Institute of British Architects in 2008 for its use of low-energy heating and its terraced gardens leading to the water. A major refurbishment of the North Range was completed in 2019, during which a previously unrecorded medieval cellar, thought to belong to a fourteenth-century tithe barn, was uncovered beneath the building's eastern end.

In 2021 the college announced the Halloran Access Programme, a scheme providing bursaries and summer residential courses for applicants from state schools in the North East and Cumbria. By 2024 the college reported that 71 per cent of its undergraduate intake came from state-maintained schools.

= = Buildings and grounds = =

The college's principal buildings are arranged around two courts. Old Court is enclosed by the North Range, the chapel and the original malt house, now used as the college's dining hall. New Court, laid out in 1934, is bounded by the library, the Senior Common Room and the Eskdale Building, a 1960s concrete teaching block that has been the subject of periodic debate over its architectural merit.

The chapel, completed in 1883, is a plain rectangular building with a hammerbeam roof and a set of stained-glass windows by the Glasgow studio of Lindsay & Weir. The organ, built by Harrison & Harrison in 1912, was restored in 2014.

The dining hall retains the brick kiln arches of the former malt house, which were exposed during a renovation in 1987. Portraits of former principals hang along its south wall, including a likeness of Josiah Halloran attributed to the circle of John Jackson.

The college gardens extend to the river and include a walled garden, an orchard of heritage apple varieties planted in 1998, and a small boathouse used by the college boat club. A footbridge, the Thwaite Bridge, links the college to the university's science campus on the western bank.

= = Organisation = =

The college is governed by its Council, comprising the principal, the fellows and representatives of students and staff. Day-to-day administration is overseen by the principal, assisted by a senior tutor, a bursar and a dean of students. The college is financially autonomous within the university, deriving its income from an endowment of approximately £58 million (2024), student rents, conference trade and donations.

= = Student life = =

Undergraduates belong to the Junior Common Room (JCR) and postgraduates to the Middle Common Room (MCR). The JCR operates a bar in the basement of the North Range, known as the Malt Cellar, and organises the college's principal social event, the Halloran Ball, held in alternate years in June.

The college boat club, founded in 1882, competes in the university's annual bumps races on the Wenn and has held the headship of the men's first division on six occasions, most recently in 2017. Other active societies include the Thwaite Society, a debating club founded in 1891; the Halloran Players, a drama society that stages an open-air production in the walled garden each summer; and the Eskdale Society, which organises geological field trips.

A long-standing tradition is the "Lantern Walk," held on the first Friday of Michaelmas term, in which new students carry paper lanterns from the college gate to the chapel, a reference to the college motto. The custom is believed to date from the 1920s.

Ruth Holgate

Professor Ruth Evelyn Holgate FRS (14 March 1912 – 2 September 1998) was an English palaeobotanist known for her research into the flora of Carboniferous coal swamps and for her reconstructions of early lycopsid trees. She spent most of her career at the University of Aldmere, where she was the first woman to hold an established chair in the natural sciences.

= = Early life and education = =

Holgate was born in Burnley, Lancashire, the second of three children of Albert Holgate, a cotton-mill overlooker, and his wife Florence (née Pickup). She attended Burnley High School for Girls, where her interest in fossils was encouraged by a science mistress who took pupils to collect plant remains from colliery spoil heaps.

In 1930 she won a county scholarship to read natural sciences at the University of Aldmere. As women were not then admitted to Halloran College as undergraduates, she was a non-collegiate student, but she was attached to Halloran for tuition through its geology fellow, Ronald Eskdale, who supervised her undergraduate dissertation on fossil seed ferns from the Lancashire coalfield. She graduated with first-class honours in 1933 and remained at Aldmere for doctoral research, completing her PhD in 1937. Halloran later counted her among its members, and she was elected an honorary fellow in 1971, the year the college first admitted women undergraduates.

= = Career = =

After a year as a research assistant at the British Museum (Natural History) in London, Holgate returned to Aldmere in 1938 as an assistant lecturer in geology. During the Second World War she worked for the Geological Survey on the assessment of coal reserves in the northern coalfields, an experience that gave her unusual access to freshly exposed mine workings and their fossil content.

Her most significant work concerned the reconstruction of whole plants from fragmentary fossils. Coal-measure plants are typically preserved as separate organs, such as bark, cones, roots and leaves, which had historically been given different scientific names. Through careful study of coal balls, nodules of mineral in which plant tissue is preserved in cellular detail, Holgate demonstrated connections between several previously separate form-genera. Her 1952 paper establishing the attachment of a particular cone type to a lycopsid trunk is regarded as a landmark in the field.

She was promoted to reader in 1955 and appointed to a personal chair in palaeobotany in 1963. She was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1968. Her textbook *Plants of the Coal Forests* (1966; revised edition 1981) was widely used in British universities for two decades.

= = Later life = =

Holgate retired in 1977 but continued to publish until the late 1980s. She never married and lived for much of her later life in a cottage near Grisedale, close to the field station her former tutor had founded. She bequeathed her collection of some 4,000 thin sections and specimens to the university, where it forms the core of the Holgate Collection in the Department of Earth Sciences. She died in Aldmere in 1998, aged 86.

* Lyell Medal of the Geological Society of London (1973)
* Honorary Fellow, Halloran College, Aldmere (1971)

Clara Mewburn

Clara Jane Mewburn (22 November 1940 – 17 April 2009) was an English novelist and short-story writer. Her work, set largely in the coastal villages and industrial towns of the North East of England, was noted for its spare prose and its attention to the lives of working women. Her best-known novel, *The Salt Road* (1972), was shortlisted for a major national fiction prize and was adapted for BBC television in 1985.

= = Early life = =

Mewburn was born in Hartlepool, County Durham, the only child of a ship's engineer and a primary-school teacher. Her father was frequently at sea, and she later described a childhood spent largely among her mother's sisters, several of whom worked in the town's fish-processing trade. She attended a local grammar school and in 1959 went to the University of Aldmere to study English, as a member of St Hild's Hall. In 1963 she moved to Halloran College to undertake postgraduate research on the regional novel in the nineteenth century, one of the first women admitted to the college's graduate community. She did not complete her thesis, leaving in 1965 to take up a post as a reporter on a regional newspaper.

= = Writing career = =

Mewburn's first published fiction, a collection of short stories titled *Low Water* (1968), drew on her experience as a journalist covering courts and inquests on Teesside. It received favourable notices but sold modestly.

*The Salt Road* (1972) follows three generations of women in a fictional salt-panning community on the Durham coast, from the 1890s to the closure of the works in the 1950s. Critics praised its unsentimental depiction of labour and family life, and it established Mewburn's reputation. Subsequent novels included *Harbour Lights* (1976), *A Winter Census* (1981) and *The Ferryman's Daughter* (1990). Her final novel, *North Light*, was published posthumously in 2010.

Mewburn was a writer-in-residence at Halloran College from 1984 to 1986, during which she established an annual short-story prize for students of the university, still awarded under the name of the Mewburn Prize.

= = Style and reception = =

Reviewers frequently compared Mewburn's work to that of earlier regional realists, though she resisted the label, stating in a 1993 interview that she wrote "about places, not regions." Her prose was characterised by short declarative sentences, sparse dialogue and close observation of physical work. Some critics found her later novels repetitive in theme, while others regarded *A Winter Census* as her most accomplished book.

= = Personal life and death = =

Mewburn married the schoolteacher David Rowntree in 1970; they had two sons. The couple lived in Staithes, North Yorkshire, from 1978 until her death. She died of cancer at a hospice in Middlesbrough in 2009, aged 68. Her papers are held by the library of Halloran College.
