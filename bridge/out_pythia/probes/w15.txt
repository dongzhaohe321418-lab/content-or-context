Anton Velisar Drăgulescu

Anton Velisar Drăgulescu (14 March 1831 – 2 November 1897) was a Romanian-born composer, organist, and teacher who spent most of his career in Vienna and Graz. He is best known for his cycle of six symphonies, the oratorio *The Lament of the Danube*, and a large body of piano and chamber music that combined Central European formal procedures with melodic material drawn from the folk music of Wallachia and Transylvania. Although widely performed during his lifetime, his music fell into relative obscurity after the First World War and was revived in the late twentieth century through a series of recordings and critical editions.

= = Early life = =

Drăgulescu was born in the town of Râmnicu Vâlcea in the principality of Wallachia, the third of five children of Ioan Drăgulescu, a cantor at the local Orthodox church, and Elena (née Popescu), the daughter of a cloth merchant. His father taught him the rudiments of Byzantine chant notation and the church modes, an influence that scholars have traced in the modal inflections of his later melodic writing. A visiting Austrian schoolmaster, Friedrich Leitner, gave the boy his first lessons on the piano and introduced him to the keyboard works of Johann Sebastian Bach and Joseph Haydn.

In 1844 the family moved to Bucharest, where Drăgulescu studied at the Saint Sava College. He took harmony lessons privately with the Bohemian musician Josef Hrabě, then the director of music at a German-language theatre in the city. Hrabě recognised his pupil's ability and encouraged the family to send him abroad. With financial assistance from a local boyar, Constantin Ghica, Drăgulescu travelled to Vienna in the autumn of 1849.

= = Education in Vienna = =

In Vienna Drăgulescu enrolled as a private student of the theorist Simon Sechter, with whom he studied counterpoint and figured bass for nearly four years. Sechter's rigorous method, based on the fundamental bass theories of Jean-Philippe Rameau, left a permanent mark on the young composer. Drăgulescu later described these years as "a long apprenticeship in obedience, from which I emerged able to disobey intelligently."

During this period he supported himself as an organist at the Greek Orthodox Church of the Holy Trinity and as a piano accompanist for singing teachers. His earliest surviving compositions date from 1851 and include a set of twelve fugues for organ, a string quartet in D minor, and several liturgical pieces in Church Slavonic and Romanian. The string quartet, later published as his Opus 1, was performed privately at the home of the publisher Carl Haslinger in 1853 and received a favourable notice in a Viennese music periodical.

= = Career = =

= = = Early career (1854–1866) = = =

In 1854 Drăgulescu was appointed second Kapellmeister at the Theater in der Josefstadt, a post that required him to arrange, conduct, and occasionally compose incidental music for spoken plays and light operettas. Although he regarded the work as drudgery, it gave him extensive practical experience with the orchestra. His first orchestral work to attract wider attention, the *Wallachian Overture* (1857), was written during this period and was taken up by several provincial orchestras in Austria and Bohemia.

His Symphony No. 1 in C minor was completed in 1859 and premiered the following year by the orchestra of the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde under the direction of Johann Herbeck. The work was received politely but without enthusiasm; critics praised its contrapuntal craftsmanship but found its slow movement, based on a Romanian funeral lament, "foreign in sentiment." Drăgulescu revised the symphony twice, in 1866 and 1881, and the final version is the one usually performed today.

In 1862 he married Theresia Bauer, the daughter of a Viennese piano maker. The couple had four children, two of whom survived to adulthood. Their son, Emil Drăgulescu (1865–1931), became a noted violinist and later edited several of his father's chamber works for publication.

= = = Graz period (1866–1884) = = =

In 1866 Drăgulescu accepted the position of music director of the Styrian Music Society (Steiermärkischer Musikverein) in Graz, an appointment that gave him control over a choir, an amateur orchestra, and a music school. The relative stability of the post allowed him to devote more time to composition, and the eighteen years he spent in Graz are generally regarded as the most productive of his career.

During this period he composed his Symphonies Nos. 2, 3, and 4, the Piano Concerto in E-flat major, two string quartets, a piano quintet, and numerous choral works for the society's singers. He also undertook a systematic collection of folk melodies during summer journeys to Transylvania and Wallachia, transcribing more than four hundred songs and dances between 1868 and 1879. Many of these transcriptions were later published as *Melodii populare române* (1882), one of the earliest printed collections of its kind, though modern ethnomusicologists have noted that Drăgulescu frequently regularised irregular rhythms and harmonised melodies according to Western conventions.

The Symphony No. 3 in A major, known as the "Carpathian", premiered in Graz in 1874 and became his most frequently performed orchestral work during his lifetime. It was conducted in Leipzig, Prague, Budapest, and London over the following decade. The Piano Concerto, first performed in 1877 with the composer's friend Julius Epstein as soloist, was also widely played.

= = = Return to Vienna and later years (1884–1897) = = =

In 1884 Drăgulescu returned to Vienna to take up a professorship in composition and counterpoint at the Conservatory of the Gesellschaft der Musikfreunde. Among his students were several composers who later achieved prominence in Austria and Romania, including the Romanian composer Dimitrie Stănescu and the Austrian song composer Karl Feiningerl.

His late works became increasingly introspective. The oratorio *The Lament of the Danube* (*Plângerea Dunării*), to a text by the Romanian poet Vasile Alecsandri, was completed in 1888 and premiered in Bucharest in 1889 at a concert attended by King Carol I. The performance was regarded as a national event, and Drăgulescu was awarded the Order of the Star of Romania. His Symphony No. 5 in F minor (1890) and Symphony No. 6 in B-flat major (1895) are often considered his finest orchestral achievements.

Drăgulescu suffered from a heart condition during his final years. He died in Vienna on 2 November 1897 and was buried in the Zentralfriedhof. In 1934 his remains were transferred to the Bellu Cemetery in Bucharest at the request of the Romanian government.

= = Music = =

= = = Style = = =

Drăgulescu's musical language is rooted in the Austro-German tradition of the mid-nineteenth century. His formal procedures owe much to the Viennese Classical composers and to Felix Mendelssohn and Robert Schumann, and his thorough contrapuntal training is evident in the frequent use of fugato passages, canonic imitation, and elaborate development sections.

His distinctive contribution lies in the integration of melodic and rhythmic elements from Romanian folk and church music into this framework. Characteristic features include the use of the augmented second, modal cadences derived from Orthodox chant, drone basses imitating bagpipes, and dance rhythms such as the *hora* and the *sârba*. Unlike some later nationalist composers, Drăgulescu rarely quoted folk tunes directly in his larger works, preferring to write original melodies in a folk-like idiom. Musicologist Irina Vlădescu has described his method as "a translation rather than a transcription of the village into the concert hall."

His orchestration is generally restrained, favouring the classical orchestra with the occasional addition of a third trombone, harp, or tuba. He made frequent use of solo woodwinds, especially the oboe and clarinet, to present folk-inflected themes.

= = = Symphonies = = =

The six symphonies form the core of Drăgulescu's reputation.

* **Symphony No. 1 in C minor, Op. 12** (1859; revised 1866 and 1881) – a four-movement work whose slow movement is a funeral march based on the rhythmic patterns of the Romanian *bocet* (lament).
* **Symphony No. 2 in D major, Op. 21** (1868) – a lighter, pastoral work sometimes compared to the symphonies of Schumann.
* **Symphony No. 3 in A major, "Carpathian", Op. 28** (1873) – his most popular symphony, notable for its scherzo in the rhythm of a *hora* and its finale built on a mountain shepherd's tune.
* **Symphony No. 4 in G minor, Op. 35** (1880) – a dramatic work in three movements, the finale of which incorporates a passacaglia on an eight-bar theme.
* **Symphony No. 5 in F minor, Op. 47** (1890) – written shortly after the death of his daughter Maria, it is the most sombre of the cycle and ends quietly in the minor key.
* **Symphony No. 6 in B-flat major, Op. 53** (1895) – a large-scale work with an extended slow introduction and a chorale-based finale, regarded by many critics as the summation of his symphonic style.

= = = Other orchestral and concertante works = = =

Besides the symphonies, Drăgulescu wrote several concert overtures, including the *Wallachian Overture* (1857) and *Overture to a Tragedy* (1871), as well as the symphonic poem *Mircea the Elder* (1886), based on the life of the fourteenth-century Wallachian prince. His Piano Concerto in E-flat major, Op. 31 (1876), and Violin Concerto in D minor, Op. 40 (1883), remained in the repertoire of several virtuosos until the early twentieth century. The Violin Concerto was dedicated to his son Emil, who performed it frequently.

= = = Choral and vocal music = = =

Drăgulescu's choral output is extensive and includes music for both the Orthodox liturgy and the concert hall. The *Liturgy of Saint John Chrysostom* for unaccompanied mixed choir (1872) was one of the first settings of the liturgy in the Romanian language to employ Western four-part harmony, and it was widely used in Romanian churches for several decades. *The Lament of the Danube* (1888), for soloists, chorus, and orchestra, is his largest single work and is divided into three parts depicting the river's course from its source to the Black Sea.

He also composed more than eighty songs for voice and piano, setting texts in German and Romanian by poets including Heinrich Heine, Nikolaus Lenau, Mihai Eminescu, and Alecsandri.

= = = Chamber and piano music = = =

The chamber works include four string quartets, a piano quintet in F minor, two piano trios, and sonatas for violin and for cello. The Piano Quintet, Op. 33 (1878), is generally considered his finest chamber composition and has been recorded several times since the 1980s.

His piano music ranges from didactic pieces written for students to large-scale concert works. The most significant are the two sets of *Romanian Dances* (1870 and 1885), the Sonata in B minor (1863), and the *Twelve Preludes and Fugues* (1892), which reflect his lifelong study of Bach.

= = = Opera = = =

Drăgulescu attempted opera only once. *Ana Lugojana*, a three-act opera on a libretto by the Viennese writer Ludwig Hartmann, was completed in 1879 but performed only twice, in Graz in 1880, before being withdrawn. The composer considered the libretto weak and planned a revision that he never completed. The opera was given its first modern staging in Cluj-Napoca in 1997, on the centenary of his death.

= = Reception and legacy = =

During his lifetime Drăgulescu enjoyed a respectable reputation in Austria and a considerable one in Romania, where he was regarded as the first composer of the country to achieve recognition abroad. The critic Eduard Hanslick, who reviewed several of his premieres in Vienna, praised his "solid workmanship and honest feeling" while expressing reservations about what he considered the "exotic coloration" of his themes.

After his death his music was gradually displaced from Austrian concert programmes by that of younger composers. In Romania, however, his works continued to be performed, and he was frequently cited as a forerunner of George Enescu and the national school of the early twentieth century. Enescu himself is reported to have conducted the "Carpathian" Symphony in Bucharest in 1912 and to have described Drăgulescu as "the man who first opened the door."

Interest in his music declined in the mid-twentieth century, partly because the communist authorities in Romania favoured composers whose work could more easily be presented as folk-based and popular. A revival began in the 1980s, when the conductor Horia Andreescu recorded the six symphonies with the George Enescu Philharmonic Orchestra. A critical edition of his complete works, prepared by the Romanian Academy in collaboration with Austrian musicologists, began publication in 1999 and had reached twenty-two volumes by 2020.

Several institutions bear his name, including a music high school in Râmnicu Vâlcea and a chamber music competition held biennially in Graz since 2005. A bronze statue of the composer by the sculptor Ion Jalea was unveiled in Bucharest in 1947, and a commemorative plaque marks his former residence in the Josefstadt district of Vienna.
