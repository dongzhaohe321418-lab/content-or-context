**St Withburga's Church, Calthorpe Mallory**

St Withburga's Church is an Anglican parish church in the village of Calthorpe Mallory, in the English county of Norfolk. It stands on a low rise at the northern edge of the village, about 9 miles (14 km) south-west of Norwich, overlooking the shallow valley of the River Tiffey. The church is notable for its round flint tower, which may date in its lower stages from the late eleventh century, and for a substantial fifteenth-century rebuilding funded largely by the wool trade. It is a Grade I listed building and is one of a small number of English churches dedicated to Saint Withburga, the seventh-century East Anglian princess and abbess associated with the town of Dereham.

The parish forms part of the Benefice of Upper Tiffey, in the Deanery of Humbleyard and the Diocese of Norwich. Services are held fortnightly, and the building is open to visitors during daylight hours.

= = History = =

= = = Origins = = =

The earliest documentary reference to a church at Calthorpe Mallory occurs in the Domesday Book of 1086. It records a church with 12 acres of glebe land within the holdings of Ralph de Bellofago. The Domesday entry calls the settlement simply *Caltorp*. The suffix "Mallory" was added in the thirteenth century from the Malori family, who held the manor from around 1240 until the late fourteenth century.

Antiquarians of the nineteenth century claimed that a timber church had stood on the site before the Norman Conquest. They pointed to a fragment of carved stone, decorated with interlace, which was found in the churchyard wall in 1871 and is now kept in the north aisle. Later scholars have been more cautious. A survey published by the Norfolk Archaeological Unit in 1989 described the fragment as "probably pre-Conquest, but not necessarily from this site". It noted that building stone was often carried some distance in a county with little natural freestone.

The local tradition connecting the dedication to Withburga holds that her body rested overnight at Calthorpe in 974, when monks from Ely carried it away from Dereham. No early source supports this story. It first appears in a parish memorandum book of 1682.

= = = Norman and early medieval period = = =

The round tower is the oldest part of the standing fabric. It is built of coursed flint rubble with some reused Roman brick. The lowest stage has two narrow, round-headed windows with splayed jambs, and the tower arch into the nave is plain and unmoulded. Both features are consistent with a date in the decades either side of 1100. The nave walls may survive in part from the same period. A blocked round-headed doorway can be traced on the north side, partly hidden by the later aisle.

The chancel was rebuilt in the second half of the thirteenth century. Evidence for this includes a pair of lancet windows on the north side and a double piscina with intersecting arches, of a type common in Norfolk in about 1270 to 1290. The Malori family are thought to have paid for the work. A worn effigy of a knight in chain mail, now in a recess in the chancel north wall, is traditionally identified as Sir Hamo Malori, who died around 1294.

In 1328 the advowson, the right to nominate the rector, passed to the Augustinian priory of Westacre. The priory appropriated the living, and from then on the parish was served by vicars. Westacre kept the patronage until the Dissolution.

= = = Fifteenth-century rebuilding = = =

The church was largely remodelled between about 1430 and 1490, the period of greatest prosperity for the cloth-producing villages of central Norfolk. Surviving wills in the Norwich Consistory Court record a series of bequests to the work:

* In 1437 John Bocking, a worsted weaver, left 40 shillings "to the new making of the yle on the south side".
* In 1452 Agnes Pell left money for the glazing of a window "in the honour of Seynt Withburgh".
* In 1468 Robert Tyler gave 6s 8d "to the battylment of the stepill".

The octagonal belfry stage was added to the round tower during this campaign. It is built of knapped flint with limestone dressings and has four two-light bell openings and a parapet with flushwork panels. The south aisle and south porch were built first. A north aisle followed in about 1460 to 1475, and a clerestory of seven closely spaced windows on each side was added to the nave. A new arch-braced roof with carved angels on the hammer-ends completed the work. A rood screen was installed in about 1485, and its painted dado partly survives.

The two-storey south porch is the most elaborate part of this work. Its front has flushwork panelling, a canopied niche above the entrance, and spandrels carved with a wodewose and a lion. The upper room, reached by a stair turret in the angle with the aisle, is thought to have served as a priest's lodging or a parish store.

= = = Reformation and seventeenth century = = =

The churchwardens' accounts survive from 1547. They record the removal of the rood loft in 1561 and the whitewashing of the walls. They also note the sale of "olde vestmentes and a crosse of latyn" for 14 shillings. Images on the screen panels were defaced at about this time, although the figures were not entirely scraped away.

In 1644 the church was visited by William Dowsing's deputies, acting under the parliamentary ordinance against "superstitious" images. According to a note in the accounts, they broke "xx pictures in the glasse" and ordered the removal of inscriptions asking for prayers for the dead. The angels on the nave roof escaped damage, apparently because they could not easily be reached. A board painted with the royal arms of Charles II, dated 1661, hangs above the north door.

= = = Eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries = = =

The fabric was poorly maintained for much of the eighteenth century. An archdeacon's visitation of 1752 reported that the chancel roof "lets in the rain" and that the north aisle was "used for the keeping of the parish fire engine". In 1784 the lead was stripped from the north aisle roof, sold, and replaced with pantiles. A west gallery was built in 1803 to hold the parish band, which accompanied services with clarinet, bassoon and bass viol until about 1850.

The longest-serving incumbent of this period was the Reverend Thomas Gedge, vicar from 1791 to 1838. He kept detailed meteorological notes in the margins of the parish registers. Extracts from these were published by the Norfolk and Norwich Naturalists' Society in 1912.

= = = Victorian restoration = = =

A thorough restoration took place between 1867 and 1871 under the architect Richard Makilwaine Phipson, who was then surveyor to the Diocese of Norwich. The work was funded mainly by the Reverend Henry Fellowes, vicar from 1862 to 1894, together with his brother-in-law, the Norwich brewer Edward Steward Lacon. The restoration involved:

* removing the west gallery and the box pews;
* reroofing the north aisle in lead;
* rebuilding the chancel east wall with a new three-light window in the Decorated style;
* laying encaustic tiles in the chancel;
* installing new oak benches, which incorporate several medieval bench ends.

Phipson's work was criticised in the twentieth century for the loss of plaster and of a medieval wall painting. A description from 1859 records that the painting showed Saint Christopher and was on the north wall of the nave. Only a small fragment survives near the north door. Phipson was, however, credited with saving the tower. Its octagonal stage had developed a serious crack, which he stabilised with iron ties.

= = = Twentieth century to the present = = =

A war memorial tablet listing fourteen men of the parish killed in the First World War was unveiled in the south aisle in 1920. Four names were added after the Second World War. Electric lighting was installed in 1934, and the church was heated by electricity from 1962.

In 1958 the rood screen was cleaned and conserved by the Council for the Care of Churches. During this work faint inscriptions naming the donors were uncovered on the dado rail. In 1976 the parish joined with neighbouring Swardeston Parva and Little Haverham to form a united benefice. The present Benefice of Upper Tiffey, comprising seven parishes, was created in 2003.

The church was listed at Grade I in 1953. Major repairs to the tower and the clerestory stonework were carried out in 2011 and 2012, with a grant from English Heritage and support from the Norfolk Churches Trust. In 2019 a kitchen and an accessible toilet were fitted into the western bay of the north aisle, behind a glazed oak screen.

= = Architecture = =

= = = Exterior = = =

The church consists of the round west tower with its octagonal belfry, a nave with clerestory, north and south aisles, a south porch, and a chancel. The main walling material is flint rubble, with knapped and squared flint used for the fifteenth-century work. Dressings are of Barnack and Clipsham limestone, the latter mostly dating from the 1867 to 1871 restoration and from twentieth-century repairs.

The round tower has an external diameter of about 5.2 metres (17 ft) at its base. Its walls are roughly 1.2 metres (3 ft 11 in) thick at ground level. The circular portion rises to about 11 metres (36 ft). The octagonal stage above it brings the total height, including the parapet, to approximately 18 metres (59 ft). A small west window was inserted in the lowest stage in the fifteenth century and has a single cinquefoiled light.

The south aisle has three-light Perpendicular windows with panel tracery. The north aisle windows are of a simpler, slightly later design with square heads. Both aisles have plain parapets. The clerestory windows are of two lights and alternate with flushwork panels, giving the upper walls of the nave a chequered appearance that is visible from some distance across the valley.

The chancel is lower than the nave and has a pantiled roof, which was restored in 1998. A priest's door on the south side has a hood mould with stops carved as human heads. Local tradition calls them "the vicar and his wife".

= = = Interior = = =

The nave is separated from each aisle by an arcade of four bays. The piers are octagonal, with moulded capitals, and the arches are double-chamfered. The north arcade is slightly taller than the south arcade, and its capitals carry a band of carved fleurons.

The nave roof is a single hammerbeam construction with arched braces. Sixteen carved angels with outspread wings project from the hammer-ends. Some hold shields, and others hold musical instruments, including a lute, a shawm and a portative organ. The wall posts rest on stone corbels carved as heads. Restorers in 2012 found traces of red and green paint on the angels' robes.

The chancel arch is fourteenth-century and has two continuous chamfers. A blocked rood stair can be seen in the north respond.

= = Fittings and furnishings = =

= = = Font = = =

The font is of the East Anglian "Seven Sacrament" type and dates from about 1470. Its octagonal bowl has carved panels showing seven sacraments of the medieval Church: baptism, confirmation, the Mass, penance, ordination, matrimony and extreme unction. The eighth panel shows the Baptism of Christ. All the figures were mutilated in the sixteenth or seventeenth century, but the compositions remain legible. The stem is carved with seated figures of the four Evangelists. The font stands on two steps, the upper one in the shape of a Maltese cross. A tall cover in the Gothic Revival style was given in 1871.

= = = Rood screen = = =

The rood screen spans the full width of the nave and has eight painted panels in its dado. Four figures stand on either side of the central opening:

* north side: Saint Withburga holding a model of a church, Saint Etheldreda, Saint Margaret and Saint Apollonia;
* south side: Saint Edmund, Saint Laurence, Saint Stephen and Saint John the Evangelist.

The faces of most figures were scratched out at the Reformation, but the robes keep much of their original gilding and gesso patterning. The partial inscription on the rail, revealed in 1958, reads "...pray for the soulys of Wyllm Pell and Kateryn his wyf...". This is taken to show that the screen was paid for by members of the Pell family, who were prominent in the parish in the later fifteenth century. The upper tracery was largely renewed in 1870.

= = = Benches = = =

Eleven medieval bench ends survive among the Victorian seating in the nave. They have poppyhead finials. Several are carved on the arm rests with animals, including a hare, a pelican feeding her young with her own blood, a dog with a bone, and a creature identified in a church guide of 1931 as a "unicorn, much decayed". One bench end in the south aisle carries the initials "I B" and a merchant's mark. It has been tentatively associated with John Bocking, the donor recorded in 1437.

= = = Stained glass = = =

Only fragments of medieval glass survive. They were gathered in the tracery lights of the south aisle east window in 1869 and include a crowned head, part of a figure of Saint Withburga with a doe, and several pieces of quarry glass with a crowned "M" monogram.

The chancel east window contains glass of 1871 by Clayton and Bell showing the Crucifixion, with the Virgin Mary and Saint John. The north aisle has a window of 1904 by James Powell and Sons, given in memory of the Reverend Henry Fellowes. In 1995 a small window by the Norfolk glass artist Ruth Penrose was installed in the west wall of the south aisle. It depicts the Tiffey valley in an abstract design of blues and greens and marks the bicentenary of the parish school.

= = = Monuments = = =

The chancel contains several memorials:

* The thirteenth-century effigy of a knight, presumed to be Sir Hamo Malori, in a recess with a cusped arch.
* A brass of about 1510 showing a civilian and his wife, with inscriptions to Thomas and Margery Rede. The figures of four sons and three daughters are lost, but their indents remain in the slab.
* A marble wall monument to Sir Francis Wodehouse Gawdy, who died in 1709, and his wife Anne. It has a pair of weeping cherubs and an urn, and has been attributed to the workshop of Robert Singleton of Norwich.
* A tablet to the Reverend Thomas Gedge, who died in 1838, with an inscription praising his "unwearied attention to the duties of his station, and to the phenomena of the heavens".

= = = Other fittings = = =

The pulpit is a Jacobean piece of carved oak, dated 1617 on its sounding board, and stands on a modern base. A parish chest of about 1400 is bound with iron straps and has three locks. The holders of the keys, traditionally the vicar and the two churchwardens, had to be present together to open it. A set of benefaction boards in the tower records charitable gifts to the poor of the parish between 1612 and 1840.

= = Bells = =

The tower contains a ring of five bells, hung in an oak frame of 1764. The oldest bell, the fourth, was cast in about 1420 at the Norwich foundry of Richard Brasyer. It carries the inscription *Sancta Withburga Ora Pro Nobis*. The other bells are:

* treble, recast by John Taylor & Co of Loughborough in 1953;
* second, cast by John Draper of Thetford in 1614;
* third, cast by Thomas Newman in 1712;
* tenor, cast by Thomas Osborn of Downham Market in 1789, weighing about 9 long hundredweight.

The bells were rehung on ball bearings in 1953 but were declared unsafe to ring full circle in 1987 because of movement in the octagonal belfry. After the tower repairs of 2011 to 2012 the bells were rehung in a new steel frame below the old oak one, which was left in place. They were rededicated by the Bishop of Thetford in May 2013 and are rung for Sunday services and special occasions by a band shared with neighbouring parishes.

= = Churchyard = =

The churchyard covers about 0.6 hectares (1.5 acres) and is enclosed by a flint wall on its south and west sides. The rest of the boundary is a hedge of hawthorn and field maple. The base and part of the shaft of a medieval churchyard cross stand south of the porch. The cross head was replaced with a simple stone cross in 1921.

Several eighteenth-century headstones are carved with cherubs, skulls and hourglasses. A chest tomb near the chancel commemorates members of the Pell family between 1693 and 1772. Two Commonwealth war graves are recorded in the churchyard: one for a soldier of the Norfolk Regiment who died in 1918, and one for an airman of the Royal Air Force who died in 1943 while serving at a nearby airfield.

Since 2005 the northern part of the churchyard has been managed as a conservation area in partnership with the Norfolk Wildlife Trust. Surveys have recorded more than 120 species of flowering plant, among them meadow saxifrage, cowslip and adder's-tongue fern. Lichens on the older headstones and the churchyard wall include several species uncommon in lowland Norfolk.

= = Parish and present-day use = =

The ecclesiastical parish of Calthorpe Mallory had a population of 412 at the 2021 census. The church is maintained by a parochial church council and is supported by a Friends of St Withburga's group, founded in 2008, which raises money for the fabric through concerts, an annual flower festival held in June, and an open gardens weekend.

The church hosts a patronal festival on or near 17 March, the feast of Saint Withburga, and a harvest supper in the village hall each October. It takes part in the Norfolk Churches Trust's annual sponsored bicycle ride. In 2016 it was included in a regional heritage trail of round-tower churches.
