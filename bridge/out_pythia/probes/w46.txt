Edmund Halloway Crane (14 March 1791 – 9 November 1834) was an English poet, essayist and translator associated with the second generation of the Romantic movement. Born in the market town of Ludlow in Shropshire, he is best known for his long narrative poem *The Severn Hours* (1823), the lyric sequence *Songs of the Marches* (1819), and the posthumously published verse drama *Alderic*. His work is noted for its close attention to the landscape and dialect of the Welsh borderlands, for its experiments with irregular stanza forms, and for a recurring preoccupation with the relationship between memory and place. Although he enjoyed modest critical success during his lifetime, Crane's reputation declined after his early death and was substantially revived only in the twentieth century, when scholars began to regard him as an important minor figure linking the rural poetics of the early Romantics with the later Victorian interest in regional identity.

= = Early life = =

Crane was born at a house on Broad Street, Ludlow, the second of five children of Thomas Crane, a glover and leather merchant, and his wife Margaret (née Halloway), the daughter of a Herefordshire farmer. The family was comfortably prosperous during Crane's early childhood, but a downturn in the glove trade after 1797 led to financial difficulties, and in 1799 the family moved to a smaller property near the river Teme on the outskirts of the town.

Crane received his early education at Ludlow Grammar School, where he studied Latin and Greek under the Reverend Josiah Pell, a classicist who later recalled the boy as "quiet, ungovernable in his reading, and indifferent to all instruction that did not arrive in verse". Crane's surviving schoolboy notebooks contain translations of Horace and Virgil alongside imitations of the poetry of William Cowper and Thomas Gray. According to his sister Anne, whose recollections were collected by the antiquarian Samuel Brough in the 1850s, Crane spent much of his adolescence walking in the hills of south Shropshire and the adjoining parts of Radnorshire, and he habitually recorded local place names, folk songs and fragments of Welsh speech in a pocketbook.

In 1808 Crane's father died, leaving the family with considerable debts. Crane, then aged seventeen, abandoned plans to attend university and took a position as a clerk in the office of a Shrewsbury solicitor, Richard Ambler. He remained in Shrewsbury for nearly five years, during which time he began to write poetry in earnest and to submit verses anonymously to provincial periodicals, including the *Salopian Journal* and the *Hereford Independent*.

= = Literary career = =

= = = London and first publications = = =

In 1813 Crane moved to London, reportedly with a letter of introduction from Ambler to the publisher Joseph Whitlow of Paternoster Row. Whitlow employed him as a reader and occasional reviewer, and in 1814 published Crane's first collection, *Poems, Chiefly Pastoral*, in an edition of 500 copies. The volume, consisting largely of conventional odes and descriptive pieces in heroic couplets, received brief and generally polite notices but sold poorly. Crane himself later dismissed it as "the exercises of an apprentice who had not yet found his tools".

During his early years in London, Crane lodged in Pentonville and supported himself through journalism, contributing essays on rural customs and reviews of contemporary poetry to several magazines. Through Whitlow he came into contact with a circle of writers and artists that included the engraver Henry Vane, the essayist Matthew Orde, and the poet Caroline Dessiter, with whom he maintained a long correspondence. Orde, in particular, encouraged Crane to abandon the Augustan models of his first volume and to write in a manner closer to the speech of his native region.

= = = Songs of the Marches = = =

Crane's second collection, *Songs of the Marches*, was published by Whitlow in 1819. It consisted of forty-two short lyrics, many of them written in a loose ballad measure and incorporating dialect words from Shropshire and the Welsh border. The sequence is loosely organised around the cycle of the agricultural year and includes poems on sheep-washing, harvest suppers, hiring fairs and the custom of "souling" on All Souls' Eve. Several poems, among them "The Drover's Road" and "At Clun in Winter", deal with the depopulation of upland farms and the movement of labourers to the industrial towns.

The collection attracted considerably more attention than its predecessor. A reviewer in the *Monthly Censor* praised its "unaffected truth to the lives of the humbler classes", while a notice in the *Edinburgh Quarterly* criticised its dialect vocabulary as "a barbarism which no glossary can redeem". The book went into a second edition in 1820, to which Crane added a glossary of some three hundred provincial words and a prefatory essay, "On the Language of Country People", in which he argued that regional speech preserved "the oldest music of the English tongue" and ought to be regarded as a legitimate medium for serious poetry. The essay has been cited by later scholars as an early statement of principles that would be developed more systematically by dialect poets of the mid-nineteenth century.

= = = The Severn Hours = = =

Between 1820 and 1823 Crane worked on his most ambitious poem, *The Severn Hours*, a narrative and meditative work of approximately 4,800 lines in twelve books. The poem follows an unnamed speaker on a journey along the river Severn from its source on Plynlimon to its estuary below Gloucester, with each book corresponding to a single hour of a summer day. The journey is interwoven with recollections of the speaker's childhood, episodes from local history and legend, and encounters with figures including a ferryman, a coracle maker, a recruiting sergeant and a dying woman at Upton.

Crane composed the poem in an unusual stanza of his own devising, consisting of nine lines of varying length with a flexible rhyme scheme, which he described in a letter to Dessiter as "a form that may widen and narrow as the river does". Critics have compared the stanza both to the Spenserian stanza and to the irregular odes of Abraham Cowley, though Crane's handling of it is generally considered distinctive.

*The Severn Hours* was published in two volumes in the spring of 1823 and was widely reviewed. Opinion was divided. Supporters admired its descriptive passages, particularly the depiction of the Ironbridge Gorge in the fourth book, in which the furnaces of Coalbrookdale are described at dusk as "a second sunset struck from stone". Detractors found the poem diffuse and its structure arbitrary; one reviewer remarked that "the reader, like the river, is carried a great distance without being certain of having arrived anywhere". Sales were moderate, and Crane received approximately £120 for the copyright, a sum that allowed him to reduce his journalistic work for a period.

= = = Later work = = =

In 1824 Crane married Eleanor Marsh, the daughter of a Worcester bookseller, and the couple settled in a rented cottage at Malvern Wells, Worcestershire. They had three children, of whom two survived infancy. The years at Malvern were among the most productive of Crane's life. He published a volume of essays, *Border Sketches* (1826), describing rural life in the counties along the Welsh border, and a translation of selected poems by the medieval Welsh poet Dafydd ap Gwilym (1828), prepared with the assistance of a Welsh-speaking clergyman, the Reverend Evan Probert of Knighton. The translation was among the earliest substantial English renderings of Dafydd's work, and although later scholars identified numerous errors, it was influential in bringing Welsh medieval poetry to the attention of English readers.

Crane's third collection of original poetry, *The Lantern and Other Poems*, appeared in 1830. It is generally considered darker in tone than his earlier work, containing several elegies, including "For a Child Buried at Colwall", written after the death of his infant daughter Margaret in 1827, and a series of meditative sonnets on the theme of loss. The title poem, a blank verse narrative concerning a lamplighter in a manufacturing town, marked a departure from Crane's predominantly rural subject matter and has been read as a response to the social unrest of the late 1820s.

From around 1831 Crane worked on a verse drama, *Alderic*, set in the Welsh Marches during the reign of Henry II and concerning a Marcher lord divided between loyalty to the English crown and sympathy for his Welsh subjects. He completed a draft in 1833 but was dissatisfied with the fifth act and continued revising it until his death.

= = Illness and death = =

Crane's health had been uncertain since his early twenties, and from 1832 he suffered from a persistent cough and periods of fever which were later understood to have been symptoms of pulmonary tuberculosis. On medical advice he spent the winter of 1833–34 at Torquay in Devon, but his condition did not improve. He returned to Malvern Wells in the spring of 1834 and died there on 9 November of that year, aged forty-three. He was buried in the churchyard of St Peter's, Cowleigh, where a plain headstone bears a line from *The Severn Hours*: "The river keeps what the river was given."

Crane left his family in straitened circumstances. A subscription organised by Matthew Orde and Caroline Dessiter raised a sum for the support of his widow and children, and Whitlow agreed to publish a collected edition of his poems for their benefit.

= = Posthumous publication = =

*The Poetical Works of Edmund Halloway Crane*, edited by Orde, was published in three volumes in 1837. It included the text of *Alderic*, printed from Crane's final manuscript with the unfinished fifth act supplemented by Orde from earlier drafts, as well as some sixty previously unpublished lyrics and fragments. Orde's editorial practices were later criticised; in particular, he was found to have regularised much of Crane's dialect spelling and to have omitted several poems he considered politically or religiously indiscreet, among them the satirical "Address to a Tithe Proctor".

A more complete edition, based on Crane's surviving manuscripts in the possession of his son, Thomas Marsh Crane, was prepared by the scholar Henrietta Lowe and published in 1894. Lowe restored Crane's original spellings and included a number of letters, notebooks and early drafts. The manuscripts were subsequently deposited at the Bodleian Library, Oxford, in 1921.

*Alderic* was first staged in 1912 in an abridged version by an amateur company in Shrewsbury. A professional production was mounted at the Malvern Festival in 1937, marking the centenary of the play's publication.

= = Style and themes = =

Critics have frequently identified place as the central concern of Crane's poetry. Unlike poets who used landscape primarily as an occasion for personal reflection, Crane tended to treat specific locations as repositories of communal memory, in which the histories of ordinary people were preserved through names, customs and speech. The critic Arthur Fenwick, writing in 1931, described him as "a poet of parishes rather than of prospects", noting his preference for close, enclosed scenes over panoramic views.

Crane's use of dialect has received sustained scholarly attention. His insistence on regional vocabulary, and the arguments of his 1820 essay, have led some critics to regard him as a precursor of later dialect poets, though others have stressed that Crane used dialect selectively and within poems that were otherwise written in standard literary English. His formal experiments, especially the stanza of *The Severn Hours*, have also been discussed as evidence of a broader Romantic interest in organic form, in which the structure of a poem was intended to mirror its subject.

Several recurring themes have been identified in Crane's work, including the effects of economic change on rural communities, the persistence of the past in the present, and the borderland as both a geographical and a cultural condition. His later poems show an increasing concern with mortality and grief, which commentators have generally connected with the deaths in his family and with his own illness.

Crane's religious views appear to have been unorthodox. Although he was baptised and buried in the Church of England, his letters express scepticism about established religion and sympathy for Dissenters, and several of his poems are critical of the tithe system and of clergy whom he regarded as neglectful of their parishioners.

= = Relationships with contemporaries = =

Crane's correspondence indicates that he read widely among his contemporaries, and he expressed particular admiration for the early poetry of William Wordsworth, although he considered Wordsworth's later work "too much the sermon and too little the song". He met Charles Lamb on at least two occasions in London and corresponded briefly with John Clare, with whom he exchanged views on the use of provincial language in verse; three letters from Crane to Clare survive. His longest and most significant literary friendship was with Caroline Dessiter, whose letters to him, published in 1902, provide much of what is known about his working methods and his views on poetry.

= = Reception and legacy = =

During the middle decades of the nineteenth century Crane was little read outside the border counties, where his poems retained some local popularity and were occasionally anthologised in collections of regional verse. Henrietta Lowe's edition of 1894 prompted renewed interest, and in the early twentieth century his work was praised by several writers associated with the revival of interest in English rural life. A selection of his poems was included in a widely used school anthology of English verse in 1925, which brought "The Drover's Road" in particular to a wider audience.

Academic study of Crane expanded considerably from the 1960s onward, as scholars of Romanticism turned their attention to writers outside the established canon. His work has been examined in relation to the history of the English dialect movement, to Anglo-Welsh literary relations, and to the representation of industrialisation in Romantic poetry. A critical edition of *The Severn Hours*, with full textual apparatus, was published in 1988.

A memorial tablet to Crane was unveiled at Ludlow Grammar School in 1891, and a walking route following part of the course described in *The Severn Hours* was established by a local heritage society in 1984. The Edmund Crane Society, founded in Shrewsbury in 1972, publishes an annual journal devoted to his life and work and to the literature of the Welsh Marches.
