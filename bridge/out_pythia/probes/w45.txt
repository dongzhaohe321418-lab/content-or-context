St. Aldric's Cathedral, Varnholm

The Cathedral Church of Saint Aldric, commonly known as St. Aldric's Cathedral or Varnholm Cathedral, is a Christian cathedral in the town of Varnholm, in the river valley of the Else. It is the seat of the Bishop of Varnholm and the mother church of the diocese of the same name. The present building was begun in 1184 on the site of an earlier Romanesque minster and was substantially completed by 1347, although work on the western towers continued intermittently until 1521. It is regarded as one of the most complete examples of the transitional style between late Romanesque and early Gothic architecture in the region. It is noted in particular for its octagonal crossing lantern, its sequence of carved capitals in the choir, and its surviving fourteenth-century glazing scheme.

The cathedral is dedicated to Saint Aldric, a missionary bishop who, according to local tradition, founded a church on the site in the late eighth century. The building is protected as a listed monument and is an active place of worship. It also serves as a venue for concerts and civic ceremonies.

= = History = =

= = = Early foundations = = =

The earliest documentary reference to a church at Varnholm occurs in a charter of 812. The charter records a grant of fishing rights on the Else to "the brethren serving God and Saint Aldric at Varnholm". Archaeological investigations beneath the nave floor in 1961 and 1962 uncovered the footings of a small single-cell church with an apsidal east end. Its walls were built of roughly coursed river cobbles set in lime mortar. Radiocarbon dating of charcoal from a hearth beneath its floor gave a range of 760 to 850. This is broadly consistent with the traditional foundation date, although no direct link between the structure and Aldric himself has been established.

Varnholm was raised to the status of an episcopal see in 1009 as part of a reorganisation of the dioceses in the valley. A larger church was constructed under Bishop Reginhard (in office 1009–1041) to serve the new cathedral chapter. Fragments of this building survive in the lower courses of the north transept wall and in the crypt. The crypt is the oldest substantially intact part of the present cathedral. Contemporary chronicles describe the Reginhard church as a basilica with a timber roof, a western tower, and an eastern apse raised above the crypt. Several column bases with simple cushion capitals were reused in the crypt during later rebuilding and remain visible.

= = = Fire and rebuilding = = =

In the spring of 1179 a fire spread from the town into the cathedral precinct and destroyed the timber roof of the Reginhard church. The annals of the nearby abbey of Kelmstedt record that "the church of Saint Aldric burned for two days, and the bells fell and were melted". The damaged walls were judged unsafe. In 1184 Bishop Hartwig of Lohne (in office 1176–1203) laid the foundation stone of an entirely new building.

Hartwig had spent several years at the archiepiscopal court and had seen the new choirs then being built further west. Historians generally attribute the ambitious scale of the new cathedral to his influence. The first phase of work concentrated on the choir and eastern transepts. These were built over and around the surviving crypt, which was extended eastward by two bays. The choir was consecrated in 1211 by Hartwig's successor, Bishop Odo II.

Work then moved west to the crossing and main transepts. It was slowed in the 1220s and 1230s by a dispute between the bishop and the town council over quarrying rights in the Brenner hills, the principal source of the cathedral's sandstone. The dispute was resolved in 1238. The council granted the chapter perpetual rights to the Upper Brenner quarry in exchange for an annual payment of wax and the right of the burgomaster to sit in the choir on the feast of Saint Aldric. The crossing lantern was completed around 1252.

= = = Completion of the nave = = =

The nave was built in two campaigns. The eastern four bays were completed between 1255 and 1290 under a master mason recorded only as Master Eberhard. The western three bays followed between 1305 and 1347. The change in campaign is visible in the building fabric. The western bays use a more slender pier section and a different profile of rib. Their window tracery is also more elaborate. The whole nave was vaulted by 1347, when Bishop Konrad of Altheim held a ceremony marking its completion. The cathedral's necrology records that the bishop "gave a feast to all the masons and their wives".

The Black Death reached Varnholm in 1349. The chapter's account rolls suggest that building activity largely ceased for nearly two decades afterwards. The western towers had been begun only to the height of the aisle roofs. They were left incomplete and temporarily roofed with timber.

= = = Western towers = = =

Work on the western front resumed in 1412 under the master mason Johann Steinhauer of Lindau. He completed the south tower to the level of the belfry by 1440. The north tower progressed more slowly. Its upper stages were only finished in 1521, to a simplified design that omitted the openwork spire shown in a surviving fifteenth-century drawing now kept in the cathedral archive. Because of this difference, the two towers are not identical. The south tower rises to 81 metres and the north tower to 76 metres. The asymmetry has become a distinctive feature of the Varnholm skyline.

= = = Reformation and later history = = =

During the Reformation the cathedral passed briefly into Protestant use between 1546 and 1553. In this period several side altars were removed and a portion of the medieval wall painting was limewashed. The building was restored to Catholic worship in 1553 under the terms of a regional settlement. Most of the medieval furnishings removed in the 1540s were not recovered.

The cathedral suffered damage during a siege of Varnholm in 1634, when artillery fire struck the north transept and destroyed its rose window. The window was replaced in 1661 with clear glass in a simplified tracery pattern. A new high altar in the Baroque style was installed in 1702. A carved and gilded pulpit followed in 1718, the work of the sculptor Matthias Kerner.

In the nineteenth century the cathedral underwent an extensive restoration directed by the architect Friedrich Wendel between 1858 and 1879. Wendel removed most of the Baroque additions, including the 1702 high altar. He replaced them with furnishings in a Gothic Revival style. He also rebuilt the north transept rose window to a conjectural medieval design and renewed much of the exterior stonework. His work was controversial at the time. It continues to be debated by architectural historians, some of whom regard it as having destroyed significant later fabric.

The cathedral sustained only minor damage during the Second World War. The windows had been removed for safekeeping in 1939 and stored in a disused salt mine. They were reinstalled between 1948 and 1953. A programme of conservation begun in 1994 has addressed the erosion of the sandstone façades, the stabilisation of the crossing lantern, and the cleaning of the interior. It was largely completed in 2019.

= = Architecture = =

= = = Plan and dimensions = = =

The cathedral has a Latin cross plan. It consists of a seven-bay nave with single aisles, transepts of three bays each, a crossing surmounted by an octagonal lantern, and a choir of four bays ending in a polygonal apse. An ambulatory surrounds the apse, with five radiating chapels. The crypt lies beneath the choir and crossing. The internal length of the building from the west door to the axial chapel is 104 metres. The width across the transepts is 46 metres. The height of the nave vault is 24.5 metres, and that of the choir vault is 22 metres. The difference reflects the earlier date of the choir.

= = = Exterior = = =

The cathedral is built principally of reddish-brown sandstone from the Brenner hills. Pale grey limestone is used for dressings, string courses and the tracery of the later windows. The contrast between the two stones is especially marked on the western front and the south transept. It is generally considered to be intentional.

The western front consists of the two towers flanking a central gabled bay. This bay contains the main portal and a large traceried window above. The portal has a deeply recessed arch of five orders, carried on detached shafts of polished dark limestone. Its tympanum depicts the Last Judgement. Christ is shown enthroned between the Virgin Mary and Saint Aldric, with the resurrection of the dead on the lintel below. Much of the tympanum sculpture was recut during Wendel's restoration. Comparison with plaster casts taken in 1851 indicates that the overall composition was preserved.

The south tower has four stages. The lower two are plain, with narrow lancet openings. The third stage has blind arcading. The belfry stage is pierced on each side by a pair of tall two-light openings with gabled hoods. It is crowned by an octagonal stone spire with crocketed angles and small pinnacles at its base. The north tower follows the same arrangement in its lower stages. Its upper stage, completed in 1521, has a single wide opening on each face and is capped by a lower pyramidal spire sheathed in copper.

The south transept façade is the most elaborate of the exterior elevations. It contains the Bishop's Door, which was used historically for episcopal processions. Above it is a rose window with twelve radiating spokes, dated by most authorities to around 1245. The north transept façade is plainer. It retains Wendel's reconstructed rose window.

The choir exterior is supported by a system of flying buttresses. These are among the earliest in the region. They are relatively heavy in proportion, with plain unadorned flyers and solid upright piers capped by simple pyramidal pinnacles. The radiating chapels have lower roofs and are lit by paired lancets.

= = = Crossing lantern = = =

The octagonal lantern over the crossing is the most celebrated feature of the cathedral. It was completed around 1252. It rises above four massive crossing piers by means of squinches, which convert the square crossing into an octagon. Each face of the octagon contains a three-light window. Above the windows the lantern is covered by an eight-part ribbed dome. The interior of the lantern reaches a height of 38 metres above the floor.

Externally, the lantern is surrounded by a gallery with a pierced parapet. It is roofed by a low octagonal spire. The original spire was of timber and lead. It was destroyed by lightning in 1593 and replaced in 1597 with the present stone spire.

Architectural historians have noted the unusual decision to build a fully glazed octagonal lantern at a date when such features were uncommon in the region. Some scholars have proposed a link with Italian or Spanish precedents. Others have argued for a local development from the octagonal crossing towers of the late Romanesque period. Structural surveys in 1996 found that the lantern had settled by approximately 14 centimetres towards the southwest since construction. Stainless steel ties were inserted at the level of the gallery as a stabilising measure.

= = = Interior = = =

The interior of the nave presents a three-storey elevation. It consists of an arcade, a triforium, and a clerestory. The arcade piers in the eastern four bays are cylindrical cores with four attached shafts. In the western three bays they are clustered piers with eight attached shafts. The change in design is visible from the west end of the church. The triforium consists of an arcaded wall passage with three openings per bay. The clerestory windows are of two lights in the eastern bays and three lights in the western bays.

The nave is covered by quadripartite rib vaults, with transverse arches of slightly pointed profile. The vault bosses in the western bays are carved with foliage and heads. One of them is traditionally identified as a portrait of Bishop Konrad of Altheim.

The choir has a lower and more massive elevation than the nave. Its piers are alternately cylindrical and octagonal, with sexpartite vaulting over the bays. It is generally regarded as the most strongly Romanesque part of the building above ground. The apse is vaulted with a seven-part ribbed half-dome. The ambulatory and radiating chapels have simple quadripartite vaults.

= = = Choir capitals = = =

The capitals of the choir piers and ambulatory shafts are among the most important ensembles of late twelfth- and early thirteenth-century sculpture in the region. There are forty-two historiated and foliate capitals in total. They have been attributed to at least three different workshops on the basis of style.

The earliest group, on the north side of the choir, consists of dense acanthus foliage with interlaced stems. Some include birds and animals. A second group, on the south side, includes narrative scenes. These comprise the Annunciation, the Adoration of the Magi, the Flight into Egypt, and several episodes from the legend of Saint Aldric, including his calming of the river Else and his consecration as bishop. The third group, in the ambulatory, shows a more naturalistic treatment of foliage, with recognisable oak, vine and hawthorn leaves. It has been compared to contemporary work in larger cathedrals further west.

One capital in the ambulatory bears an inscription, "WERNHERUS ME FECIT" ("Wernher made me"). This is the only signed piece of sculpture in the building. Nothing further is known of the sculptor.

= = = Crypt = = =

The crypt extends beneath the whole of the choir and crossing. It is reached by stairs from the north and south choir aisles. Its western part retains masonry from the eleventh-century Reginhard church. This includes eight columns with cushion capitals supporting groin vaults. The eastern extension, built in the 1180s, has slightly taller columns with simple waterleaf capitals.

The crypt contains the tomb of Saint Aldric. This is a stone sarcophagus with a carved lid of the twelfth century, placed in a shallow recess at the eastern end. The relics of the saint were translated to a silver-gilt shrine in the choir in 1302. That shrine was melted down in 1548, and the relics, or what were believed to be them, were returned to the crypt in 1557.

= = Stained glass = =

St. Aldric's Cathedral retains a substantial proportion of its medieval stained glass. Most of it dates from between 1310 and 1360. The most complete scheme is in the clerestory of the western nave bays. It comprises fourteen windows depicting prophets in the north clerestory and apostles in the south clerestory. Each figure stands beneath an architectural canopy against a ground of alternating red and blue. The prophets each hold a scroll bearing a text, and the apostles each hold the instrument of their martyrdom. They are arranged in pairs so that each prophet faces an apostle across the nave.

The axial chapel contains three windows with scenes from the life of the Virgin. These date from about 1330. They have been extensively restored, but they retain much original glass.

The great west window was reglazed in 1883 by the Munich firm of Heinrich Lorenz with a depiction of the Tree of Jesse. It replaced a largely clear window of the seventeenth century. The north transept rose window contains glass of 1876 depicting the Heavenly Jerusalem.

In 2008 the chapter commissioned the artist Maria Holtzer to design new windows for two chapels in the south choir aisle. These had been glazed with clear glass since the eighteenth century. The windows were installed in 2011. They use an abstract composition of graduated blues and greys intended to evoke the river Else.

= = Furnishings = =

= = = Choir stalls = = =

The oak choir stalls date from between 1462 and 1474. They are the work of a workshop headed by the joiner Lorenz Hagen. There are fifty-four stalls in two rows on either side of the choir. Each has a carved misericord. The misericords depict a wide range of subjects, including scenes from the Old Testament, animals, mythical creatures, and episodes of daily life. Among the daily-life scenes are a woman churning butter, two men wrestling, and a fisherman on the Else. The bishop's throne at the eastern end of the south stalls has a tall canopy carved with pinnacles and tracery. It was heavily restored in 1871.

= = = Pulpit and altar = = =

The Baroque pulpit by Matthias Kerner, installed in 1718, was removed during Wendel's restoration. It was stored in the cathedral's lumber rooms until 1937, when it was given to the parish church of St. Michael in the nearby village of Dornau, where it remains. The present pulpit dates from 1866 and is made of limestone and alabaster in the Gothic Revival style.

The present high altar was installed in 1972 in accordance with the liturgical reforms of the Second Vatican Council. It consists of a simple block of Brenner sandstone, set forward at the crossing. Wendel's high altar of 1868 remains in the apse. It is now used as a setting for the reserved sacrament.

= = = Organ = = =

The cathedral's principal organ stands on a gallery at the west end of the nave. The first recorded organ in the cathedral was installed in 1386. The present instrument was built in 1931 by the firm of Wilhelm Sauerbrey. It was extensively rebuilt and enlarged in 1989. It has 78 stops across four manuals and pedal. Its case incorporates carved panels from a seventeenth-century predecessor. A smaller choir organ of 22 stops, built in 2004, is located in the north choir aisle.

= = = Bells = = =

The south tower houses a ring of eight bells. The largest, known as the Aldricusglocke, weighs approximately 5.2 tonnes. It was cast in 1468. It survived the requisitions of both World Wars because it was classified as being of historical importance. The other bells were recast in 1951 to replace instruments lost in 1917 and 1942. The north tower contains a single bell of 1522, used to sound the hours.

= = Burials = =

Fourteen bishops of Varnholm are buried within the cathedral. The earliest identified grave is that of Bishop Reginhard, located in the crypt. The most elaborate surviving monument is the tomb of Bishop Konrad of Altheim (died 1359) in the north choir aisle. It consists of a tomb chest with an effigy in full vestments, and the chest is surrounded by small figures of mourners set in niches. Several members of local noble families are also commemorated, notably the counts of Lohne. Their family chapel occupies the northernmost radiating chapel.

= = Cathedral close = =

The cathedral stands on raised ground at the north side of Varnholm's old town. It is surrounded by an irregular close of former canons' residences, most of which date from the sixteenth to eighteenth centuries. The cloister, on the south side of the nave, was built between 1290 and 1320. It retains three of its four original ranges. The east range was demolished in 1811 and replaced by the present diocesan museum building. The museum houses the cathedral treasury. Its holdings include a twelfth-century processional cross, a collection of liturgical vestments, and a number of fragments of medieval sculpture removed during Wendel's restoration.

= = Reception and significance = =

St. Aldric's Cathedral has been the subject of scholarly interest since the publication of the first architectural survey of the building by Karl Ostermann in 1847. It is frequently cited as an example of the gradual adoption of Gothic structural techniques in a region where Romanesque traditions persisted late. This is especially evident in the contrast between the heavy forms of the choir and the lighter construction of the western nave. Architectural historians have praised the crossing lantern as an inventive solution to the problem of lighting the centre of the building. The choir capitals are regarded as an important link between the sculptural traditions of the Rhineland and those further east.

The cathedral's appearance has made it a popular subject for painters and printmakers, particularly in the nineteenth century. The asymmetric western towers rising above the Else appear in a well-known series of lithographs by Ernst Feldmann published in 1838. Today the cathedral receives an estimated 400,000 visitors each year. The annual feast of Saint Aldric on 7 February remains an important event in the civic calendar of Varnholm. It is still marked by a procession from the town hall to the cathedral, in which the burgomaster takes the seat in the choir granted to the office in 1238.
