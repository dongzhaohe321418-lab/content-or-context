= Battle of the Lykos Ford =

The **Battle of the Lykos Ford** was fought in late September 884 near a crossing of the upper Lykos River in northeastern Anatolia, between a Byzantine field army under the *strategos* of the Armeniakon theme, Leon Kourkouas the Elder, and a raiding force from the Arab frontier emirate of Theodosiopolis led by the emir's nephew, Abu'l-Qasim ibn Sahl. The engagement ended in a decisive Byzantine victory. Much of the raiding army was destroyed while trying to cross the river with its plunder and captives. Contemporary standards place the battle among the smaller actions of the Arab–Byzantine frontier wars. Historians nonetheless regard it as an early sign of the shift in the military balance along the eastern frontier during the reign of Emperor Basil I and the first years of Leo VI.

The battle is known chiefly from three sources: a short passage in the continuation of the chronicle attributed to the circle of Theophanes, a longer and partly legendary account in the eleventh-century *Life of Saint Eustratios of Koloneia*, and two sentences in a later Arabic universal history. These sources disagree on several points, including the size of the armies and the exact date. Modern reconstructions of the battle therefore remain tentative.

= = Background = =

During the ninth century the frontier between the Byzantine Empire and the Abbasid Caliphate ran across the highlands of eastern Anatolia and western Armenia. By the 860s central Abbasid authority in the region had weakened considerably. Defence of the Muslim borderlands, known in Arabic as the *thughur*, fell increasingly to semi-autonomous emirs based in fortified cities such as Melitene, Tarsus and Theodosiopolis. These rulers conducted regular seasonal raids into Byzantine territory. Their aims were plunder, captives for ransom, and religious prestige rather than permanent conquest.

The Byzantine defeat of the emir of Melitene at the Battle of Lalakaon in 863 is usually seen as the beginning of a Byzantine recovery in the east. Under Basil I (r. 867–886), imperial armies destroyed the Paulician stronghold of Tephrike in 878. They also campaigned several times beyond the Euphrates, with mixed results. Raiding from Theodosiopolis continued, however. The city's position on the high plateau allowed its garrison to strike westward along the valleys of the Lykos and the upper Halys into the Armeniakon and Chaldia themes.

The emirate of Theodosiopolis in the early 880s was ruled by an elderly emir whom the Greek sources call "Sachles" and the Arabic tradition names as Sahl ibn Ja'far. Both the Byzantine and Arabic accounts portray him as cautious. They say that day-to-day military affairs had largely passed to his nephew Abu'l-Qasim, a younger commander who had led several successful raids in the late 870s. According to the *Life of Eustratios*, Abu'l-Qasim had sacked the monastery of the Holy Apostles near Koloneia in 881. That event made him notorious in the region.

In the Byzantine theme system, provincial armies were raised from soldiers who held military lands, supplemented by small professional units. The Armeniakon theme was one of the largest and oldest of the Anatolian themes. Its *strategos* in 884, Leon Kourkouas, belonged to a family of Armenian origin that would become prominent in the following century. Little is known of his earlier career. A seal bearing the inscription "Leon, imperial *protospatharios* and *strategos* of the Armeniakoi" has been tentatively attributed to him.

= = Prelude = =

= = = The raid of 884 = = =

The raid that led to the battle appears to have begun in late August 884. According to the chronicle, Abu'l-Qasim left Theodosiopolis "with all the young men of the city and many from the mountains." This phrase suggests that the regular garrison was joined by volunteers, possibly including Kurdish or Armenian auxiliaries. The *Life of Eustratios* gives the raiders' number as 12,000, a figure modern historians consider greatly exaggerated. Estimates in recent scholarship range from 2,500 to 4,000 men, most of them mounted.

The raiders crossed into Byzantine territory by way of the pass later known as Kara Bel. They then descended into the upper Lykos valley. Over roughly three weeks they plundered villages around Koloneia and Nikopolis and took a large number of captives and livestock. The *Life* records that the inhabitants of several villages took refuge in the fortified hilltop church of Saint Theodore at Abaris, which the raiders besieged without success for two days before moving on.

= = = Byzantine response = = =

Byzantine defensive practice of the period, later codified in the treatise known as *On Skirmishing* (*De velitatione*), favoured shadowing raiding forces rather than meeting them at once. The goal was to occupy the passes and river crossings along the raiders' return route, when they would be slowed by plunder and captives. Kourkouas appears to have followed this approach. The chronicle states that he "did not go out against them at first, but gathered the *tourmai* and waited." The *tourmai* were the territorial subdivisions of the theme army.

By mid-September, Kourkouas had assembled a force that the chronicle describes as "not large, but chosen." It probably consisted of the theme's cavalry together with infantry drawn from the *tourmai* of Koloneia and Pimolissa. Modern estimates of its size vary from 3,000 to 5,000 men. The *Life of Eustratios* adds that a detachment from the neighbouring Chaldia theme, under a *tourmarches* named Bardas, joined the army shortly before the battle. The chronicle does not mention this detachment, and some historians consider it a later addition intended to honour a local family.

Byzantine scouts reported that the raiders had begun to withdraw eastward along the southern bank of the Lykos. Kourkouas then moved his army through the hills to the north of the river. His intention was to reach the principal ford ahead of the enemy. This crossing lay at a point where the river widened over a gravel bed, a short distance below its confluence with a small tributary that the sources call the Kalopotamos. The Arabic account refers to the crossing simply as "the ford of the river of the wolves," a rendering of the Greek name *Lykos*, meaning "wolf."

= = Battle = =

= = = Forces and terrain = = =

The Lykos Ford was a natural obstacle for any force returning to Theodosiopolis. The southern approach ran through open meadows. The northern bank rose more steeply toward a line of wooded ridges. Upstream, the river ran through a narrow gorge that was impassable to a large mounted force. Downstream, the banks were marshy for several kilometres. A raiding army burdened with captives, cattle and wagons would have had to cross at the ford itself or make a lengthy detour.

Kourkouas appears to have arrived at the ford on the evening before the battle. He concealed most of his army in the woods on the northern ridges. A smaller body of infantry was placed in a fortified position near the water's edge, apparently protected by a rough barricade of felled trees. The chronicle states that the infantry were ordered "to appear few and frightened," which suggests a deliberate attempt to draw the enemy into an attack.

= = = Course of the battle = = =

The battle took place on a date that the *Life of Eustratios* gives as 24 September, the feast of Saint Thekla. The chronicle states only that it occurred "at the end of summer." Most modern historians accept the late September date as plausible, though not certain.

According to the reconstructed sequence of events, Abu'l-Qasim's vanguard reached the ford in the morning. It found the small Byzantine force holding the northern bank. The raiders' commander seems to have judged that the defenders were a local militia. He ordered an immediate crossing in order to clear the way for the main column and the captives. The first Arab attack was met by archery and javelins from behind the barricade, and the attackers fell back with some losses. A second assault, made with greater numbers, reached the northern bank. Fighting there continued for some time.

While the raiders were committed to the crossing, with part of their army in the river and the baggage train crowded on the southern meadows, Kourkouas ordered his concealed cavalry to attack. The chronicle describes the charge as coming "down from the woods like a torrent." The Byzantine horsemen struck the raiders who had reached the northern bank, driving many of them back into the river. At around the same time a smaller Byzantine detachment, which had apparently crossed the river upstream during the night by a difficult path through the gorge, attacked the baggage train on the southern bank. The *Life* attributes this detachment to Bardas of Chaldia.

The attack on the baggage caused confusion among the raiders. According to the *Life*, many of the captives broke free and joined in the fighting with whatever weapons they could find. Abu'l-Qasim attempted to rally his men on the southern meadows. The Arabic account states that he "fought until his horse was killed beneath him." He then escaped toward the east with a small group of horsemen. The rest of the raiding force broke apart. Many were killed in the river or on the meadows, and others were captured over the following days as they tried to reach the passes.

= = = Casualties = = =

The sources do not give reliable figures for losses on either side. The *Life of Eustratios* claims that "not one in ten" of the raiders returned home and that the Byzantines lost only "thirty-three men, as many as the years of the Lord." This figure is clearly symbolic. The chronicle states more cautiously that "many of the Hagarenes perished and few escaped." The Arabic source acknowledges a serious defeat but does not quantify it. Modern historians generally agree that the raiding army suffered heavy losses, possibly amounting to half its strength or more. Byzantine casualties were likely modest given the circumstances of the engagement.

All sources agree that the greater part of the captives and plunder was recovered. The *Life* states that the freed captives were led in procession to the church of Saint Theodore at Abaris, where a service of thanksgiving was held.

= = Aftermath = =

Abu'l-Qasim reached Theodosiopolis with the survivors of his force. According to the Arabic account, his uncle the emir reproached him publicly for his rashness. The defeat appears to have weakened the emirate significantly. No major raid from Theodosiopolis into the Armeniakon theme is recorded for the following six years. In 890 Abu'l-Qasim is mentioned leading a smaller expedition toward Chaldia, which was turned back without a battle. He disappears from the historical record after this point.

Kourkouas sent a report of the victory to Constantinople together with several captured standards and a number of prominent prisoners. The chronicle reports that Basil I received the news "with joy." The emperor is said to have ordered the standards displayed in the Church of the Theotokos of the Pharos within the Great Palace. Kourkouas was apparently rewarded with an elevation in rank, though the sources do not specify the dignity he received. He is not mentioned again after 886, and it is not known whether he died or was removed from command following the accession of Leo VI.

The prisoners taken at the Lykos Ford were included in a general prisoner exchange held on the Lamos River in Cilicia, which Byzantine sources date to the late 880s. According to one account, the exchange freed more than 2,000 Byzantine captives.

= = Significance = =

The Battle of the Lykos Ford had no immediate strategic consequences beyond the relief it brought to the northeastern frontier. Theodosiopolis remained in Muslim hands until the 940s, when it was finally taken by the Byzantine general John Kourkouas, a later member of the same family. Some genealogists have suggested that Leon Kourkouas was John's grandfather. This connection rests on the shared surname and the family's continued association with the eastern themes, and it is not supported by direct evidence.

Historians of Byzantine warfare have treated the battle as an example of the defensive tactics described in tenth-century military manuals. The emphasis on shadowing a raiding force, gathering local troops, and ambushing the enemy at a river crossing during its withdrawal closely matches the methods recommended in *On Skirmishing*. That treatise was compiled several decades later, possibly under the direction of Emperor Nikephoros II Phokas. Some scholars have suggested that engagements such as the Lykos Ford contributed to the body of practical experience on which the manual was based. Others caution that the surviving narratives may themselves have been shaped by later military ideals, particularly in the case of the *Life of Eustratios*, which was written more than a century after the event.

The battle has also been studied as a case of local memory in Byzantine Anatolia. The *Life of Eustratios* presents the victory as the result of the saint's intercession. It claims that Eustratios, a hermit living near Koloneia, appeared in a dream to Kourkouas on the night before the battle and revealed the hidden path through the gorge. The hagiographer also records that a small chapel dedicated to Eustratios was built near the ford in commemoration. The chapel was reportedly still in use in the author's own time. No physical remains of it have been identified.

= = Sources and historiography = =

= = = Primary sources = = =

The principal Greek narrative is a short passage in the continuation of Theophanes, a collection of chronicles compiled in the tenth century under the patronage of Emperor Constantine VII. The passage occupies fewer than twenty lines in the standard edition. It is generally considered the most reliable account, although it is written in a formulaic style and omits much detail.

The *Life of Saint Eustratios of Koloneia* survives in a single manuscript of the twelfth century now held in a monastic library on Mount Athos. The text contains the most detailed description of the battle. It includes names, dates and topographical details absent from other sources. Its value as a historical source is debated. Its author clearly had access to local traditions, but the narrative is shaped by hagiographical conventions and includes miraculous elements.

The Arabic tradition is represented by a brief notice in a universal history compiled in the thirteenth century. The author probably drew on an earlier chronicle from the Jazira that no longer survives. The notice confirms the defeat of a raiding force from Theodosiopolis "in the year 271," corresponding to 884–885 in the Christian calendar. It names Abu'l-Qasim as its commander and briefly describes his escape.

= = = Modern scholarship = = =

The battle received little attention from historians before the twentieth century. It was first discussed in detail in a 1934 study of the Armeniakon theme. The author of that study accepted the *Life of Eustratios* largely at face value. Later scholarship has been more sceptical. A 1978 article on Byzantine defensive warfare argued that the role of the Chaldian detachment and the night march through the gorge were probably literary inventions. A subsequent study disputed this conclusion. It pointed out that the topography of the upper Lykos does allow passage along the gorge on foot, a claim supported by a survey of the valley conducted in the 1990s.

The precise location of the ford has also been disputed. Two sites have been proposed. One lies near the modern village of Kılıçkaya, and the other some twelve kilometres further downstream. The upstream site better fits the description of the gorge and the tributary. The downstream site lies closer to the line of a known Roman road. A small number of iron arrowheads and a bronze buckle of probable ninth-century date were recovered near the upstream site during road construction in 1987. They have been cited in support of that location, though the finds are too few to be conclusive.

= = Legacy = =

The battle is commemorated in the region chiefly through the tradition surrounding Saint Eustratios. His feast, recorded in some later Synaxaria on 24 September, coincides with the date the *Life* assigns to the battle. Historians have debated whether the date of the battle was adjusted to match the feast or the feast was established in memory of the battle.

In modern times the battle has been mentioned occasionally in popular histories of the Byzantine–Arab wars, usually as an illustration of the frontier warfare of the ninth century. It has not been the subject of any major work of fiction or popular culture.
