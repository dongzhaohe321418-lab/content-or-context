The Brindlecote Canal is a narrow canal in the English Midlands. It runs for 18 miles (29 km) from a junction with the Coventry Canal near Polesworth in Warwickshire to the market town of Brindlecote in north-west Leicestershire. It was authorised by Act of Parliament in 1794 and completed in 1802. Its main purpose was to carry coal from the Ashby and Measham coalfields and lime from quarries near Breedon to the national canal network. It has 21 locks, a 612-yard (560 m) tunnel at Hollins Hill, and a three-arch aqueduct over the River Mease.

Commercial traffic declined after the opening of competing railways in the 1840s and 1850s. The upper section above Measham Wharf was formally abandoned in 1944 after years of mining subsidence. The lower 11 miles (18 km) stayed navigable and is now used mainly by leisure boats. Since 1987 the Brindlecote Canal Trust has worked to restore the abandoned section. By 2024 it had reopened about 3 miles (4.8 km), including the restored Snarestone Locks.

= = Route = =

The canal leaves the Coventry Canal at Gorsey Lane Junction, about 1 mile (1.6 km) south-east of Polesworth. The junction is marked by a cast-iron roving bridge made by the Horseley Ironworks in 1821, which replaced an earlier timber structure. From the junction the canal heads north-east through farmland. It follows the 270-foot (82 m) contour for its first 4 miles (6.4 km), and this lock-free stretch is known locally as the "Long Level".

At Austrey Bottom the canal rises through the five Austrey Locks to reach the valley of the River Mease. It crosses the river on Mease Aqueduct, a brick and sandstone structure of three segmental arches, each spanning 28 feet (8.5 m). Beyond the aqueduct the canal climbs through the four Netherseal Locks and passes the former Netherseal Colliery basin. It then reaches Measham Wharf, which was once the canal's busiest interchange. The wharf area contains a restored warehouse of 1803, a weighing machine house, and the foundations of a lime kiln bank.

North of Measham the canal enters the abandoned section. It continues through the six Snarestone Locks and then enters Hollins Hill Tunnel. The tunnel has no towpath, and boats were originally legged through. Beyond the northern portal the canal crosses an embankment near Swepstone. It then rises through the final six locks, the Brindlecote Flight, to reach its terminal basin on the southern edge of Brindlecote. The terminus once had two arms. The Coal Arm served the town's gasworks and coal merchants. The Lime Arm ran to kilns supplied by tramroad from the Breedon quarries.

= = History = =

= = = Origins and authorisation = = =

Proposals for a canal connecting the north-west Leicestershire coalfield to the Coventry Canal were first made in the 1770s. The coalfield's owners were cut off from the growing markets of Coventry, Oxford and London by poor roads, and coal sent by packhorse or wagon cost about twice as much at the Coventry Canal wharves as at the pithead. A survey commissioned in 1781 by a group of landowners led by Sir Thomas Chetwyn of Seal Hall proposed a route of 22 miles (35 km). It was not taken further because of the economic uncertainty that followed the American War of Independence.

The canal speculation of the early 1790s revived interest in the scheme. In 1792 a public meeting at the George Inn, Brindlecote, appointed the engineer Robert Whitworth junior to survey a new line. His report, presented in February 1793, proposed a shorter route with a summit tunnel at Hollins Hill. This avoided a long detour around the ridge between the Mease valley and the Brindlecote basin. His estimated cost was £96,400.

The promoters faced opposition from the proprietors of the Ashby Canal, whose own Bill was being prepared at the same time, and from several mill owners on the River Mease, who feared the loss of water. A compromise was reached in which the Brindlecote company agreed to build a reservoir at Gilwiskaw Hollow and pay compensation for any water taken from the Mease. The Brindlecote Canal Act received royal assent on 11 April 1794. It authorised the company to raise £100,000 in shares of £100 each, and a further £30,000 if needed.

= = = Construction = = =

Work began at Gorsey Lane in July 1794 under resident engineer James Barnes. The Long Level was finished quickly, and by the end of 1795 boats were working as far as Austrey Bottom. The Mease Aqueduct gave more trouble. A flood in February 1797 damaged the partly built central pier, and the work had to be underpinned on piles driven into the river gravels. The aqueduct opened in October 1798, and coal from the Netherseal pits began moving south shortly afterwards.

Hollins Hill Tunnel was the largest engineering work on the line. Seven construction shafts were sunk along it. Waterlogged sand at the southern end made progress slow, and in 1799 a section of about 40 yards (37 m) collapsed and had to be rebuilt with a thicker brick lining. Rising costs forced the company to raise the further £30,000 allowed under the Act and to borrow £18,000 more on mortgage. The tunnel was completed in June 1801, and the whole canal opened on 24 May 1802. A procession of decorated boats travelled from Measham to Brindlecote, where a dinner was held for the proprietors.

The final cost of the canal was about £148,000, more than 50 per cent above Whitworth's estimate.

= = = Operation and prosperity = = =

For its first forty years the canal was moderately profitable. Coal was always its main traffic. In its peak year, 1838, the canal carried 187,000 tons of goods, of which about 134,000 tons were coal and 31,000 tons were lime and limestone. The rest was mostly grain, bricks, timber and general merchandise. Dividends began in 1809 at 2 per cent and reached 6.5 per cent by the mid-1830s.

The lime trade relied on the Breedon Tramroad, a horse-drawn plateway 4.5 miles (7.2 km) long, opened by the company in 1804. Limestone and burnt lime came down to the Lime Arm at Brindlecote in wagons. Part of it went on by canal, and part was burnt in the company's own kilns at the basin. The tramroad's stone sleeper blocks can still be seen in several places along a public footpath that follows the route.

Water supply was a recurring problem. The Gilwiskaw Hollow reservoir proved too small in dry summers. A second reservoir at Swepstone Moor was built in 1826, and in 1831 a steam pumping engine was installed at Snarestone to lift water back up the flight. The engine house still stands, although the engine was removed in about 1890.

= = = Railway competition and decline = = =

The Midland Counties Railway, and later the lines that became part of the Midland Railway, gave the coalfield direct rail access to Leicester and the south. The opening of the Leicester and Swannington Railway's extensions and of a branch line to Brindlecote in 1849 took much of the canal's long-distance coal trade. Toll receipts fell by nearly half between 1845 and 1852, and dividends stopped in 1856.

The company considered selling the canal to the Midland Railway. Negotiations in 1846 and 1857 both failed over the price. The canal stayed independent and served mainly local traffic, in particular coal from the pits along its banks to wharves on the Coventry Canal and to the town of Brindlecote itself.

Subsidence from coal working became a serious problem in the second half of the 19th century. The section between Measham and Snarestone sank by up to 9 feet (2.7 m) in places. The banks had to be raised repeatedly, and several locks had to be rebuilt. The company's minute books record claims for compensation against colliery owners almost every year from 1868 onwards, although few were successful.

= = = Twentieth century and abandonment = = =

By 1900 traffic had fallen to about 40,000 tons a year. The Hollins Hill Tunnel needed costly repairs after a partial roof collapse in 1907, and the upper section was closed for eleven months. After the First World War the remaining coal traffic from the Netherseal and Measham pits continued, but the trade above Measham almost disappeared. The last recorded commercial boat to reach Brindlecote basin arrived in 1931 with a load of roadstone.

The company sold the canal to the Coventry Canal Company in 1937 for £4,200. The new owner applied for a warrant of abandonment of the section above Measham Wharf, and this was granted by the Ministry of War Transport in 1944. The tunnel portals were bricked up. Several bridges were lowered, and parts of the channel north of Swepstone were filled in during the 1950s and 1960s for farmland and road improvements. The Brindlecote basin was filled and built over in 1962. Its site is now occupied partly by a supermarket car park.

The lower section from Gorsey Lane to Measham carried coal until 1958, when Netherseal Colliery's canal loading point closed. After nationalisation in 1948 the canal passed to the British Transport Commission and then, in 1963, to the British Waterways Board. Under the Transport Act 1968 the lower section was classed as a "remainder" waterway, which meant its future was uncertain. Pressure from boating groups and local authorities led to its reclassification as a cruising waterway in 1979.

= = Restoration = =

The Brindlecote Canal Society was formed in 1984. It became the Brindlecote Canal Trust, a registered charity, in 1987. Its stated aim is to restore navigation to Brindlecote and to create a new terminal basin, since the original site has been built over.

Early work was done by volunteer working parties. They cleared vegetation from the Snarestone Locks and rebuilt the lock chambers using reclaimed brick. Restoration was slow for its first decade, held back by questions about land ownership and the cost of rebuilding two lowered road bridges. A feasibility study commissioned in 1996 by Leicestershire County Council and North West Leicestershire District Council estimated the cost of full restoration at £21 million.

The first restored section, from Measham Wharf to the foot of the Snarestone flight, reopened in 2003. Grants from the Heritage Lottery Fund and the East Midlands Development Agency paid for the rebuilding of the six Snarestone Locks between 2009 and 2016, and a new road bridge was built at Snarestone Lane in 2014. The flight reopened to boats in September 2017. The Snarestone pumping engine house was restored at the same time and now serves as a visitor centre and the trust's headquarters.

Hollins Hill Tunnel is the greatest remaining obstacle. A structural survey in 2019 found the central section in fair condition but recommended relining about 180 yards (165 m) at the southern end. The northern approach is complicated by the line of the A444 road, which was built across the former canal bed in 1971. The trust's preferred plan includes a new channel diverted east of the original route, with a new aqueduct over the road, and a terminal basin on land set aside in the Brindlecote local plan. As of 2024 the trust was raising money for the tunnel works and holding the planned route through planning protections.

= = Engineering features = =

= = = Locks = = =

The canal's locks were built to the standard narrow dimensions of the Midlands canals, about 72 by 7 feet (21.9 by 2.1 m). Most have single bottom gates, which was unusual for the region, where mitred pairs were common. Barnes is believed to have chosen this design to save money. The original chambers were built of red brick with sandstone copings from quarries near Measham. The rebuilt Snarestone Locks keep the single bottom gates, made in oak to the original pattern.

The total rise from Gorsey Lane to the former Brindlecote basin is 152 feet (46 m).

= = = Mease Aqueduct = = =

The Mease Aqueduct is a Grade II* listed building. It carries the canal about 26 feet (7.9 m) above the river. The trough was originally lined with puddled clay in the traditional way. During repairs in 1934 the trough was relined in concrete, and in 2008 British Waterways installed a waterproof membrane after leaks had caused damage to the arches. Its downstream face carries a carved stone tablet recording the names of the company's chairman and engineer and the date 1798.

= = = Hollins Hill Tunnel = = =

Hollins Hill Tunnel is 612 yards (560 m) long and has a width of about 9 feet (2.7 m), which is enough for only one narrowboat at a time. Boatmen legged their craft through by lying on boards and pushing against the walls with their feet. Professional leggers worked from a hut at the southern portal until about 1870, charging one shilling and sixpence per boat in the 1840s. The southern portal is built of brick with a stone keystone and has been restored. The northern portal is partly buried. Three of the original construction shafts were kept as ventilation shafts, and their brick tops remain visible in fields on the hill.

The tunnel is home to a colony of Daubenton's bats. Plans for restoration include measures to protect their roosting sites, and any work inside the tunnel will need a licence from Natural England.

= = Wildlife and ecology = =

The lower canal passes through mostly farmland and has varied water and bank vegetation, including yellow water-lily, arrowhead, and flowering rush. The abandoned section north of Snarestone, which has been largely free of boats for over eighty years, has developed rich wetland habitats. The stretch between Swepstone and the northern tunnel portal was designated a Site of Special Scientific Interest in 1992 because of its aquatic plants and invertebrates, including several scarce species of water beetle.

Restoration has therefore caused some disagreement. Conservation groups have argued that boat traffic would damage the SSSI. The trust has proposed a nature reserve on a parallel offline channel, to be dug next to the restored navigation, which would give wildlife a place free of boat traffic. Natural England has agreed to the approach in principle, subject to detailed survey work.

Kingfishers, grey herons and water voles are regularly recorded along the canal. Otters were confirmed on the Mease near the aqueduct in 2011, the first record in that part of the catchment for more than forty years.

= = Leisure and heritage = =

The navigable section of the canal is used by both private boats and hire boats, with a hire base at Austrey Wharf. Boat movement figures recorded by the Canal & River Trust, which took over British Waterways' responsibilities in England and Wales in 2012, show between 2,500 and 3,000 boat passages a year through Austrey Locks.

The towpath is a public right of way along its whole navigable length and part of the abandoned section. It forms part of the Mease Valley Way, a long-distance footpath of 34 miles (55 km). Angling is popular on the Long Level, where the fishing rights are managed by a local angling association.

Measham Wharf is a conservation area. Its 1803 warehouse was converted into a museum of local mining and canal history in 1999. The museum's collection includes the company's original seal, the minute books from 1794 to 1937, and a horse-drawn tramroad wagon recovered from the Breedon line. The trust holds an annual boat rally at Measham each August, which attracts several hundred visitors.

= = Notable structures = =

Several structures along the canal are listed buildings. Besides the Mease Aqueduct, they include:

* Gorsey Lane roving bridge (1821), Grade II
* Measham Wharf warehouse (1803), Grade II
* Snarestone pumping engine house (1831), Grade II
* The southern portal of Hollins Hill Tunnel (1801), Grade II
* Austrey Top lock cottage (c. 1796), Grade II

The canal company's former office at Brindlecote, a three-storey brick house on Wharf Street built in 1803, survives. It is used as a solicitors' office. A blue plaque marks its connection with the canal.
