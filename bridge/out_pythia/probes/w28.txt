= Cornerstone Weather =

"Cornerstone Weather" is a song by American hip-hop group Third Rail Kollective, released in March 1995 as the second single from their debut studio album, *Transit Authority* (1995). The song was written by group members Darnell "Dee Roc" Whitfield, Marcus "Lo-Key" Abernathy, and Terrence "T-Brick" Sloane, with production handled by Gilbert "Gilly Fingaz" Ocasio. It was released through Undertow Records, a subsidiary of the larger Meridian Music Group.

The song is built around a slowed sample of a 1972 soul recording and describes a single day in the group's home neighborhood in Paterson, New Jersey, using changes in the weather as a framing device. It became the group's most commercially successful release, peaking at number 34 on the US *Billboard* Hot 100 and at number 7 on the Hot Rap Singles chart. Its music video, directed by Celeste Varga, received regular rotation on cable music channels and was nominated for Best Rap Video at a regional music video awards ceremony in 1995.

Critics praised "Cornerstone Weather" for its narrative structure and its restrained production. Hip-hop journalists and fans have since counted it among the more notable East Coast singles of the mid-1990s, though the group never matched its success.

= = Background = =

Third Rail Kollective formed in 1990 in Paterson, New Jersey, when Whitfield and Abernathy, who had attended Eastside High School together, began performing at house parties and community center talent shows. Sloane, a cousin of Abernathy, joined the group in 1991 after the three met at a local open-microphone night held in the basement of a record store on Market Street. The group's name came from the electrified rail that powers subway trains. Whitfield later said in an interview that the name was meant to suggest "something you don't touch unless you know what you're doing."

Between 1991 and 1993, the group recorded several demo tapes, which they sold at local shows and distributed to college radio stations in northern New Jersey and New York City. One of these tapes reached Gilbert Ocasio, a producer from the Bronx who had previously worked as an engineer on several independent twelve-inch releases. Ocasio began producing for the group in 1993. Later that year he brought their material to Undertow Records, which had been founded in 1992 as an outlet for regional hip-hop acts.

Undertow signed Third Rail Kollective in early 1994. The group's first single, "Fare Evasion," was released in August 1994 and received modest airplay on mix shows in the New York metropolitan area, but it did not enter any national chart. Undertow executives then encouraged the group to develop material with what label vice president Harold Brisbane later called "a stronger emotional center." Shortly afterward the group began work on the song that became "Cornerstone Weather."

= = Writing and recording = =

According to Abernathy, the idea for the song came during a rainstorm in the summer of 1994, when the three members were waiting out the weather under the awning of a corner bodega near the group's regular rehearsal space. Abernathy recalled that the group watched how the people on the block changed their behavior as the rain began and ended, and that Whitfield suggested writing a song that followed "one corner, one day, from sunrise to the streetlights." The title refers both to the corner where the idea arose and to the idea of the neighborhood as a foundation for the people who live there.

Each member wrote one of the song's three verses, and each verse covers a different part of the day. Sloane's opening verse is set in the morning and describes clear, cold weather. Abernathy's verse covers the afternoon and a sudden thunderstorm. Whitfield's closing verse takes place in the evening after the rain has stopped. The hook, sung by Paterson-based vocalist Renata Dukes, was written collectively by the group and Ocasio during the recording sessions.

Recording took place over four days in October 1994 at Lantern Sound, a small studio in Union City, New Jersey. Ocasio built the instrumental on an Akai MPC60 sampler, using a slowed and filtered loop taken from "Rain on Hollis Street," a 1972 single by the Detroit soul group the Velvet Assurance. Ocasio said he lowered the sample's pitch by roughly three semitones to give it "a heavier, almost humid feeling." He added a separate drum break sampled from a 1970s funk record, which he declined to name in interviews for many years. In a 2011 interview, he identified it as an obscure B-side by a Cleveland-area band.

Session musician Lionel Hartwell contributed a live bass line, recorded on a fretless electric bass. Ocasio said he wanted the bass to "move under the sample like water under a street." Dukes recorded her vocals in a single evening session. Most of the final hook was taken from her second take.

Clearing the Velvet Assurance sample delayed the song's release by roughly two months. Undertow ultimately reached an agreement with the rights holders that credited the original songwriters, Clarence Mabry and Odessa Fulton, as co-writers of "Cornerstone Weather."

= = Composition and lyrics = =

"Cornerstone Weather" is a hip-hop song that runs for four minutes and fifty-one seconds on the album version and four minutes and nine seconds on the radio edit. It is set in common time at a tempo of approximately 86 beats per minute and is written in the key of E-flat minor. The production is sparse by the standards of the period. It relies mainly on the looped soul sample, the drum break, and Hartwell's bass line, with occasional sound effects of rain and distant thunder mixed low beneath the second verse.

The song follows a verse–chorus structure, with each of the three verses introduced by a brief spoken line that states the time of day. The opening verse begins with Sloane's line "Six-fifteen and the sky's still deciding." It describes the neighborhood waking up: shop owners raising metal security gates, children walking to school, and older residents gathering at a bus stop. Writing for *The Low End*, critic Paula Renwick described the verse as "a series of snapshots more than a story."

Abernathy's second verse shifts in tone as the storm arrives. The lyrics describe people crowding under awnings and into doorways, and an argument between two young men that is interrupted when the downpour forces both of them to take shelter in the same store. Abernathy later said the verse was meant to show "how the weather makes everybody equal for a few minutes." The rhythm of his delivery is noticeably faster than in the other two verses, which several reviewers took to mirror the intensity of the rain.

Whitfield's closing verse is set after the storm, as the streetlights come on and residents return to the corner. It is the most reflective part of the song, with references to friends who had moved away, been incarcerated, or died. The verse ends with the line "Puddles hold the light like the block holds us," which is often quoted as the song's central image.

The hook, sung by Dukes, repeats the phrase "Whatever the weather, we hold the corner together," with slight lyrical variations each time it appears. Music writers have pointed out that the hook can be read either as an affirmation of community or as an ambiguous reference to street life. The group has generally favored the first interpretation in interviews.

= = Release = =

Undertow Records released "Cornerstone Weather" as a twelve-inch vinyl single and a cassette single on March 14, 1995. A CD single followed two weeks later. The commercial releases contained the album version, the radio edit, an instrumental, and an a cappella version. The CD single also included a non-album B-side, "Token Booth Blues."

The label promoted the single first through mix-show DJs in New York, Philadelphia, and Washington, D.C., before moving on to commercial radio formats. Brisbane later said the label had initially expected the song to be "a street record" with limited crossover potential, but that requests from listeners at urban contemporary stations in several mid-Atlantic markets led to a broader promotional push in April 1995.

*Transit Authority*, the group's debut album, was released on May 2, 1995, partly on the strength of the single's growing airplay.

= = Critical reception = =

Music critics received "Cornerstone Weather" favorably on release. Writing for the hip-hop magazine *The Low End*, Paula Renwick called it "one of the most carefully built singles to come out of New Jersey in years." She praised its three-part structure and said each member's verse "earns its place." *Rhythm Ledger* gave the single four out of five, noting that Ocasio's production was "patient in a year when a lot of records are in a hurry."

Reviewing the parent album for a major metropolitan newspaper, critic Jonathan Pryce singled out "Cornerstone Weather" as the album's highlight. He described it as "a short story set to a drum loop." He was more critical of the remainder of *Transit Authority*, which he felt did not consistently reach the same level of songwriting.

Some reviews were mixed. A writer for the alternative weekly *Northern Current* found the song's sentimentality "a little heavy-handed in the final verse," while acknowledging the strength of the production. Dukes' hook was also described in one review as "pleasant but overly smooth" next to the rougher verses.

= = Commercial performance = =

"Cornerstone Weather" entered the US *Billboard* Hot 100 in the week of April 15, 1995, at number 89. It climbed steadily over the following weeks and peaked at number 34 in the week of June 3, 1995. It spent a total of seventeen weeks on the chart. On the Hot R&B Singles chart, the song peaked at number 21. It reached number 7 on the Hot Rap Singles chart, where it remained in the top ten for six consecutive weeks.

The single sold strongly in the northeastern United States, especially in New Jersey, New York, and Pennsylvania. In July 1995, the Recording Industry Association of America certified it gold for shipments of 500,000 units. Outside the United States, the song had limited commercial impact. It reached number 58 on the UK Singles Chart after a limited British release in September 1995, and it charted briefly in the Netherlands.

*Transit Authority* peaked at number 41 on the *Billboard* 200 and at number 9 on the Top R&B/Hip-Hop Albums chart. Undertow later attributed most of the album's sales to the success of "Cornerstone Weather."

= = Music video = =

The music video for "Cornerstone Weather" was directed by Celeste Varga, who had previously directed videos for several independent rock and hip-hop acts. It was filmed over two days in February 1995 at the intersection of Twelfth Avenue and East Eighteenth Street in Paterson, near the corner that inspired the song. A stage crew created rain effects with water trucks and overhead sprinklers. Varga said the cold weather during filming made the rain sequences "genuinely miserable for everyone involved." She also said the discomfort helped the performers appear convincing.

The video follows the song's structure. It opens in pale early-morning light, moves to darker, desaturated tones during the storm sequence, and ends in warm orange streetlight. Varga cast many neighborhood residents as extras, including the owner of the bodega that appears throughout the video. Renata Dukes appears during each chorus, singing from a fire escape above the corner.

The video premiered in late March 1995 and received regular rotation on hip-hop and R&B programming on national cable music channels. It was nominated for Best Rap Video at the 1995 Tri-State Music Video Awards, a regional ceremony, but lost to another nominee. Varga's use of color grading to mark the time of day was later cited by other video directors as an influence.

= = Live performances = =

Third Rail Kollective performed "Cornerstone Weather" on several syndicated television music programs in 1995, including a late-night hip-hop showcase broadcast from New York. Dukes did not tour with the group, so on many live dates the hook was sung by backup vocalist Tasha Greer or performed with a pre-recorded vocal track.

The group closed nearly every concert on its 1995 and 1996 tours with the song, often extending the final verse with an additional improvised section dedicated to friends from their neighborhood. After the group reunited in 2009, they performed it at a benefit concert in Paterson organized to raise funds for a local youth center.

= = Legacy = =

"Cornerstone Weather" is generally regarded as Third Rail Kollective's signature song and is the release for which the group is best known. Their second album, *Express Local* (1997), received weaker reviews and lower sales. None of its singles entered the Hot 100. Undertow Records ceased operations in 1998, and the group went on hiatus soon afterward as its members pursued solo projects and work outside the music industry.

In later years, music journalists have pointed to the song as an example of the narrative-focused hip-hop that emerged from smaller East Coast markets during the mid-1990s. In 2005, a retrospective feature in *Rhythm Ledger* placed the song at number 62 on a list of the best hip-hop singles of the 1990s. The magazine described it as "a regional record that understood how to be universal."

Ocasio's production on the song has also been recognized. Several producers have cited its use of a slowed soul sample with a live bass overdub as an influence, and the Velvet Assurance's "Rain on Hollis Street" saw renewed interest among sample-based producers and record collectors after the single's success. A remastered reissue of the Velvet Assurance's catalog in 2003 included liner notes discussing the sample's use in "Cornerstone Weather."

The song has been sampled or interpolated by at least a dozen later hip-hop and R&B recordings. Most borrow Dukes' hook or Whitfield's closing line. In 2015, on the single's twentieth anniversary, the city of Paterson held a small public event at the corner featured in the video, and the surviving members of the group performed the song there with Dukes.
