USS Halvorsen (DD-612)

USS Halvorsen (DD-612) was a Benson-class destroyer of the United States Navy, in service from 1942 to 1946 and briefly reactivated from 1951 to 1954. She was named for Lieutenant Commander Einar Halvorsen (1889–1918), a naval officer killed when the armed tug he commanded was torpedoed in the Bay of Biscay during the First World War. Halvorsen served in the Atlantic and Mediterranean during the Second World War. She escorted convoys, supported the landings in North Africa, Sicily, and southern France, and was credited with sinking one German submarine. After a short period in the Atlantic Reserve Fleet, she returned to service during the Korean War era as a training and escort vessel before being decommissioned for the last time and sold for scrap in 1972.

= = Namesake = =

Einar Halvorsen was born on 14 March 1889 in Duluth, Minnesota, to Norwegian immigrant parents. He worked on Great Lakes ore carriers as a young man before enlisting in the Navy in 1908. He was commissioned an ensign in 1914 after completing a course of instruction for promising enlisted men. When the United States entered the First World War, he held the rank of lieutenant and was serving on a gunboat on the Atlantic coast.

In early 1918, Halvorsen was given command of the armed tug Wrenford. The tug was assigned to towing and rescue work in support of convoys approaching the French coast. On the night of 22 September 1918, Wrenford went to help a torpedoed collier off Belle Île and was herself struck by a torpedo from an unidentified German submarine. Halvorsen stayed aboard to supervise the launching of the tug's boats and was lost with eleven members of his crew when the vessel sank. He was posthumously awarded the Navy Cross and promoted to lieutenant commander.

= = Design and construction = =

Halvorsen was one of a group of Benson-class destroyers ordered under the naval expansion programs of 1939 and 1940. The class was a development of the earlier Sims class. It differed most visibly in having two funnels and a machinery layout that alternated boiler and engine rooms, which reduced the chance that a single hit would disable the whole propulsion plant.

The ship was laid down on 3 February 1941 by the Bethlehem Steel Company at its Staten Island yard in New York. She was launched on 19 November 1941, sponsored by Mrs. Ingrid Halvorsen Pratt, the namesake's niece. The war in Europe was by then well advanced, and the attack on Pearl Harbor followed less than three weeks later. As a result, fitting out was accelerated. Halvorsen was commissioned on 28 April 1942 with Lieutenant Commander Robert T. Ashby in command.

As completed, Halvorsen had a standard displacement of 1,620 long tons and an overall length of 348 feet 3 inches (106.15 m). Her beam was 36 feet 1 inch (11.0 m). Four high-pressure boilers drove two geared steam turbines, which produced 50,000 shaft horsepower and gave a designed speed of 37.5 knots. Her armament originally consisted of four 5-inch/38 caliber guns in single mounts, five 21-inch torpedo tubes in a single quintuple mount, and a light anti-aircraft battery of .50 caliber machine guns. For anti-submarine work she carried depth charge racks at the stern and depth charge projectors amidships. Her wartime complement was approximately 16 officers and 260 enlisted men.

= = Service history = =

= = = Shakedown and early convoy duty, 1942 = = =

Halvorsen conducted her shakedown off Casco Bay, Maine, in May and June 1942. During this period her light machine guns were exchanged for 20 mm Oerlikon cannon, and a Type SC air-search radar was installed. She was then assigned to Destroyer Squadron 13 and reported to the Commander, Destroyer Forces Atlantic Fleet.

Her first operational assignment began on 11 July 1942, when she sailed from Norfolk, Virginia, as part of the screen for a troop convoy bound for Northern Ireland. The crossing passed without enemy contact. Several men were injured, however, when heavy seas struck the ship south of Iceland, carrying away a whaleboat and damaging the forward torpedo director. Halvorsen arrived at Londonderry on 24 July and returned to New York in mid-August with a westbound convoy.

Over the following two months she made a second round trip to the United Kingdom and took part in exercises in Chesapeake Bay. The exercises were preparation for Operation Torch, the Allied invasion of French North Africa.

= = = Operation Torch = = =

On 24 October 1942, Halvorsen sailed from Hampton Roads with the Center Attack Group of the Western Naval Task Force. This group was assigned to land troops near Fedala, on the Atlantic coast of Morocco. In the early hours of 8 November, she took up a position on the seaward flank of the transport area to guard against submarines and surface attack.

At about 07:10, French warships came out of Casablanca to contest the landings. These included the light cruiser Primauguet and several destroyers and large torpedo boats. Halvorsen joined other destroyers of the screen in engaging them. Over roughly an hour and a half, she fired more than 400 rounds of 5-inch ammunition. She also made smoke to cover the transports. Shell splashes straddled her several times, and splinters wounded four men on the after deckhouse, but she suffered no direct hits. Her gunnery officer later stated that she had scored at least two hits on a French destroyer. Postwar French records, however, could not confirm which Allied ship had been responsible for the damage to the vessels engaged that morning.

After the Casablanca garrison surrendered on 11 November, Halvorsen patrolled off Fedala to protect the unloading transports. She was present on the night of 11–12 November when German submarines attacked the anchorage and sank several transports. She spent much of that night picking up survivors and recovered 87 men from the water. She left Morocco on 17 November and arrived at Norfolk on 30 November.

= = = Atlantic convoys and the sinking of U-447 = = =

During the first half of 1943, Halvorsen escorted convoys between the United States and North Africa. She received a refit at the New York Navy Yard in February. During the refit, two twin 40 mm Bofors mounts were added, an improved surface-search radar was fitted, and her depth charge capacity was increased.

On 9 April 1943, Halvorsen was screening convoy UGS-7 about 400 nautical miles west of the Azores when her sonar operator reported a contact at 2,100 yards on the convoy's port bow. Lieutenant Commander Ashby made an initial attack with a ten-charge pattern. After she regained contact, Halvorsen made three more attacks over the next ninety minutes. Following the fourth, oil and debris rose to the surface, including wooden fittings, part of a mattress, and a quantity of German-language paper. The convoy continued without loss.

The Navy credited Halvorsen with a probable kill at the time. After the war, a review of German records led to her being credited with the destruction of the submarine U-447, which had failed to report after being ordered to the area. Some historians have questioned this identification, noting that U-447 may have been lost to aircraft in a separate action. The Navy's official records continue to credit Halvorsen. Ashby received the Legion of Merit for the action, and the ship's sonar team received commendations.

= = = Sicily and Italy = = =

In June 1943, Halvorsen was assigned to the Eighth Fleet in the Mediterranean. She took part in Operation Husky, the invasion of Sicily, as a fire-support destroyer with the force that landed American troops near Licata on 10 July. Over the first three days of the operation, she carried out more than twenty fire missions against coastal batteries, troop concentrations, and road junctions. Shore fire control parties directed her fire, and on one occasion a spotting aircraft did so.

On the evening of 11 July, a German Junkers Ju 88 attacked her while she was close inshore. The bomb fell about 30 yards off the starboard quarter. The blast caused minor flooding in the after steering compartment and damaged the starboard propeller shaft. Halvorsen remained on station overnight. She then withdrew to Bizerte, Tunisia, for temporary repairs, which kept her out of action until early August.

When she returned, she escorted convoys between North Africa and Sicily. In September 1943, she screened follow-up convoys to the Salerno beachhead during Operation Avalanche. She stood off the beaches for four days and was frequently called to general quarters by air raids. During this time her gunners claimed one German aircraft shot down. In the closing months of 1943, she made a series of convoy runs between Oran, Algiers, and Naples. In December she returned to the United States for overhaul at the Boston Navy Yard.

= = = Anzio and the return to convoy duty = = =

Halvorsen returned to the Mediterranean in February 1944 and was assigned to support the Anzio beachhead, where Allied forces had been contained by strong German counterattacks. Between 21 February and 30 March, she made three tours on the gunfire support line off Anzio and Nettuno. She fired on German artillery positions and on troops assembling for attacks. On 7 March, a radio-controlled glide bomb narrowly missed her. During the same period she rescued 34 survivors from a British landing ship that had struck a mine.

In April and May 1944, Halvorsen escorted convoys in the western Mediterranean. She was briefly detached to search for a German submarine reported off the Algerian coast, but did not find it. Lieutenant Commander Ashby was relieved by Commander Francis L. Merritt on 3 May 1944.

= = = Operation Dragoon = = =

Halvorsen was next assigned to Operation Dragoon, the Allied landings on the Mediterranean coast of France. On 15 August 1944, she supported the landing of American troops in the Camel Beach area east of Saint-Raphaël. Her bombardment was directed against German strongpoints on the headland overlooking the beach. During the morning she fired on a coastal battery that had straddled a nearby landing craft and silenced it after about forty rounds.

In the days that followed, Halvorsen remained off the French Riviera. She provided call fire for advancing troops and protected the anchorage against German explosive motorboats and human torpedoes, which were operating from bases near Nice and San Remo. On the night of 26 August, her lookouts spotted a small craft approaching the anchorage. Halvorsen illuminated it with her searchlight and opened fire, and the boat blew up about 600 yards from the ship. She continued patrols along the Franco-Italian border into October 1944 and fired periodic bombardments at German positions in the Maritime Alps.

= = = Final wartime service = = =

Halvorsen left the Mediterranean on 21 November 1944 and arrived at New York on 6 December. After a period of leave and maintenance, she was assigned to escort duty along the Atlantic coast and in the Caribbean. She also served as a plane guard for escort carriers conducting pilot qualification off Florida.

In May 1945, after Germany surrendered, she was scheduled for conversion to strengthen her anti-aircraft battery so that she could be deployed to the Pacific. The work had not yet started when Japan surrendered in August 1945. Halvorsen spent the remaining months of 1945 carrying returning servicemen between European ports and the United States as part of Operation Magic Carpet. On one such voyage, from Le Havre to Boston in October 1945, she carried 148 passengers in addition to her crew.

Halvorsen was decommissioned at Charleston, South Carolina, on 14 May 1946 and placed in the Atlantic Reserve Fleet.

= = = Reactivation, 1951–1954 = = =

The outbreak of the Korean War in June 1950 led to a major expansion of the Navy. Many ships were reactivated from the reserve fleets so that newer vessels could be released for service in the Far East. Halvorsen was recommissioned at Charleston on 9 February 1951, with Commander Lawrence D. Pike in command.

Before recommissioning, her torpedo tube mount was removed and replaced with additional anti-aircraft weapons. Her anti-submarine equipment was also improved, including the installation of a Hedgehog forward-firing mortar. Her radar suite was modernized at the same time.

For most of her second commission, Halvorsen was based at Newport, Rhode Island. She served as a training ship for naval reservists and for students of the Fleet Sonar School at Key West, Florida. In 1952, she took part in the NATO exercise Mainbrace in the North Atlantic. During the exercise she operated as part of the anti-submarine screen for an Allied carrier group off Norway. In 1953, she made a midshipman training cruise with stops at Lisbon, Portugal, and Gibraltar.

On 18 January 1954, while operating in heavy weather off Cape Hatteras, Halvorsen answered a distress call from the Liberian-flagged freighter Santa Teodora, which had lost power and was drifting toward the shoals. The destroyer stood by the freighter through the night and passed a towing line at first light. She held the vessel in position until a Coast Guard cutter arrived to take over the tow. The ship's company received a letter of commendation from the Commandant of the Coast Guard.

With the end of hostilities in Korea, the Navy began reducing its active fleet. Halvorsen was decommissioned for the second and last time at Philadelphia on 30 October 1954 and returned to the Atlantic Reserve Fleet.

= = Disposal = =

Halvorsen remained in reserve at Philadelphia for more than fifteen years. By the 1960s, the Benson and Gleaves classes were considered too small for modernization under the Fleet Rehabilitation and Modernization program, which instead concentrated on the larger Fletcher, Allen M. Sumner, and Gearing classes. In 1968, Halvorsen was briefly considered for transfer to a foreign navy under the Military Assistance Program, but the proposal was not carried forward.

She was struck from the Naval Vessel Register on 1 July 1971. On 22 June 1972, she was sold to the Union Minerals and Alloys Corporation of New York and was later broken up for scrap at Kearny, New Jersey.

= = Awards = =

Halvorsen received five battle stars for her service in the Second World War, for the following operations:

* North African occupation (Operation Torch), November 1942
* Sicilian occupation (Operation Husky), July 1943
* Salerno landings (Operation Avalanche), September 1943
* West coast of Italy operations, including the Anzio and Nettuno beachhead, February–March 1944
* Invasion of southern France (Operation Dragoon), August 1944

She also received the American Campaign Medal, the European–African–Middle Eastern Campaign Medal, the World War II Victory Medal, the Navy Occupation Service Medal, and the National Defense Service Medal.

= = Legacy = =

The ship's bell was removed before she was scrapped. It was presented to the city of Duluth, Minnesota, where it is displayed at a maritime museum on the waterfront alongside a portrait of Lieutenant Commander Einar Halvorsen. A reunion association of former crew members met annually from 1961 until 2009, when it was dissolved because of the advanced age of its remaining members. The association's papers, which include wartime diaries, photographs, and copies of the ship's deck logs, were donated to a naval history archive in Washington, D.C.

No other ship of the United States Navy has borne the name Halvorsen.
