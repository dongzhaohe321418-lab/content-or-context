The Lantern Keeper is a musical with music by Daniel Okafor-Reyes, lyrics by Miriam Halberstadt, and a book by Thomas Ellery Vance. It is based loosely on the 1931 novel *The Light at Gannet Rock* by the Canadian author Agnes Pell. The story concerns Eleanor Marsh, the widow of a lighthouse keeper on a remote island off the coast of Nova Scotia. After her husband's death she takes over his duties in secret, so that the government will not remove her from the post. The musical follows her over three years. During that time she shelters the survivors of a shipwreck, has an uneasy friendship with a visiting inspector, and comes to terms with her grief and her isolation.

The show premiered at the Goodspeed Opera House in East Haddam, Connecticut, in the summer of 2009. It opened on Broadway at the Ethel Barrymore Theatre on March 14, 2011, and ran for 612 performances before closing on September 2, 2012. It received eleven Tony Award nominations and won four, including Best Original Score and Best Performance by a Leading Actress in a Musical for Catherine Doyle-Ashby. Critics praised its score, which uses folk and maritime musical idioms, and its sparse staging. Some reviewers found the second act slow. A North American tour, a London production, and several regional and international productions followed.

= = History = =

= = = Origins and development = = =

Okafor-Reyes and Halberstadt first met in 2002, when both were students in the BMI Lehman Engel Musical Theatre Workshop in New York. They wrote several short pieces together, including a song cycle called *Small Harbors*, which was performed at a cabaret venue in Greenwich Village in 2004. Halberstadt later said she had found Pell's novel in a used bookshop in Halifax while visiting relatives. She gave a copy to Okafor-Reyes, who had grown up in a fishing community on the coast of Maine. He recalled that the novel "read like something I already knew, even though I had never seen it before."

The pair obtained the stage rights from Pell's estate in 2005. They began working with Vance, a playwright best known at the time for the off-Broadway drama *Cold Wells*. Early drafts kept the novel's framing device, in which an elderly Eleanor tells her story to a journalist. Vance cut this device in 2007, saying that it "drained the urgency out of every scene." The writers also merged several of the novel's minor characters. The most significant change was the creation of Inspector Robert Calloway from two separate government officials in the book.

A staged reading took place at the Eugene O'Neill Theater Center's National Music Theater Conference in July 2007, with Laura Benning as Eleanor. The reading attracted the attention of producer Harold Stavros. He secured a developmental production at Goodspeed's Norma Terris Theatre in Chester, Connecticut, which ran for six weeks in 2008. Director Sarah Wendt-Morales joined the project at this stage. Her approach was minimalist: a single revolving platform and a large rotating light fixture that represented the lighthouse lamp.

= = = Goodspeed production = = =

The full premiere took place on the Goodspeed Opera House main stage from June 19 to August 30, 2009. Catherine Doyle-Ashby, a Canadian actress known mainly for her work at the Stratford Festival, was cast as Eleanor after a lengthy search. Michael Trent played Calloway. The production was a commercial success for the theatre and was extended twice. In a review for *The Hartford Courant*, Frank Delgado called it "the most quietly devastating new musical to come out of Connecticut in a decade." He praised Doyle-Ashby's performance in particular.

During the Goodspeed run, the writers replaced three songs. They also substantially rewrote the second-act opening, which had originally been a comic number for the shipwreck survivors. The replacement, "What the Water Takes," later became one of the show's best-known songs.

= = = Broadway = = =

Stavros, together with co-producers Linden Theatrical Group and Mariner Productions, announced in early 2010 that the show would transfer to Broadway. The Ethel Barrymore Theatre was chosen partly for its relatively intimate size. Wendt-Morales argued that the show's close focus on a few characters would suffer in a larger house.

Previews began on February 18, 2011, and the official opening was on March 14. Doyle-Ashby and Trent returned to their roles. Newcomer Julian Abernathy played Tobias Wren, the young sailor rescued from the wreck. The production had a capitalization of approximately $9.2 million, which was modest by the standards of Broadway musicals of the period.

Ticket sales were slow during the first weeks. They improved substantially after the Tony nominations were announced in May 2011, and again after the cast performed "Keep the Light" on the awards telecast. The production recouped its investment in February 2012. It closed on September 2, 2012, after 612 regular performances and 28 previews. Doyle-Ashby left the production in April 2012 and was succeeded by Janine Forsythe for the final months of the run.

= = Synopsis = =

= = = Act One = = =

The musical opens in 1924 on Gannet Rock, a small island about eleven miles off the southern coast of Nova Scotia. Eleanor Marsh and her husband, Samuel, have kept the lighthouse there for nine years. In the opening number ("The Turning"), the ensemble, representing the sea and the voices of the mainland, describes the routine of the light. Samuel falls ill during a winter storm. With no way to reach the mainland, he dies after several days ("Hold Fast").

Eleanor knows that the Department of Marine and Fisheries will not allow a woman to keep the light alone. She buries Samuel on the island and continues to send his monthly reports under his name ("His Hand, My Hand"). The supply boat captain, an old man named Jory Pike, discovers the deception. He agrees to keep her secret, partly out of loyalty to Samuel ("Old Men Don't Talk").

Months pass. Eleanor struggles with solitude and begins talking aloud to her dead husband ("Conversations"). In the autumn of 1925 a schooner, the *Margaret Hale*, runs aground on the reef below the lighthouse. Eleanor rescues four survivors. They are Tobias Wren, a young sailor; Hester and Abel Crane, a married couple travelling to Boston; and a cook named Mr. Oduya. The survivors are stranded on the island for several weeks until the weather clears. Their arrival disrupts Eleanor's careful isolation ("Strangers in the House").

Tobias becomes attached to Eleanor and helps her with the work of the light. Meanwhile the Cranes' marriage shows signs of strain. The act ends as the supply boat arrives carrying Inspector Robert Calloway, who has been sent to look into irregularities in Samuel's reports. Eleanor greets him at the landing, and the act closes on the company number "Keep the Light."

= = = Act Two = = =

The second act opens with "What the Water Takes," in which the survivors and the ensemble reflect on what the shipwreck cost each of them. Calloway quickly suspects that Samuel is dead, but he does not reveal what he knows. He stays on the island for some time, ostensibly to inspect the equipment. During this period he and Eleanor develop a guarded respect for each other ("The Inspector's Waltz").

The Cranes leave on the supply boat, having decided to separate when they reach Boston ("Two Tickets South"). Tobias, who has fallen in love with Eleanor, asks her to leave the island with him. She refuses, recognizing that his feelings come from gratitude and youth ("Not Yet, Not You"). Tobias departs with Mr. Oduya.

Calloway confronts Eleanor with the truth, and she admits the deception. In the musical's climactic scene, he recounts how his own wife died while he was stationed far away ("A Man Who Wasn't There"). He admits that he envies the loyalty Eleanor has shown. He returns to the mainland and files a report recommending that "the present keeper" be retained, without naming her.

In the final scene, set in 1927, Eleanor receives a letter from the Department confirming her appointment under her own name. She lights the lamp alone as the ensemble reprises "The Turning," and the stage slowly goes dark except for the rotating beam.

= = Music and style = =

Okafor-Reyes's score draws heavily on the folk traditions of Atlantic Canada and New England, including sea shanties, Celtic fiddle music, and Protestant hymnody. The original Broadway orchestra had eleven players. The orchestration, by Leah Kowalczyk, featured fiddle, accordion, concertina, cello, and a pump organ. Brass was largely avoided, and Okafor-Reyes said he wanted the orchestra to sound "like weather, not like a band." Several numbers, including "Hold Fast" and "Letter from Ottawa," are accompanied mainly by solo instruments.

Halberstadt's lyrics were noted for their restraint and their use of regional vocabulary. In *The New York Times*, critic Ben Aldridge observed that the lyrics "rarely reach for a rhyme they cannot earn." Commentators often singled out "His Hand, My Hand" as an example of how the show uses song to dramatize character. In it, Eleanor gradually adopts her husband's phrasing as she writes his report, and the melody shifts from her own theme to his.

A recurring motif of a descending three-note figure, associated with the rotation of the lamp, runs throughout the score. It appears in the opening, in the act one finale, and in the final reprise. Musicologist Patricia Hennessey has described the motif as "a structural clock" that marks the passage of time in a show otherwise lacking in spectacle.

= = Production design = =

Scenic designer Ivo Lindqvist created a single unit set: a raised, rotating wooden platform surrounded by a shallow pool of water, which was lit from below. The lighthouse was not shown directly. It was suggested instead by a large suspended lens assembly that rotated above the stage throughout the performance. Lighting designer Ama Kessler used the beam from this assembly to sweep across the audience at key moments. Many reviewers commented on the effect. Costume designs by Henrietta Vole were based on photographs of Maritime fishing families from the 1920s.

The water feature required a specialized drainage and heating system, and it was cited as one of the production's main technical challenges. During the first week of previews, a malfunction flooded part of the orchestra pit, and two performances were cancelled.

= = Reception = =

= = = Critical response = = =

The Broadway production received generally positive reviews. Aldridge, writing for *The New York Times*, called it "a chamber musical of rare patience and integrity." He praised Doyle-Ashby's performance as "the kind of work that makes you hold your breath for fear of disturbing it." In *Variety*, Marcus Lindell described the score as "the finest of the season." He expressed reservations about the second act, however, which he found "becalmed in places."

Some critics were less enthusiastic. Joanne Ferris of *New York* magazine described the show as "handsome, sincere, and a little airless." She suggested that its restraint sometimes turned into monotony. Several reviewers felt that the subplot involving the Crane marriage was underdeveloped. The character of Mr. Oduya was also criticized as thinly written. In later productions the writers expanded his role, adding a new song, "Salt and Bread," for the 2014 tour.

= = = Commercial performance = = =

The Lantern Keeper took several months to build an audience. Industry observers credited its eventual profitability to strong word of mouth and its success at the Tony Awards. It reached its highest weekly gross in the week ending January 1, 2012, taking in $1.04 million. The production was also noted for drawing a higher-than-average share of audience members from Canada.

= = Subsequent productions = =

= = = North American tour = = =

A non-Equity North American tour opened at the Neptune Theatre in Halifax, Nova Scotia, in October 2013. It then played thirty-one cities across the United States and Canada through 2015. Directed by Wendt-Morales's associate Colin Avery, the tour replaced the water feature with a projection system so that the set could travel more easily. Kara Lindholm played Eleanor.

= = = London = = =

The show opened in the West End at the Duchess Theatre in February 2016, with British actress Fiona Galbraith as Eleanor. The London production received mixed reviews and closed after five months. It was later nominated for the Laurence Olivier Award for Best New Musical but did not win.

= = = Other productions = =

The musical has been widely licensed to regional, community, and educational theatres since 2016, and it is especially popular in Atlantic Canada. A Japanese-language production opened in Tokyo in 2018. A German translation, *Der Leuchtturmwärter*, premiered in Hamburg in 2019. In 2022 the Stratford Festival mounted a revival, with Doyle-Ashby returning to the role of Eleanor more than a decade after the Broadway premiere. Critics widely described the production as definitive.

= = Legacy = =

The Lantern Keeper is often cited as part of a wave of small-scale, character-driven musicals that found commercial success on Broadway in the early 2010s. Theatre historian Elaine Ruskin has called it "a proof of concept for the idea that intimacy could be a selling point rather than a liability." The success of the show also renewed interest in Pell's novel, which was reissued by McClelland & Stewart in 2012 after being out of print for several decades.

"Keep the Light" and "His Hand, My Hand" have become standard audition and cabaret repertoire. "What the Water Takes" has been performed at several memorial events in Nova Scotia, including a 2018 commemoration of those lost at sea off the province's coast.
