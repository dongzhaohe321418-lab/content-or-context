Halvorsen's lanternshark

Halvorsen's lanternshark ( Etmopterus halvorseni ) is a species of small deep-water dogfish shark in the family Etmopteridae. It is known from the continental slopes and seamounts of the southwestern Indian Ocean, principally off southern Mozambique, the eastern coast of South Africa, and the southern Madagascar Ridge, at depths between 480 and 1,350 m ( 1,570 and 4,430 ft ). Like other members of its genus, it possesses bioluminescent photophores on its ventral surface and flanks, which produce a faint blue-green light. Adults typically measure 32 – 41 cm ( 13 – 16 in ) in total length, with females reaching larger sizes than males.

The species was formally described in 1987, although specimens had been collected during exploratory trawl surveys more than two decades earlier and had been misidentified as the related velvet belly lanternshark ( Etmopterus spinax ) and the African lanternshark ( Etmopterus polli ). It is distinguished from its relatives by a distinctive arrangement of flank markings, the shape of its dermal denticles, and a comparatively elongated caudal region. Halvorsen's lanternshark feeds primarily on small bony fishes, cephalopods, and crustaceans. It is aplacental viviparous, giving birth to litters of between four and eleven pups.

The International Union for Conservation of Nature ( IUCN ) has assessed the species as Near Threatened, citing its low reproductive rate and its frequent capture as bycatch in deep-water crustacean trawl fisheries.

= = Taxonomy = =

Halvorsen's lanternshark was described by the South African ichthyologist Margaret E. Coetzee and the Norwegian marine biologist Pål S. Haugland in a 1987 issue of the Annals of the Durban Natural History Museum. The specific epithet honours Einar Halvorsen, the Norwegian fisheries scientist who led the 1964 – 1966 research cruises aboard the trawler RV Sørkapp, during which the first specimens were collected. The holotype, an adult female measuring 38.6 cm ( 15.2 in ) in total length, was trawled from a depth of 760 m ( 2,490 ft ) off Inhaca Island, Mozambique, in March 1965. Nineteen paratypes were designated, collected from various localities between Maputo Bay and the Agulhas Bank.

Prior to its formal description, specimens of the species had been deposited in museum collections in Durban, Cape Town, and Bergen under several different names. Coetzee and Haugland noted in their original description that at least 34 catalogued specimens labelled as Etmopterus spinax were in fact referable to the new species, and that the confusion had led earlier authors to report the velvet belly lanternshark from the southwestern Indian Ocean in error.

Within the genus Etmopterus, Halvorsen's lanternshark has traditionally been placed within the " Etmopterus spinax clade ", a group of species characterised by irregularly arranged, hooked dermal denticles and the presence of elongated flank markings that extend both anterior and posterior to the base of the pelvic fins. A molecular phylogenetic study published in 2011, based on mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I ( COI ) and NADH dehydrogenase subunit 2 ( ND2 ) sequences, recovered E. halvorseni as the sister taxon to Etmopterus granulosus, with the two lineages estimated to have diverged during the late Miocene. A subsequent study in 2019, incorporating nuclear markers, supported a slightly different arrangement in which E. halvorseni formed a clade with E. granulosus and an as - yet undescribed lanternshark from the Mozambique Channel. The relationships within the spinax clade remain the subject of ongoing research.

Common names for the species include Halvorsen's lanternshark, Mozambique lanternshark, and, in some South African fisheries literature, the " black - belly spiny dogfish ". In Portuguese - language publications from Mozambique it is sometimes referred to as tubarão - lanterna - de - Halvorsen.

= = Description = =

Halvorsen's lanternshark is a slender - bodied shark with a moderately long, flattened snout and large, oval eyes positioned laterally on the head. The eyes are equipped with a reflective tapetum lucidum, which gives them a greenish sheen in fresh specimens. The spiracles are moderately large and situated just behind and slightly above the eyes. The nostrils are preceded by short, triangular flaps of skin. The mouth is broadly curved, with short furrows at the corners.

The teeth of the upper and lower jaws differ markedly in shape, a condition common in the genus. The upper teeth are small, with a narrow central cusp flanked by two to three pairs of smaller lateral cusplets; these teeth are arranged in several functional rows and are used for grasping prey. The lower teeth are larger and blade - like, with a single strongly oblique cusp, and are interlocked to form a continuous cutting edge. There are typically 24 – 30 upper tooth rows and 32 – 38 lower tooth rows.

There are two dorsal fins, each preceded by a grooved spine. The first dorsal fin is small and positioned well behind the pectoral fins, with its spine noticeably shorter than the fin itself. The second dorsal fin is approximately twice the area of the first, and its spine is long, slightly curved, and may exceed the height of the fin. The pectoral fins are small and rounded. There is no anal fin, a characteristic of the order Squaliformes. The caudal peduncle is relatively long compared to related species, accounting for approximately 11 – 13 % of total length, and the caudal fin has a well - developed lower lobe and a ventral notch near the tip of the upper lobe.

The dermal denticles are small, widely spaced, and hooked, with a single conical crown curving toward the rear of the body. They are irregularly scattered over the body and do not form the regular longitudinal rows found in some other lanternsharks. The density of denticles is lower on the underside of the head and abdomen, giving the skin of these regions a smoother texture.

The dorsal coloration is dark brown to greyish - brown, becoming abruptly black on the ventral surface. The demarcation between the dorsal and ventral coloration is sharp along the sides of the head and trunk. A distinctive black marking is present above and behind the pelvic fins; its anterior branch is long and narrow, extending forward approximately to the level of the first dorsal fin spine, while the posterior branch is shorter and broader. A second, smaller dark marking is present at the base of the caudal fin, and a faint dark stripe runs along the midline of the upper caudal lobe. The fin margins are pale or translucent in life.

Females mature at approximately 33 cm ( 13 in ) and reach a maximum recorded length of 44.2 cm ( 17.4 in ). Males mature at approximately 29 cm ( 11 in ) and are not known to exceed 37 cm ( 15 in ). The maximum recorded weight is 410 g ( 14 oz ), from a gravid female collected off Durban in 2003.

= = = Bioluminescence = = =

Like all lanternsharks, Halvorsen's lanternshark possesses numerous minute photophores embedded in the skin, which emit light through an intrinsic chemical reaction. The photophores are concentrated on the ventral surface of the body and within the black flank and caudal markings, and are absent from the dorsal surface. In a 2014 study, live specimens maintained briefly in a darkened shipboard aquarium emitted a continuous blue - green glow with a peak wavelength of approximately 484 nm, closely matching the spectrum of downwelling sunlight at the depths the species inhabits.

The ventral photophores are believed to function in counter - illumination, a form of camouflage in which the animal's silhouette is obscured when viewed from below against the faint light filtering from the surface. The concentration of photophores within the flank markings, by contrast, produces a distinct luminous pattern visible from the side. Researchers have proposed that these markings may serve in intraspecific communication, such as species recognition, schooling, or mate identification. The second dorsal fin spine is also associated with a cluster of photophores, and it has been suggested that illumination of the spine may act as a warning signal to potential predators.

Light emission in the species is controlled hormonally rather than neurally. Experiments on excised skin samples have shown that melatonin and prolactin induce light production, while adrenocorticotropic hormone inhibits it, a pattern consistent with that recorded in other members of the family Etmopteridae.

= = Distribution and habitat = =

Halvorsen's lanternshark is endemic to the southwestern Indian Ocean. Its documented range extends along the continental slope from the vicinity of Bazaruto Island in central Mozambique southward to the region off East London in South Africa's Eastern Cape province. Additional populations have been recorded on the Madagascar Ridge and on the Walters Shoals, a group of seamounts approximately 700 km ( 430 mi ) south of Madagascar. Isolated records from the southern Mozambique Channel and from waters off southwestern Madagascar have been reported, but some of these are considered doubtful and may represent the related undescribed species noted in phylogenetic studies.

The species is demersal or benthopelagic, occurring on or near the bottom on the upper and middle continental slope. The majority of specimens have been captured between 600 and 1,000 m ( 2,000 and 3,300 ft ), with the shallowest confirmed record at 480 m ( 1,570 ft ) and the deepest at 1,350 m ( 4,430 ft ). It is most frequently associated with soft sediment substrates of mud and fine sand, although specimens collected on the Walters Shoals came from areas of mixed rocky and sandy bottom. Water temperatures within its range typically vary between 4 and 10 ° C ( 39 and 50 ° F ).

There is evidence of size and sex segregation within the species. Trawl surveys off KwaZulu - Natal in the 1990s found that juveniles were most abundant at the shallower end of the species' depth range, between 480 and 650 m, while large adult females predominated at depths greater than 900 m. Catches frequently consisted of individuals of predominantly one sex, suggesting that males and females may occupy partially separate areas outside the breeding season.

= = Biology and ecology = =

= = = Diet = = =

Halvorsen's lanternshark is an opportunistic predator feeding on a range of small midwater and bottom - dwelling animals. An analysis of the stomach contents of 312 specimens collected off southern Mozambique between 1998 and 2001 found that bony fishes were the most important prey by mass, accounting for approximately 48 % of identifiable stomach contents. Lanternfishes ( family Myctophidae ) and bristlemouths ( family Gonostomatidae ) were the most frequently identified fish prey, followed by small grenadiers ( family Macrouridae ). Cephalopods, mainly small squids of the families Enoploteuthidae and Histioteuthidae, accounted for approximately 27 % of prey mass, while crustaceans, including penaeid and pandalid shrimps and euphausiids, made up most of the remainder.

The composition of the diet changes with size. Juveniles below 20 cm ( 7.9 in ) feed predominantly on crustaceans, while larger individuals consume progressively greater proportions of fishes and cephalopods. The presence in some stomachs of cephalopod beaks belonging to squids considerably larger than the shark itself has led to the suggestion that the species may feed in groups, or may scavenge on dead and dying prey. Its blade - like lower teeth are capable of excising pieces of flesh from larger animals, a feeding method that has been documented in some other lanternsharks.

Many of the prey species consumed by Halvorsen's lanternshark undertake diel vertical migrations, rising toward the surface at night. Stomach fullness data suggest that the shark feeds most actively during daylight hours, when these animals are concentrated at depth, although the extent to which the shark itself migrates vertically remains unknown.

= = = Predators and parasites = = =

Known predators of Halvorsen's lanternshark include larger sharks, such as the bluntnose sixgill shark ( Hexanchus griseus ) and the kitefin shark ( Dalatias licha ), as well as large bony fishes such as the oilfish ( Ruvettus pretiosus ). Remains have also been recovered from the stomachs of swordfish ( Xiphias gladius ) caught by longline off the KwaZulu - Natal coast.

Parasites recorded from the species include the tapeworm Aberrapex coetzeeae, which was described from its spiral valve in 1995 and named in honour of the shark's co - describer, and the copepod Eudactylina oliveri, which attaches to the gill filaments. A species of monogenean fluke of the genus Squalonchocotyle has also been recorded from the gills, though it has not been identified to species level.

= = = Reproduction and life history = = =

Halvorsen's lanternshark is aplacental viviparous ( ovoviviparous ), with the developing embryos sustained by a yolk sac until birth. Mature females possess a single functional ovary, located on the right side, and two functional uteri. Ovarian eggs measure up to 2.4 cm ( 0.94 in ) in diameter before ovulation. Litter size ranges from four to eleven, with an average of approximately seven, and shows a positive correlation with the length of the mother. Pups measure between 12 and 14 cm ( 4.7 and 5.5 in ) at birth.

Gravid females have been recorded throughout the year, indicating that reproduction is not strongly seasonal. However, a 2008 study of females collected off Durban reported that the proportion of females bearing near - term embryos peaked between August and November, suggesting that parturition may be concentrated in the austral spring. The length of the gestation period has not been determined directly; on the basis of embryonic growth rates, it has been estimated at between 18 and 24 months, and a resting period may follow birth before the next ovulation, meaning that females likely reproduce no more often than every two to three years.

Age and growth have been studied through the counting of growth bands on the second dorsal fin spine. A 2012 study estimated that males reach maturity at an age of approximately seven years and females at approximately nine years. The maximum age recorded was 19 years for a female and 15 years for a male. These figures indicate a slow - growing, late - maturing species, a pattern typical of deep - water sharks and one that renders the species vulnerable to population decline.

Embryos develop functional photophores before birth. Photophores have been observed on embryos measuring as little as 7 cm ( 2.8 in ), and near - term pups are capable of producing light, which has been interpreted as an adaptation allowing them to employ counter - illumination immediately upon birth.

= = History = =

The first specimens of Halvorsen's lanternshark were collected during a series of Norwegian - South African exploratory fishing surveys conducted between 1964 and 1966. The surveys, coordinated by Einar Halvorsen of the Institute of Marine Research in Bergen, were intended to assess the commercial potential of deep - water crustacean and fish stocks along the southeast African slope. More than 200 small dogfish were collected during the cruises; most were preserved and sent to institutions in Bergen and Durban, where they were initially catalogued as Etmopterus spinax.

In 1979 Coetzee, then working at the Durban Natural History Museum, began a review of the region's deep - water sharks. She noted that the specimens identified as E. spinax differed from North Atlantic material in the form of the flank markings and the arrangement of the denticles. Through correspondence with Haugland, who had independently observed similar discrepancies in the Bergen material, the two researchers undertook a joint comparison of specimens from both collections, culminating in the formal description of the species eight years later.

During the 1990s and early 2000s the species attracted increasing scientific attention as a result of its regular occurrence in the bycatch of the expanding deep - water shrimp trawl fishery off Mozambique. Much of the existing knowledge of its diet and reproductive biology derives from specimens obtained through fisheries observer programmes during this period. The first live observation of the species in its natural habitat was made in 2016, when a remotely operated vehicle filmed a group of four individuals swimming close to the bottom at a depth of 830 m ( 2,720 ft ) on the Walters Shoals during an expedition organised by the French National Museum of Natural History.

= = Relationship to humans = =

Halvorsen's lanternshark is not targeted by any fishery. Its small size and the low market value of its flesh mean that individuals caught incidentally are almost always discarded, and very few are likely to survive capture owing to the barotrauma and physical damage associated with being hauled from great depths. The species poses no danger to humans.

The principal source of mortality from human activity is the crustacean trawl fishery operating on the slope off southern Mozambique, which targets deep - water prawns and langoustines. Observer data from this fishery between 1999 and 2006 indicated that lanternsharks, the majority of which were E. halvorseni, made up between 3 and 9 % of the total catch by weight in some trawl hauls. Smaller numbers are taken by South African demersal trawlers on the Eastern Cape slope and by longline fisheries targeting deep - water fishes on seamounts in the southwestern Indian Ocean.

= = Conservation = =

The International Union for Conservation of Nature first evaluated Halvorsen's lanternshark in 2004 as Data Deficient. A reassessment in 2020 listed the species as Near Threatened, based on its slow life history, its limited geographic range, and the substantial overlap between its preferred depth range and areas of intensive trawling. The assessment noted that standardised catch rates from research surveys off KwaZulu - Natal showed a decline of approximately 25 % between 1990 and 2015, although the reliability of these data was limited by changes in survey methodology.

The Walters Shoals are partly protected by a benthic fishing closure established in 2006 by the Southern Indian Ocean Fisheries Agreement, which prohibits bottom trawling over a portion of the seamount complex. The iSimangaliso Marine Protected Area off northern KwaZulu - Natal, which was expanded in 2019 to encompass offshore waters, also covers part of the species' range on the upper slope. Researchers have recommended improved monitoring of bycatch in the Mozambican trawl fishery, the adoption of species - specific catch reporting, and further studies of population structure to determine whether the Madagascar Ridge and seamount populations are genetically distinct from those on the African continental slope.
