Étienne-Marcel Lavergne

Étienne-Marcel Lavergne, comte de Saint-Aulaye (14 March 1768 – 22 November 1834) was a French military officer who served in the armies of the French Revolution and the First French Empire, rising to the rank of général de division. He is best known for commanding a division of the IV Corps during the War of the Fifth Coalition, for his defence of the town of Kreuzbach in 1809, and for his administration of the occupied Illyrian district of Gorizia between 1810 and 1812. Captured during the retreat from Leipzig in 1813, he spent the final months of the Napoleonic Wars as a prisoner in Bohemia. He later served the Bourbon Restoration as an inspector of infantry and sat briefly in the Chamber of Peers under the July Monarchy. His name is inscribed on the east pillar of the Arc de Triomphe.

= = Early life = =

Lavergne was born in Ribérac, in the province of Périgord, the third of five children of Pierre Lavergne, a notary, and Marguerite Delpeuch, the daughter of a local tanner. The family was prosperous but not noble. He was educated at the Oratorian college in Périgueux, where his teachers recorded an aptitude for mathematics and drawing. His father intended him for the law, and in 1785 he was placed as a clerk in the office of a procureur in Bordeaux.

In 1787, against his father's wishes, Lavergne enlisted as a private soldier in the Régiment de Guyenne. Surviving regimental registers describe him as 1.76 metres tall, with brown hair and a scar on the left hand, the result of a childhood accident. He was promoted to corporal in 1788 and to sergeant in early 1790. His literacy and his knowledge of legal procedure led to his employment as a regimental clerk, a post that kept him in garrison at Perpignan during the first years of the Revolution.

= = Revolutionary Wars = =

= = = Army of the Pyrenees = = =

When war with Spain broke out in 1793, Lavergne was elected captain by the men of the 2nd Battalion of Volunteers of the Dordogne, a unit raised largely from his home district. The battalion was attached to the Army of the Eastern Pyrenees and saw action at the Battle of Mas Deu in May 1793, where it lost nearly a third of its strength. Lavergne was wounded in the thigh during the withdrawal and was mentioned in a report by the representative on mission, who praised his "coolness under fire and his care for the wounded."

After his recovery he was promoted to chef de bataillon in December 1793. During the campaign of 1794 he served under General Pérignon at the Battle of the Black Mountain, commanding a column that seized a Spanish redoubt near the village of Sant Llorenç. Following the amalgamation of volunteer and line units, he became second-in-command of the 61st Demi-Brigade of Line Infantry. He took part in the siege of Roses in the winter of 1794–1795, during which he contracted a fever that left him unfit for duty for several months.

= = = Italy and Switzerland = = =

After the Peace of Basel ended hostilities with Spain in 1795, the 61st Demi-Brigade was transferred to the Army of Italy. Lavergne arrived in Nice in early 1796 and participated in the opening phase of Bonaparte's Italian campaign. He was present at the battles of Lodi and Castiglione, though his unit was held largely in reserve at the latter. At the Battle of Arcole in November 1796 he led two battalions in a series of unsuccessful attempts to cross the dikes along the Alpone and was wounded a second time, in the shoulder.

His conduct in Italy brought him to the attention of General André Masséna, under whom he later served in Switzerland. Promoted to chef de brigade in 1798, he commanded the 61st Demi-Brigade during the campaign of 1799. At the Second Battle of Zurich in September of that year, his demi-brigade formed part of the force that crossed the Limmat at Dietikon before dawn, and he was credited in Masséna's report with the capture of six guns and some 800 Russian prisoners. He was promoted to général de brigade on the battlefield, a promotion confirmed by the Directory in October 1799.

= = = Consulate = = =

During the Consulate, Lavergne held a series of garrison and administrative commands. From 1800 to 1802 he commanded a brigade in the Army of the Rhine, though he saw little fighting and was not present at Hohenlinden. In 1803 he was appointed to command the military department of the Charente-Inférieure, with headquarters at La Rochelle, where he was responsible for coastal defence and the suppression of brigandage. Contemporary correspondence suggests he found the post tedious, and he made repeated requests to be returned to field service.

In 1804 he was made a member of the Legion of Honour and, the following year, a commander of the order. He married Hortense-Adélaïde Rouffignac, the daughter of a Bordeaux shipowner, in April 1804. The couple had three children, two of whom survived to adulthood.

= = Napoleonic Wars = =

= = = Campaigns of 1805–1807 = = =

At the formation of the Grande Armée in 1805, Lavergne was given command of a brigade in the division of General Saint-Hilaire, part of Marshal Soult's IV Corps. He marched with the corps through Bavaria and Austria and was present at the Battle of Austerlitz on 2 December 1805. His brigade was engaged in the assault on the Pratzen Heights, and he was slightly wounded by a musket ball that struck the hilt of his sabre. Soult's after-action report described his brigade as having "held its ground against repeated counter-attacks of the Russian Guard."

During the War of the Fourth Coalition, Lavergne fought at Jena in October 1806 and took part in the pursuit of the Prussian army to the Baltic coast, including the operations around Lübeck. In the winter campaign in Poland he was present at the Battle of Eylau in February 1807, where his brigade suffered heavy losses in the snowstorm that disrupted the French centre. He was promoted to général de division in March 1807 and received a dotation of lands in the former Prussian province of Westphalia, worth an annual income of 10,000 francs. He was created a baron of the Empire in 1808.

= = = Fifth Coalition and Kreuzbach = = =

In 1809, at the outbreak of war with Austria, Lavergne was given command of the 3rd Division of IV Corps, now under Marshal Masséna. The division comprised some 9,000 men in four regiments of line and light infantry, together with two batteries of foot artillery.

His most notable independent action of the war took place at Kreuzbach, a small market town on the right bank of the Danube, between 4 and 7 May 1809. Ordered to hold the town and its bridge while the main body of the corps moved eastward, Lavergne found himself facing an Austrian force of approximately 18,000 men under Feldmarschall-Leutnant Anton von Hessler. Over three days the French garrison repelled four major assaults. Lavergne barricaded the principal streets, loopholed the walls of the town's Capuchin monastery, and used his artillery to sweep the approaches to the bridge. On the third day, with ammunition running short and the bridge partially burned, he organised a counter-attack by two battalions of the 46th Line that drove the Austrians from the suburb of Unterau.

The defence of Kreuzbach delayed Hessler's column long enough to prevent it from joining the Austrian concentration near Vienna. French casualties were estimated at 1,600 killed and wounded; Austrian losses were reported at about 3,000. Napoleon referred to the action in the bulletin of 9 May, and Lavergne was made a grand officer of the Legion of Honour shortly afterwards.

Lavergne's division fought at Aspern-Essling later that month, where it was committed to the defence of Aspern village on the second day of the battle. At Wagram in July, the division formed part of the French left and was heavily engaged against the Austrian III Corps. Lavergne was thrown from his horse when it was killed beneath him but continued in command. He was created comte de Saint-Aulaye in 1810, taking the title from a small estate near Ribérac that he had purchased in 1806.

= = = Illyria = = =

Following the Treaty of Schönbrunn, which ceded substantial Austrian territories along the Adriatic to France, Lavergne was appointed military governor of the district of Gorizia within the newly created Illyrian Provinces. He held this post from February 1810 until early 1812.

His administration was marked by efforts to reorganise local government along French lines, introduce the Napoleonic Code, and improve roads linking Gorizia to Ljubljana and Trieste. He oversaw the construction of a military road through the Vipava valley and established a primary school for the children of the local garrison and civil servants. He also enforced the Continental System, which caused considerable hardship among merchants dependent on trade with Britain and its allies, and which led to a riot in Gorizia in August 1811 that he suppressed with the garrison.

Historians have offered differing assessments of his tenure. Some have described him as a capable and relatively moderate administrator who protected local clergy from excessive requisitions; others have emphasised his rigorous conscription policy and the harshness of his response to the 1811 disturbances. His surviving correspondence with Governor-General Bertrand indicates frequent disagreements over the extent to which local customs should be accommodated.

= = = Campaigns of 1812–1813 = = =

Lavergne was recalled to France in early 1812 but was not assigned to the army being assembled for the invasion of Russia, an omission that has been variously attributed to his age, his poor health, and a quarrel with Marshal Berthier over his conduct in Illyria. He instead commanded the 11th Military Division at Bordeaux, where he was responsible for organising reinforcements and the National Guard.

After the destruction of the Grande Armée in Russia, he was recalled to active service in March 1813 and given command of a division in the newly formed III Corps under Marshal Ney. The division was composed largely of young conscripts, and Lavergne complained in letters to his wife about their lack of training and equipment. He fought at Lützen in May 1813, where his division held the village of Kaja against Prussian attacks for several hours, and at Bautzen later that month.

During the autumn campaign he was engaged at the Battle of Dennewitz in September, where his division was routed. At the Battle of Leipzig in October, his division was posted to the north of the city. During the chaotic retreat across the Elster on 19 October, after the premature destruction of the principal bridge, Lavergne and much of his staff were cut off. He was captured by Austrian troops near the suburb of Lindenau and sent as a prisoner of war to the fortress of Theresienstadt in Bohemia.

= = Restoration and later life = =

= = = Return to France = = =

Lavergne was released following the abdication of Napoleon in April 1814 and returned to France in June. He submitted to the restored Bourbon monarchy and was made a knight of the Order of Saint Louis. During the Hundred Days in 1815 he declined to rejoin Napoleon, pleading ill health, and remained at his estate in the Dordogne. This decision allowed him to avoid the purges that followed the second Restoration, but it was criticised by some former comrades; General Delmas reportedly described him as "a man who knew how to fall ill at the right moment."

= = = Inspector of infantry = = =

From 1816 to 1823 Lavergne served as inspector-general of infantry for several military divisions in the south-west of France. In this capacity he was involved in the reorganisation of the army into departmental legions and later into regiments. He wrote a number of reports on the training and discipline of infantry, including a memorandum of 1819 advocating the wider use of skirmishers and the simplification of drill manoeuvres, which was circulated within the War Ministry but not adopted.

He was placed on the reserve list in 1823 and retired fully in 1826. In retirement he devoted himself to the management of his estates, the improvement of local agriculture, and the writing of memoirs, which remained unfinished at his death.

= = = July Monarchy = = =

Following the July Revolution of 1830, Lavergne, who had maintained cordial relations with several liberal deputies, was named a peer of France by Louis Philippe I in 1832. He attended the Chamber of Peers infrequently and spoke on only two recorded occasions, once in favour of improved pensions for retired soldiers and once on a bill concerning the regulation of rural roads.

He died at the Château de Saint-Aulaye on 22 November 1834, at the age of 66, after a long illness. He was buried in the parish cemetery at Ribérac.

= = Personal life = =

Lavergne's marriage to Hortense-Adélaïde Rouffignac was by most accounts a stable one. His surviving letters to her, more than 400 of which are held in the departmental archives of the Dordogne, are a significant source for the history of the campaigns of 1805–1813. They provide detailed descriptions of camp life, the condition of the troops, and his opinions of fellow officers, often expressed with considerable candour.

His elder son, Auguste-Pierre Lavergne (1806–1871), served as an officer in the French army in Algeria and later as a prefect under the Second Empire. His daughter, Clémence (1809–1887), married a magistrate of the court of Bordeaux. A younger son died in infancy in 1811.

Lavergne was known among his contemporaries for his interest in architecture and drawing. He produced a number of sketches of fortifications and towns during his campaigns, some of which survive. He was also a collector of books on military history and agriculture, and his library of some 3,000 volumes was donated by his son to the municipal library of Périgueux in 1858.

= = Assessment = =

Lavergne is generally regarded by historians as a competent and reliable divisional commander rather than an outstanding one. His defence of Kreuzbach is frequently cited as an example of effective use of urban terrain in the Napoleonic period, and it has been studied in French military schools. His administrative record in Illyria has received increased attention since the late 20th century, particularly from Slovenian and Italian historians interested in the French period in the region.

His reluctance to rejoin Napoleon in 1815 has coloured some assessments of his character. Older Bonapartist accounts tended to portray him as opportunistic, while later historians have pointed to evidence that he was genuinely in poor health at the time and that his loyalties had been strained by his treatment after 1812.

= = Honours and legacy = =

Lavergne received the following honours during his lifetime:

Member of the Legion of Honour (1804)
Commander of the Legion of Honour (1805)
Grand Officer of the Legion of Honour (1809)
Baron of the Empire (1808)
Comte de Saint-Aulaye (1810)
Knight of the Order of Saint Louis (1814)

His name, written as LAVERGNE, is inscribed on the east pillar of the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. A street in Ribérac, the Rue du Général-Lavergne, was named in his honour in 1880, and a bronze bust by the sculptor Théodore Mallet was erected in the town's main square in 1891. The bust was removed and melted down during the German occupation in 1942 and was replaced with a stone copy in 1956.

The Château de Saint-Aulaye remained in the possession of his descendants until 1919, when it was sold. It is now privately owned and is not open to the public.
