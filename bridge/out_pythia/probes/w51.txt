= Clan MacIlvarran =

Clan MacIlvarran (Scottish Gaelic: Clann Mhic Giolla Bhearain) is a Highland Scottish clan historically associated with Glen Torrach and the northern shore of Loch Sruthain in what is now the western part of the council area of Highland. The clan was never among the most powerful in the Highlands, but it held a compact territory for nearly four centuries and appears frequently in the records of the Lordship of the Isles, the Privy Council of Scotland, and the Jacobite risings. The clan does not currently have a chief recognised by the Lord Lyon King of Arms and is therefore classed as an armigerous clan. A clan society, the MacIlvarran Association, was founded in Glasgow in 1898 and remains active.

= = Etymology = =

The name MacIlvarran is an anglicised form of the Gaelic Mac Giolla Bhearain, meaning "son of the servant of Bearan". Bearan is generally taken to be a hypocoristic form of the name of an early Christian missionary venerated locally, sometimes identified with the Saint Berchan commemorated in several Highland place names, although this identification is disputed. The earliest written forms of the surname, found in fourteenth-century charters, include *M'Gillebaran*, *Makilwarane* and *McKilberane*. The spelling MacIlvarran became standard in the late eighteenth century, though variants such as McIlvarren, Kilvarran and Barran persist among descendants, particularly in Ulster and North America.

= = History = =

= = = Origins = = =

Clan tradition, recorded in a genealogy compiled by the seanchaidh Ewen MacIlvarran in about 1690, traces the family to a hereditary keeper of a small chapel at Kilbearan on the shore of Loch Sruthain. According to this account, the keepers held lands from the church in exchange for maintaining the chapel and its relic, a bronze handbell known as the *Clag Bhearain*. Historians have treated the genealogy cautiously, noting that its early generations closely follow a pattern common to other clans claiming ecclesiastical descent.

The first figure attested in contemporary documents is Gillebarran mac Donnchaidh, who witnessed a grant of land by the Earl of Ross in 1342. A second charter, dated 1367, confirms "Gillebarran and his heirs" in possession of the davoch of Torrach, a unit of land roughly corresponding to the lower part of the glen. These records suggest that by the mid-fourteenth century the family had moved from purely church tenancy to holding lands as secular vassals.

= = = Under the Lordship of the Isles = = =

During the late fourteenth and fifteenth centuries the MacIlvarrans were followers of the Lords of the Isles, who held the earldom of Ross for part of this period. A MacIlvarran named Donald Ban is listed among the men of the Lord of the Isles at the Battle of Harlaw in 1411, though his role there is unknown. The clan's chiefs appear to have acted as minor tacksmen and occasional bailies for the Lordship, collecting rents in Glen Torrach and the neighbouring district of Ardmeanach.

The clan's principal seat in this period was Caisteal na Sròine, a small tower built on a rocky promontory on Loch Sruthain. Archaeological survey carried out in 1974 found that the surviving masonry dates largely from the early sixteenth century, but identified the footings of an earlier enclosure, possibly a hall house, beneath the later tower.

The forfeiture of the Lordship of the Isles in 1493 left the MacIlvarrans without a powerful patron. In the following decades they were drawn into the competition between larger neighbours, notably the Mackenzies to the east and the MacDonalds of Glengarry to the south.

= = = Sixteenth century = = =

The sixteenth century was the most turbulent period in the clan's documented history. In 1527 the chief, Ewen MacIlvarran of Torrach, was summoned before the Privy Council in connection with a raid on cattle belonging to a Mackenzie tenant. He failed to appear and was declared a rebel, though he was pardoned in 1530 on payment of a fine of three hundred merks.

The most frequently cited episode of this period is the so-called Burning of Kilbearan in 1544. According to later accounts, a party of men from a rival kindred set fire to the chapel at Kilbearan while a number of MacIlvarrans were sheltering inside following a dispute over grazing rights. The number of dead is given as anywhere from eleven to forty in different sources. No contemporary record of the event survives, and some historians have suggested that the story conflates several smaller incidents. The ruins of the chapel, which show evidence of fire damage, were nonetheless associated with the event by the eighteenth century.

In 1563 Alasdair Ruadh MacIlvarran, chief of the clan, entered into a bond of manrent with Colin Mackenzie of Kintail, undertaking to serve him "in all his lawful affairs" in return for protection. The bond, which survives in the Mackenzie papers, marks the beginning of a long period in which the MacIlvarrans operated largely within the Mackenzie sphere of influence while retaining their own lands and identity.

The clan adopted Protestantism gradually in the decades after the Scottish Reformation. A minister was installed at the parish of Kilbearan by 1581, although reports from the presbytery complained into the 1620s of continuing pilgrimages to the old chapel and of the veneration of the handbell.

= = = Seventeenth century = = =

In the early seventeenth century the clan's fortunes improved modestly. Under John MacIlvarran, 6th of Torrach (chief c. 1601–1648), the family obtained a crown charter for their lands in 1611, converting their tenure from a dependent holding into direct possession. John MacIlvarran is also recorded as having enlarged Caisteal na Sròine and built a new house, later known as Torrach House, in the more sheltered upper glen.

During the Wars of the Three Kingdoms the MacIlvarrans divided their loyalties. The chief's eldest son, Kenneth, served with the Royalist forces under the Marquess of Montrose and was present at the Battle of Auldearn in 1645. A younger branch, the MacIlvarrans of Achnafern, supported the Covenanters, and a member of this branch sat on the local committee of war in 1646. Tradition holds that the division was a deliberate strategy to protect the family's lands whichever side prevailed, a pattern seen among a number of Highland families during the period.

After the Restoration of 1660 Kenneth MacIlvarran, now chief, was granted a small pension in recognition of his Royalist service. He was among the Highland gentry who signed bonds for the good behaviour of their tenants following the government's efforts to pacify the Highlands in the 1680s.

= = = Jacobite risings = = =

The clan's involvement in the Jacobite risings was limited but well documented. In 1689 a company of about sixty men from Glen Torrach joined the forces of Viscount Dundee, though they are not recorded as having fought at Killiecrankie, and most appear to have returned home before the campaign ended.

During the rising of 1715, Roderick MacIlvarran, 9th of Torrach, raised around one hundred and twenty men and marched with the Earl of Seaforth's contingent. The MacIlvarrans were present at the Battle of Sheriffmuir, where several men from the clan were killed. Roderick was attainted after the collapse of the rising, but the estate was saved from forfeiture through a legal arrangement in which it had previously been conveyed to his brother-in-law, a Lowland lawyer named Robert Cairncross. The estate was formally restored to the family in 1724.

The clan's most significant involvement came during the rising of 1745. The chief at the time, Alexander MacIlvarran, 10th of Torrach, was elderly and did not take the field, but his son, Captain Hector MacIlvarran, led a company of about ninety men that served with the Jacobite army from the autumn of 1745. The company fought at the Battle of Falkirk Muir in January 1746 and at the Battle of Culloden in April 1746, where it was attached to a larger Highland regiment on the Jacobite right. Hector MacIlvarran was wounded at Culloden and escaped to France, where he served in the French army and died at Douai in 1759.

In the aftermath of Culloden, government troops occupied Glen Torrach in May 1746. Caisteal na Sròine was partly demolished and Torrach House was burned. Several tenants were taken prisoner, and at least four were transported to the American colonies. The estate was forfeited to the Crown and administered by the Commissioners for the Annexed Estates until 1784, when it was restored to Hector's nephew, Alexander MacIlvarran, under the Disannexing Act.

= = = Clearances and emigration = = =

The restored estate was heavily indebted. Alexander MacIlvarran, 12th of Torrach, and his successors attempted to increase revenue through the introduction of sheep farming. Between 1808 and 1826 much of upper Glen Torrach was let to sheep farmers from the south, and a large part of the tenant population was removed to coastal crofts at Inverbearan or left the district altogether. Contemporary estimates suggest the population of the glen fell from about 640 in 1801 to fewer than 200 by 1841.

Many emigrants from Glen Torrach settled in Nova Scotia, particularly in Pictou County, and in the Upper Canada settlements of Glengarry County. A smaller group sailed to North Carolina in 1803 aboard the brig *Hope of Leith*. Descendants in Canada formed the first overseas MacIlvarran society in 1872.

The estate itself passed out of the family in 1849, when it was sold by trustees acting for the creditors of Donald MacIlvarran, 14th of Torrach, to a Manchester cotton merchant, Josiah Hartley. Donald MacIlvarran emigrated to Australia in 1852 and died in Victoria in 1867. The direct male line of the chiefs is believed to have ended with his son, who died unmarried in 1891.

= = = Twentieth century to present = = =

Interest in the clan revived in the late nineteenth century as part of the broader growth of clan societies. The MacIlvarran Association was established in 1898, and its members funded the restoration of the chapel ruins at Kilbearan in 1911. A memorial cairn was erected near the site of Caisteal na Sròine in 1946 to mark the bicentenary of Culloden.

Several claims to the chiefship have been made since the 1950s, most notably by descendants of the Achnafern branch living in Ontario. In 1988 the Lord Lyon declined to recognise a claimant on the grounds that insufficient evidence had been presented of descent from the last recognised chief. A family convention was held at Inverbearan in 2004 but did not result in the appointment of a commander, and the clan remains without a recognised head.

= = Territory = =

The clan's heartland was Glen Torrach, a narrow valley running roughly north-east for about eleven miles from the shore of Loch Sruthain. The lower glen contained most of the arable land and the principal townships, including Kilbearan, Achnafern, Balnacoil and Torrach itself. The upper glen consisted largely of summer pasture, with shielings recorded at more than twenty sites.

At its greatest extent in the early seventeenth century the clan's lands also included the island of Eilean Dubh in Loch Sruthain and a detached holding at Ardmeanach, held from the Mackenzies of Kintail. The Ardmeanach lands were lost in a dispute over feu duties in 1679.

= = Castles and buildings = =

* **Caisteal na Sròine** – the clan's medieval and early modern seat, a small rectangular tower of three storeys standing on a promontory on Loch Sruthain. Partly demolished in 1746, the surviving remains consist of the ground-floor vault and parts of the south and east walls. The site is a scheduled monument.
* **Torrach House** – a laird's house built around 1620, burned in 1746 and rebuilt on a larger scale in 1789. It was remodelled in the Scottish baronial style by the Hartley family in the 1860s and is now in private ownership.
* **Kilbearan Chapel** – the ruins of a small medieval chapel associated with the clan's traditional origins. The roofless structure measures roughly nine by five metres and contains several carved grave slabs of the West Highland type, two of which bear swords and foliage patterns attributed to the Loch Sween school of carving.

= = Clan symbols = =

= = = Crest and motto = = =

The crest badge used by members of the clan consists of a hand holding a bell, within a strap and buckle bearing the motto *Fuaim gun eagal*, Scottish Gaelic for "Sound without fear". The bell is generally understood as a reference to the *Clag Bhearain*. An earlier motto, *Seas gu daingeann* ("Stand firm"), appears on a carved panel from Torrach House dated 1622 and was used by the family until the eighteenth century.

The arms recorded for MacIlvarran of Torrach in the Public Register of All Arms and Bearings in 1673 were described as *Azure, a bell Or between three salmon naiant argent*. The salmon are thought to refer to the fishing rights on Loch Sruthain, which formed a significant part of the estate's income.

= = = Tartan = = =

No tartan is reliably associated with the clan before the nineteenth century. The MacIlvarran tartan in current use was first published in 1842 in a collection of clan tartans of doubtful provenance. It consists of a dark green and blue ground with a red overcheck and a narrow yellow line. A hunting variant with a brown ground was registered by the clan association in 1967.

= = = Plant badge = = =

The traditional plant badge of the clan is bog myrtle (*Myrica gale*), which grows abundantly in the wetter parts of Glen Torrach.

= = = War cry = = =

The clan's war cry is recorded as *Creag an Fhithich!* ("The Raven's Rock!"), the name of a crag overlooking the lower glen that was said to have served as a gathering place.

= = The Clag Bhearain = =

The *Clag Bhearain* is a quadrangular iron handbell coated in bronze, approximately twenty-three centimetres high, traditionally held by the clan as a relic of its founding saint. Bells of this type are generally dated to between the eighth and tenth centuries. Until the early eighteenth century the bell was kept by a hereditary keeper at Kilbearan and was used in the swearing of oaths and the settlement of disputes. It was reportedly carried into battle at Sheriffmuir in 1715.

The bell was removed from the glen in 1746 and passed through several private hands before being acquired by the Society of Antiquaries of Scotland in 1868. It is now held by National Museums Scotland in Edinburgh. The clan association has periodically requested its loan for display in the district, and it was exhibited at Inverbearan for a short period in 1996.

= = Septs = =

The following surnames have been considered septs of the clan, either because they are variants of the name or because their bearers were historically associated with MacIlvarran lands:

* Barran
* Bearan
* Kilvarran
* McGilvarran
* McIlvarren
* Torrach
* Ferns (from the Achnafern branch)

As with many Highland clans, the lists of septs promoted in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries were often compiled with little historical evidence, and the inclusion of particular surnames does not necessarily reflect a genealogical connection.

= = Notable members = =

* **Ewen MacIlvarran** (fl. 1680–1705), seanchaidh and compiler of the clan genealogy, also known for a collection of Gaelic verse preserved in a manuscript now held by the University of Edinburgh.
* **Captain Hector MacIlvarran** (c. 1712–1759), Jacobite officer at Falkirk and Culloden, later in French service.
* **Mairi Nighean Alasdair MacIlvarran** (c. 1790–1862), Gaelic poet whose songs of emigration from Glen Torrach were collected in Nova Scotia in the 1850s.
* **Reverend Duncan MacIlvarran** (1821–1894), Free Church minister and campaigner on crofting reform, who gave evidence to the Napier Commission in 1883.
* **Catherine Barran MacIlvarran** (1903–1981), Canadian historian and founding president of the Ontario branch of the clan association.
