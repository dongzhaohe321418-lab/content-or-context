= Battle of Hollenstein =

The Battle of Hollenstein was fought on 14 May 1809 near the market town of Hollenstein an der Ybbs, in what is now Lower Austria, during the War of the Fifth Coalition. An Austrian corps of about 14,000 men under Feldmarschall-Leutnant Ignaz von Trautenberg tried to hold the upper Ybbs valley against a Franco-Bavarian force of about 17,500 under Général de Division Louis-Antoine Dessalle. After a day of fighting in difficult wooded terrain, the Austrians withdrew southeast toward Lunz and the Ötscher passes. Neither side was destroyed. Both lost heavily, with roughly 2,900 Austrian and 2,300 Franco-Bavarian casualties.

The battle was a secondary action in the campaign that ended at Aspern-Essling and Wagram. Historians have nonetheless studied it closely, for three reasons. It is an early example of a combined French and Rhine Confederation force fighting under mixed command. It shows the limits of Austrian reforms to light infantry tactics. And it delayed the junction of Dessalle's column with the main French army by several days. Some historians have argued that this delay affected the French order of battle at Aspern-Essling on 21–22 May, but the claim remains disputed.

= = Background = =

In April 1809 Austria went to war against the French Empire. Archduke Charles's main army invaded Bavaria and was defeated in the actions around Eckmühl and Regensburg between 19 and 23 April. Charles retreated north of the Danube into Bohemia. The southern Austrian wing, under Feldmarschall-Leutnant Johann von Hiller, fell back along the right bank of the river toward Vienna, fighting rearguard actions at Ebelsberg on 3 May and elsewhere. Napoleon advanced directly on the Austrian capital and occupied it on 13 May.

During this retreat several Austrian detachments were separated from Hiller's main body. They withdrew into the Alpine foothills south of the Danube. The largest was a mixed force under Trautenberg, formed largely from troops that had been guarding the line of the Enns and from Landwehr battalions raised in Upper and Lower Austria that spring. Trautenberg had orders from Hiller's headquarters to protect the routes between the Danube valley and Styria. He was also to keep open a possible line of communication with Archduke John's Army of Inner Austria, which was then retreating from Italy.

The French command saw these Alpine detachments as a threat to the long line of communication along the Danube. On 9 May Marshal Lannes ordered Dessalle to clear the Ybbs and Erlauf valleys. Dessalle's division was attached to Lannes's corps and included two Bavarian brigades from the VII Corps. Dessalle was then to rejoin the main army near Vienna. Dessalle was a veteran of the Italian campaigns of 1796–1797 and of Hohenlinden. He had a reputation as a methodical rather than a dashing commander.

= = Opposing forces = =

= = = Austrian = = =

Trautenberg's corps on the morning of 14 May had about 14,000 men and 22 guns. Its regular infantry came from the Infantry Regiments No. 14 (Klebek) and No. 49 (Kerpen), together with a battalion of the 7th Jäger and two companies of Grenz infantry from the Ogulin regiment. These regular troops numbered about 8,200. The remaining infantry, about 4,800 men, came from six Landwehr battalions, most of which had been embodied for less than two months. Many of these men were armed with older muskets, and several companies had no regular officers.

The cavalry was small, as the terrain was unsuited to mounted action. It consisted of four squadrons of the Chevaulegers Regiment No. 3 (O'Reilly), about 600 sabres. The artillery included two brigade batteries of six-pounders, a position battery of twelve-pounders, and a small number of three-pounder mountain guns that had been brought up from the arsenal at Steyr.

Trautenberg's chief of staff was Oberstleutnant Franz Xaver Mollenbeck, a graduate of the Austrian staff school. His surviving notes are the main Austrian source for the battle.

= = = Franco-Bavarian = = =

Dessalle's column numbered about 17,500 men and 28 guns. Its French component was a division of two brigades. The first, under Général de Brigade Jean-Pierre Courvoisier, included the 10th Light Infantry and the 57th Line. The second, under Général de Brigade Étienne Malroux, included the 88th and 103rd Line. In total there were about 9,600 French infantry. The cavalry was the 13th Chasseurs à Cheval, with about 450 men.

The Bavarian component came from the division of Generalleutnant Bernhard von Erlach-Rhein. It was organised into two brigades under Generalmajor Karl von Stengel and Generalmajor Joseph von Hardegg-Lorch. These brigades comprised three line regiments and two light battalions, about 6,900 men, together with a squadron of chevaulegers. The Bavarian light battalions, especially the 5th Light Battalion (Buttler), were considered among the best skirmishing troops in the column.

Command relations within the column were not clearly defined. Dessalle held overall command by Lannes's order. Erlach-Rhein, however, had received instructions from the Bavarian headquarters to keep his division together and to avoid its being broken up among French brigades. This disagreement shaped the conduct of the battle.

= = Terrain = =

Hollenstein lies in a narrow section of the Ybbs valley, roughly where the river turns from a north-south to a northwest course. Wooded ridges rising 300 to 500 metres above the valley floor flank the town to the east and west. In 1809 a single road ran up the valley, broadly following the left bank of the river. Smaller tracks led over the Königsberg ridge to the east toward Göstling and Lunz.

Trautenberg chose a position just north of the town, where the valley opens into a small basin about 1.5 kilometres wide. The basin was crossed by the Hollensteiner Bach, a tributary stream with steep banks. His centre held the stream line and a group of farm buildings called the Pöchlhof. His right extended up the lower slopes of the Königsberg to a wooded spur known locally as the Galgenleiten. His left rested on the Ybbs, with outposts on the far bank near a sawmill and a wooden bridge.

Contemporaries described the position as strong in front but vulnerable on the flanks. Both ridgelines could be climbed by light infantry, although with difficulty. The woods made it hard for either commander to see more than a fraction of the field.

= = Prelude = =

Dessalle's column left Waidhofen an der Ybbs on 12 May and advanced up the valley against light opposition. On 13 May the advance guard, made up of the Bavarian 5th Light Battalion and a squadron of chasseurs, skirmished with Austrian outposts at Opponitz and drove them back. That evening French patrols reported that the Austrians had halted and were entrenching north of Hollenstein.

Dessalle held a council of war at Opponitz that night. According to the memoirs of his aide-de-camp, Capitaine Henri Lavergne, Dessalle proposed a frontal attack in the valley, supported by a single flanking column on the eastern ridge. Erlach-Rhein objected and argued for a wider turning movement over the western heights by the whole Bavarian division. A compromise was reached. The Bavarian brigade of Stengel would climb the western ridge. Courvoisier's French brigade would attempt the eastern slopes toward the Galgenleiten. Malroux's brigade and Hardegg-Lorch's Bavarians would make the main attack up the valley road once the flanking columns were in position.

Heavy rain fell overnight. The Ybbs rose, and the tracks on both ridges became slick. Stengel's brigade set out at about 3:00 a.m. and Courvoisier's an hour later.

= = Battle = =

= = = Morning: the flanking movements = = =

Courvoisier's brigade reached the lower slopes of the Königsberg at about 6:30 a.m. There it met the Austrian Jäger battalion and two Landwehr battalions posted in the woods below the Galgenleiten. The fighting in the forest was confused and lasted most of the morning. The 10th Light Infantry pushed the Landwehr back from a line of felled trees. An Austrian counterattack by a battalion of the Klebek regiment then retook the position at around 8:00 a.m.

The French 57th Line came up in support, and the struggle for the spur continued. By about 10:30 a.m. Courvoisier held the lower half of the Galgenleiten, but the Jäger kept the crest. Losses on both sides were heavy, particularly among officers. Courvoisier himself was wounded in the arm but stayed with his brigade.

Stengel's brigade on the western ridge made slower progress. The route chosen by its guides turned out to be longer than expected, and rain had washed out sections of the track. The brigade did not reach a position overlooking the Austrian left until about 10:00 a.m. Even then, its artillery had been left behind, apart from two light guns that had been manhandled up the slope. Mollenbeck had anticipated the movement. He had placed two companies of the Ogulin Grenzers and a Landwehr battalion along the wooded ridge west of the Ybbs, and these delayed Stengel's advance for another two hours.

= = = Midday: the main assault = = =

Dessalle had planned to launch the main attack once he heard gunfire from both flanks. Smoke and the noise of the river made it difficult to judge the progress of either column. At about 11:00 a.m., under pressure from Malroux, he ordered the valley attack to begin before Stengel's position was confirmed.

Malroux's brigade advanced in two columns on either side of the road, with the 88th Line leading. The French met heavy fire from the Austrian position batteries on the slope behind the Pöchlhof and from infantry lining the Hollensteiner Bach. The first assault reached the stream but could not cross it. The 88th fell back with heavy losses, including its colonel, Jean-Baptiste Arnoult, who was killed near the stream bank.

A second assault at about 12:30 p.m. included the 103rd Line and two of Hardegg-Lorch's Bavarian battalions. It succeeded in crossing the stream on the Austrian centre-left and capturing the outbuildings of the Pöchlhof. Trautenberg counterattacked with the Kerpen regiment, supported by two squadrons of the O'Reilly Chevaulegers. The cavalry charge caught a Bavarian battalion in the open meadows near the river and drove it back in disorder. The Austrians took about 200 prisoners and a regimental colour. The colour, belonging to the 2nd Battalion of the Bavarian 11th Line Infantry Regiment, was later displayed at the Arsenal in Vienna. It was returned to Bavaria in 1815.

By 1:30 p.m. the front in the valley had largely stabilised. The French and Bavarians held a narrow strip on the far side of the stream, while the Austrians still held the main farmstead and the slopes behind it.

= = = Afternoon: collapse of the Austrian left = = =

The turning point came on the western flank. At about 2:00 p.m. Stengel's brigade, reinforced by the 5th Light Battalion, finally pushed the Grenzers and the Landwehr off the western ridge. Stengel brought his two light guns into action on a clearing above the sawmill. From there they could fire across the Ybbs into the flank and rear of the Austrian position.

The appearance of Bavarian troops on the heights above the river had a strong effect on the Austrian left. That wing was made up largely of inexperienced Landwehr. Mollenbeck later wrote that two Landwehr battalions "dissolved as if by a word" when they came under fire from across the river. The Austrian outposts at the wooden bridge were driven in. Bavarian skirmishers began to cross the river on a ford a short distance upstream.

Trautenberg now risked being cut off from the road south through Hollenstein, his only practicable line of retreat. At about 3:00 p.m. he ordered a general withdrawal. The withdrawal was covered by the Jäger on the Galgenleiten and by the Klebek regiment, which held the Pöchlhof until about 4:30 p.m. The O'Reilly Chevaulegers made two further charges in the valley to delay the Franco-Bavarian advance. In the second of these, Major Anton Freiherr von Lessing was killed leading the regiment's first squadron.

The Austrians fell back through Hollenstein. They abandoned four guns whose teams had been killed or lamed and set fire to the bridge over the Ybbs at the southern edge of the town. Fires spread to several houses near the bridge. By evening the town was in Franco-Bavarian hands, and the Austrian main body was retreating toward Göstling.

= = = Pursuit = = =

The Franco-Bavarian pursuit was limited. Dessalle's troops were exhausted after more than twelve hours of marching and fighting in difficult terrain. The single squadron of chasseurs was too weak to press the retreating enemy. Erlach-Rhein proposed to send the Bavarian light battalions after the Austrians at first light on 15 May. Dessalle refused and cited Lannes's standing orders to rejoin the main army without delay. The decision was criticised in Bavarian accounts after the war. They argued that a vigorous pursuit could have destroyed Trautenberg's corps as a fighting force.

= = Casualties = =

The figures for losses differ between sources. Mollenbeck's notes give Austrian losses as 412 killed, 1,236 wounded, and 1,280 missing or captured, a total of 2,928. Many of the missing were Landwehr who later returned to their units or went home, and the French claimed only about 900 prisoners. The Austrians also lost four guns.

French returns compiled at Waidhofen on 17 May list 318 killed and 1,104 wounded in the French brigades. The 88th Line suffered the heaviest losses, about 470 of all ranks. Bavarian losses, according to the regimental histories published later in the nineteenth century, were 196 killed, 541 wounded, and 214 captured, giving a combined Franco-Bavarian total of roughly 2,370. Several historians consider the French figures too low, especially for the wounded in Courvoisier's brigade.

The civilian population of Hollenstein also suffered. A parish register records that eleven houses burned and that six inhabitants were killed during the fighting and its aftermath.

= = Aftermath = =

Trautenberg's corps retreated through Lunz and over the passes toward Mariazell. It reached Styria in late May with about 9,500 men, having lost many more stragglers during the march. It later joined Archduke John's army. Parts of it fought at the Battle of Raab on 14 June 1809.

Dessalle's column remained in the Ybbs valley until 16 May to collect its wounded and rest. It then marched north to the Danube and rejoined Lannes's corps near Vienna on 20 May. Because of its losses and the late arrival, Lannes assigned the column to the reserve rather than to the troops that first crossed to the Lobau island. At Aspern-Essling the French brigades of the division saw limited action on 22 May. The Bavarian brigades had meanwhile been ordered back to the VII Corps and took no part in the battle.

Trautenberg was criticised in Vienna for abandoning the position. A commission of inquiry in 1810 cleared him, finding that he had been outflanked by a superior force and had saved the bulk of his corps. He retired from active service in 1811. Dessalle was made a Baron of the Empire in 1810. He later served in Spain, where he died of fever at Talavera in 1812.

= = Assessment = =

Military historians have drawn different conclusions from the battle. The Austrian official history of the 1809 campaign, published in 1907, presented Hollenstein as a successful delaying action. It emphasised the steadiness of the regular regiments and the effective use of cavalry in a confined valley. The same work was openly critical of the Landwehr. Its authors argued that the collapse of the left wing showed the dangers of placing hastily raised militia in positions where their nerve would be tested.

French writers paid little attention to the battle. Treatments by Bavarian historians, particularly the work of Oberst Ludwig von Hettinger in the 1880s, were more detailed. They praised the flanking march of Stengel's brigade and blamed Dessalle for both the premature valley attack and the failure to pursue. Later scholarship has argued that the divided command was the root of both problems. Neither Dessalle nor Erlach-Rhein had clear authority over the other's troops, and the plan agreed at Opponitz was a compromise that neither commander fully supported.

The claim that the delay at Hollenstein influenced Aspern-Essling has been made by several authors but is generally considered overstated. The column's French brigades would probably have been placed in reserve in any case. The Bavarian brigades were already under orders to return to their parent corps.

= = Legacy = =

A memorial cross was erected at the Pöchlhof in 1859, on the fiftieth anniversary of the battle. It bears the names of Austrian officers killed in the fighting. A second monument, a stone obelisk commemorating the Bavarian dead, was set up on the western ridge in 1909 by veterans' associations from Upper Bavaria. Both monuments survive, and a marked footpath connects them.

The local museum in Hollenstein an der Ybbs holds a small collection of items recovered from the battlefield. These include musket balls, buttons from the uniforms of the Klebek regiment, and a damaged three-pounder barrel thought to be one of the guns abandoned in the retreat. Re-enactment groups have staged commemorations at the site at irregular intervals since the bicentenary in 2009.
