Kestrel Lake is a freshwater lake in the Thompson-Okanagan region of south-central British Columbia, Canada. It lies about 38 kilometres (24 mi) northeast of the city of Kamloops, on the Shuswap Highland plateau, at an elevation of 1,142 metres (3,747 ft). The lake covers 17.6 square kilometres (6.8 sq mi) and has a maximum depth of 64 metres (210 ft). It is the largest of a chain of glacially formed lakes drained by Kestrel Creek, a tributary of the North Thompson River.

The lake lies within the traditional territory of the Secwépemc (Shuswap) people, who know it as Tsk'elúl'ecw, a name usually translated as "place of the diving birds." Europeans first recorded it in the 1860s. Its later history includes placer mining, commercial logging, a short-lived fish hatchery and, since the mid-twentieth century, recreational fishing and cottage development. Kestrel Lake Provincial Park, established in 1974, protects much of the northern shoreline. The lake is known regionally for its population of wild rainbow trout and for a breeding colony of common loons, one of the largest in the southern interior of the province.

= = Geography = =

Kestrel Lake is elongated along a northwest–southeast axis. It measures about 11.4 kilometres (7.1 mi) long and 2.3 kilometres (1.4 mi) across at its widest point, near the centre of the lake. The shoreline totals about 34 kilometres (21 mi), including the perimeters of its seven named islands. The largest, Pellerin Island, covers about 41 hectares (100 acres) and lies in the southern basin. It is wooded and, apart from a single wilderness campsite, undeveloped.

Rolling forested hills surround the lake. They rise between 150 and 300 metres (490 and 980 ft) above the water. The highest nearby point is Mount Darrow (1,587 m), about 5 kilometres (3.1 mi) east of the lake's eastern shore. The western shore is generally gentle and has several gravel beaches. The eastern shore is steeper, with exposed granite bluffs along parts of its length. A narrow, shallow channel called the Narrows divides the lake into a deeper northern basin and a shallower southern basin. Its minimum width is about 90 metres (300 ft), and it is crossed by a single-lane timber bridge built in 1958 and rebuilt in 2003.

The lake's watershed covers about 214 square kilometres (83 sq mi). The main inflows are Darrow Creek, which enters at the northeast corner, and Lisle Creek, which drains a series of smaller lakes and wetlands to the north. A number of smaller seasonal streams also feed the lake. The single outflow, Kestrel Creek, leaves the southern end of the lake and flows about 27 kilometres (17 mi) southwest to join the North Thompson River near the community of Heffley Creek. Average discharge at the outlet is about 2.1 cubic metres per second (74 cu ft/s). Flow peaks sharply during the spring freshet in late May and early June.

Hydrologists estimate the lake's water residence time at about 6.5 years. This relatively long period is attributed to the lake's large volume compared with the modest size of its catchment.

= = Geology = =

The basin occupied by Kestrel Lake was shaped mainly by glaciation during the Pleistocene epoch. Bedrock in the area belongs largely to the Baldy Batholith, a body of Cretaceous granodiorite. Along the southern shore it gives way to metamorphosed sedimentary rocks of the Eagle Bay assemblage. The northern basin follows a pre-existing fault zone that ice later deepened and widened during the Fraser Glaciation, the last major glacial advance in the region.

When the Cordilleran Ice Sheet retreated about 11,000 years ago, it left extensive deposits of glacial till and outwash across the plateau. A recessional moraine at the southern end of the lake forms a natural dam about 18 metres (59 ft) high. This moraine is responsible for much of the lake's present depth in the southern basin. Kestrel Creek has cut a shallow channel through it, and the remains of an old timber weir can still be seen near the outlet.

Sediment cores taken from the deepest part of the northern basin in 1997 and 2011 have yielded a continuous record of deposition going back about 10,800 years. A layer of volcanic ash about 4 centimetres (1.6 in) thick in these cores has been correlated with the eruption of Mount Mazama in present-day Oregon, about 7,600 years ago. It provides a useful dating marker for regional palaeoenvironmental studies. Pollen preserved in the cores records a shift from open, pine-dominated parkland in the early Holocene to the mixed forest of Douglas fir and spruce that characterizes the area today.

= = Climate = =

Kestrel Lake has a humid continental climate (Köppen Dfb). Winters are cold and summers are warm. The nearest long-term weather station, at Darrow Lookout, recorded a mean January temperature of −9.4 °C (15.1 °F) and a mean July temperature of 15.8 °C (60.4 °F) for the period 1991–2020. Annual precipitation averages about 540 millimetres (21 in), of which roughly 40 percent falls as snow.

The lake usually freezes over between late November and mid-December. It thaws between late April and early May, giving an average ice-covered season of about 150 days. Records kept by local residents and park staff since 1951 show a gradual shortening of the ice season. The average date of ice-off has moved about nine days earlier over that period. The earliest recorded ice-off was on 11 April 2016. The latest was on 19 May 1955.

The lake is dimictic: its waters mix completely twice a year, in spring and autumn. During summer the northern basin develops a well-defined thermocline at depths of about 8 to 12 metres (26 to 39 ft). Surface water temperatures in late July and August commonly reach 19 to 22 °C (66 to 72 °F).

= = History = =

= = = Indigenous history = = =

The Secwépemc have used the lands around Kestrel Lake for thousands of years. Archaeological surveys carried out between 1979 and 1984 identified at least 23 sites around the shoreline. They include lithic scatters, cache pits, and the remains of three pit-house villages along the western shore. Radiocarbon dates from the largest village site, near the mouth of Lisle Creek, range from about 2,900 to 400 years before present.

Oral histories describe the lake as an important late-summer and autumn fishing location. Trout and suckers were caught with weirs and dip nets, then dried on racks along the shore. The surrounding uplands were used for hunting deer and for gathering plant foods, including saskatoon berries, black huckleberries, and the roots of the avalanche lily. Several pictograph panels painted in red ochre are found on the granite bluffs of the eastern shore. Their exact age is not known. They are considered culturally significant by the Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc and the Simpcw First Nation, and their precise locations are not published.

= = = European exploration and settlement = = =

The first European record of the lake is generally credited to the surveyor Alfred Penhallow. He passed through the area in 1862 while looking for a route between the North Thompson valley and the Cariboo goldfields. His field notes describe "a long lake of great clearness, much frequented by divers and other water-fowl." On the map that accompanied his report he named it Kestrel Lake, apparently after a small falcon he saw near the outlet. The name was formally adopted by the Geographic Board of Canada in 1911.

Prospectors working placer claims along Darrow Creek in the 1870s and 1880s recovered modest amounts of gold. No large deposits were found, and by 1890 most claims had been abandoned. A few prospectors stayed on as trappers and subsistence farmers. The first permanent homestead on the lake was established in 1896 by Jean-Baptiste Pellerin, a French-Canadian trapper. The largest island in the lake is named after him.

= = = Logging and industry = = =

Commercial logging began in the 1910s. In 1919 the Thompson Valley Lumber Company built a sawmill at the southern end of the lake. Logs were boomed across the lake and milled at the outlet, and the cut lumber was hauled out by wagon, and later by truck, along a road built to Heffley Creek. At its peak in the late 1920s the mill employed about 60 workers. A small company settlement known as Kestrel Landing grew up around it, with a school, a general store, and a post office that operated from 1923 to 1948.

The mill closed in 1932 during the Great Depression. It reopened under new ownership in 1937 and finally closed in 1951, after the most accessible stands of timber had been cut. Most of the buildings at Kestrel Landing were later dismantled or destroyed by fire. The former schoolhouse survives and is used as a community hall. Concrete foundations of the mill and submerged log debris remain visible near the outlet.

Between 1934 and 1941 the provincial Game Department ran a trout hatchery on Darrow Creek, about a kilometre upstream from the lake. Eggs collected from spawning trout in the creek were raised and distributed to lakes throughout the southern interior. The hatchery was closed during the Second World War and was never reopened.

= = = Recreation and conservation = = =

After the Second World War, improved roads made the lake accessible to visitors from Kamloops. Its reputation as a trout fishery grew. The first fishing lodge, Kestrel Lodge, opened on the western shore in 1949. A second, Darrow Bay Resort, followed in 1962. During the 1960s and 1970s the provincial government sold leases and freehold lots along the southern and western shores, and about 140 recreational cottages had been built by 1980.

Concern over shoreline development and declining water quality prompted local residents to form the Kestrel Lake Stewardship Society in 1971. The society lobbied for protection of the undeveloped northern shoreline. In 1974 the province established Kestrel Lake Provincial Park, which initially covered 820 hectares (2,000 acres). The park was enlarged in 1995 and again in 2008 and now covers 2,310 hectares (5,700 acres). It includes the full northern basin shoreline, the Lisle Creek wetlands, and the slopes of Mount Darrow.

In 2017 the Tk'emlúps te Secwépemc and BC Parks signed a collaborative management agreement for the park. It provides for joint planning, the protection of cultural sites, and the inclusion of Secwépemc place names on park signage.

= = Ecology = =

= = = Fish = = =

Kestrel Lake supports a native population of rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss). Genetic studies published in 2004 found them to be distinct from hatchery strains stocked elsewhere in the region. The population spawns mainly in Darrow Creek and Lisle Creek in late May and June. Other native species include longnose sucker, largescale sucker, redside shiner, peamouth chub, and prickly sculpin. Kokanee salmon were introduced in 1958 and have established a self-sustaining population. They are an important food source for larger trout.

Unauthorized introductions of yellow perch were first detected in 2009. The species was most likely introduced by anglers using live bait, which has been prohibited on the lake since 1976. Fisheries biologists are concerned that perch may compete with juvenile trout for food. A monitoring program run jointly by the province and the stewardship society has tracked their numbers since 2010. As of 2022 the perch population was reported to be stable but established throughout the southern basin.

The lake is managed under special angling regulations. Fishing is restricted to artificial flies and single barbless hooks, and the daily catch limit is one trout. Spawning tributaries are closed to fishing between 1 April and 15 July.

= = = Birds and wildlife = = =

The lake is particularly noted for its breeding common loons. Surveys by the Canadian Lakes Loon Survey have counted between 14 and 22 breeding pairs in most years since 1990. This makes Kestrel Lake one of the most important loon breeding sites in the southern interior of British Columbia. The islands and the sheltered bays of the northern basin provide most of the nesting habitat. Other breeding waterbirds include red-necked grebe, Barrow's goldeneye, common merganser, and bufflehead. Ospreys nest on snags along the eastern shore, and bald eagles are common throughout the year.

Mammals found in the watershed include moose, mule deer, black bear, beaver, river otter, and mink. Grizzly bears are seen occasionally in the northern part of the park. The Lisle Creek wetlands support a population of western toad, and the Columbia spotted frog has also been recorded there.

= = = Vegetation = = =

The forests around the lake belong mainly to the Interior Douglas-fir and Montane Spruce biogeoclimatic zones. Douglas fir, lodgepole pine, and trembling aspen dominate the lower slopes. Engelmann spruce and subalpine fir become more common at higher elevations. Large parts of the watershed were affected by the mountain pine beetle outbreak of the early 2000s, and extensive salvage logging was carried out outside the park boundaries between 2005 and 2012. A wildfire in August 2021 burned about 1,900 hectares (4,700 acres) on the eastern slopes of Mount Darrow, including part of the park. No structures were lost.

= = Water quality = =

Kestrel Lake is generally classed as oligotrophic to mesotrophic. Water clarity, measured by Secchi depth, has averaged 6.8 metres (22 ft) during summer since monitoring began in 1985. The southern basin, which is shallower and receives runoff from developed shorelines, tends to have lower clarity and higher nutrient levels than the northern basin.

In the late 1980s, rising phosphorus concentrations and late-summer algal blooms in the southern basin were linked to failing septic systems and shoreline clearing. In 1993 the Thompson-Nicola Regional District introduced bylaws requiring setbacks for new construction and regular inspection of septic systems. Phosphorus levels in the southern basin fell by about 30 percent over the following decade. A small blue-green algae bloom in Pellerin Bay in September 2018 led to a temporary advisory, which was lifted after three weeks.

= = Recreation = =

Kestrel Lake Provincial Park has two vehicle-accessible campgrounds, at Lisle Creek and North Beach, with a combined 86 sites. There are also five boat-access wilderness campsites, including the site on Pellerin Island. A day-use area at North Beach has a boat launch, picnic shelters, and a swimming area. Hiking trails include the 4.5-kilometre (2.8 mi) Bluffs Trail along the eastern shore and an 11-kilometre (6.8 mi) route to the summit of Mount Darrow, where a former fire lookout gives views over the lake and the North Thompson valley.

Motorized boats are permitted on the lake, but a speed limit of 10 kilometres per hour (6.2 mph) applies within 100 metres of the shore and throughout the Narrows. Canoeing, kayaking, and fly fishing are the most popular summer activities. In winter the lake is used for ice fishing, cross-country skiing, and snowshoeing. An annual ice-fishing derby has been held on the southern basin each February since 1968.

= = Communities = =

There is no incorporated municipality on the lake. The largest settlement is the unincorporated community of Kestrel Landing at the southern end. It had a permanent population of 112 at the 2021 census, which rises to an estimated 600 to 800 during summer. Services there include a general store, a volunteer fire department established in 1987, and the community hall in the former schoolhouse. Kestrel Lodge and Darrow Bay Resort continue to operate as commercial fishing resorts. The lake is reached by Kestrel Lake Road, a paved road that leaves Highway 5 at Heffley Creek. The last 6 kilometres (3.7 mi) to the provincial park are gravel.
