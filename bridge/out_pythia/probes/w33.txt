Castell Rhyd-y-Gors is a ruined medieval castle standing on a rocky spur above the confluence of the River Tywi and its tributary the Afon Gwili, approximately 3 kilometres (1.9 mi) north-east of the market town of Llandeilo Fach in Carmarthenshire, Wales. The castle was founded as an earth-and-timber fortification by Norman settlers in the early twelfth century and was rebuilt in stone during the thirteenth century. Over the following two hundred years it changed hands repeatedly between Welsh princes and Anglo-Norman lords. It was besieged during the revolt of Owain Glyndŵr in 1403 and was partially slighted after the English Civil War. The surviving remains include a polygonal inner ward, a cylindrical keep, a twin-towered gatehouse and substantial sections of curtain wall. The site is a scheduled monument and Grade I listed building, and is managed by the Welsh heritage body Cadw.

 = = Name = = 

The name Rhyd-y-Gors is usually interpreted as "ford of the marsh" (Welsh *rhyd*, "ford", and *cors*, "bog" or "marsh", mutated to *gors*). It probably refers to a crossing point on the Gwili below the castle, where low-lying wetland lay until it was drained in the nineteenth century. Medieval documents record the name in several forms, including *Rudegors* (1137), *Ridegores* (1210) and *Castrum de Rethegors* (1283). In English sources of the later Middle Ages the castle is sometimes called **Gorse Castle**, a folk rendering that has no connection with the gorse plant. Some antiquarian writers of the eighteenth century connected the name with a supposed battle at the ford, but there is no documentary evidence for this.

 = = Geography and setting = = 

The castle occupies the southern end of a ridge of Ordovician mudstone and sandstone, which rises about 45 metres (148 ft) above the floodplain of the Tywi. Steep slopes protect the site on its western, southern and eastern sides. The only level approach is from the north, along the spine of the ridge. This position gave the castle command over the Tywi valley, which was one of the main routes between the coastal lowlands of south-west Wales and the uplands of Cantref Mawr, and over the ford across the Gwili.

The surrounding area was part of the commote of Mallaen in the medieval cantref of Cantref Mawr, a region that remained under strong Welsh control for much of the Middle Ages. The castle lay close to the frontier between native Welsh territory and the Anglo-Norman lordships along the coast. This border position explains much of its turbulent history.

 = = History = = 

 = = = Norman foundation = = = 

The first fortification on the site is generally attributed to Richard fitz Baldwin, a Norman knight. He is recorded in the *Brut y Tywysogion* as having "raised a castle at Rhyd-y-Gors" in 1106, during the Anglo-Norman expansion into Deheubarth that followed the death of Rhys ap Tewdwr in 1093. Some historians have proposed that this entry refers to a different castle near Carmarthen. However, excavations at the site in 1974 found pottery and timber post-pits datable to the early twelfth century, which supports an early Norman origin.

The first castle appears to have been a ringwork, a roughly oval enclosure defended by an earthen bank and an external ditch cut into the rock. A timber palisade topped the bank. Traces of the ringwork bank survive beneath the later stone curtain wall on the northern side of the inner ward.

In 1116, during the rising of Gruffydd ap Rhys, Welsh forces attacked the castle and burned the outer defences. The garrison, led by a steward named Walter de Kerlion, held the inner enclosure. The *Brut* says the attackers withdrew "having taken neither castle nor tower", which suggests that some kind of tower, probably of timber, already stood within the ringwork by this date.

 = = = Welsh control = = = 

In the uprisings that followed the death of Henry I in 1135, Welsh forces under Gruffydd ap Rhys and his sons captured and burned Rhyd-y-Gors in 1137. It then appears to have remained in Welsh hands for most of the next eighty years. Under Rhys ap Gruffydd, known as the Lord Rhys, who ruled Deheubarth from 1155 until 1197, the castle was rebuilt and became one of a network of fortified sites protecting the Tywi valley. A charter of about 1184, preserved in a fourteenth-century copy at the abbey of Talley, refers to "our castle of Rhyd-y-Gors" and grants the abbey pasture rights on the adjoining hillside.

After the Lord Rhys died in 1197, his sons fought among themselves for the inheritance of Deheubarth, and the castle changed hands several times. In 1204 it was held by Maelgwn ap Rhys. In 1210 it was surrendered to English royal forces under Falkes de Breauté during King John's campaign in Wales, but the Welsh recovered it in 1212. In 1216, at the council of Aberdyfi, Llywelyn ab Iorwerth, Prince of Gwynedd, divided the lands of Deheubarth among the descendants of the Lord Rhys. Rhyd-y-Gors was assigned to Rhys Gryg, who held it until his death in 1234.

 = = = Stone castle = = = 

Most of the surviving masonry is thought to date from the period of Welsh control in the first half of the thirteenth century. The cylindrical keep in particular has close parallels with towers built by the Welsh princes of Gwynedd and Deheubarth in the 1220s and 1230s. Architectural historians have suggested that Rhys Gryg or his son Maredudd ap Rhys Gryg built the keep and the first stone curtain wall. They may have been influenced by the round keeps of Anglo-Norman castles in the Marches.

Maredudd ap Rhys Gryg held the castle for most of the period from 1234 to 1271, although his loyalty moved between Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, Prince of Wales, and the English crown. In 1257 Maredudd sided with the English. A Welsh army loyal to Llywelyn then besieged the castle for three weeks, and it surrendered after its water supply failed. A royal survey of 1259 records that the castle had been "much broken" during the siege and that its "great tower" had been damaged by fire.

 = = = Edwardian period = = = 

During the first war of Welsh independence in 1277, the castle was held for Llywelyn by Rhys Wyndod, grandson of Rhys Gryg. An English army under Payn de Chaworth advanced up the Tywi valley in the spring of that year, and the castle surrendered without significant resistance in June 1277. Edward I granted custody to the royal justiciar of West Wales. Rhys Wyndod rose in revolt again in 1282, recaptured the castle briefly, and lost it once more in the autumn of that year.

After the Edwardian conquest of Wales, Rhyd-y-Gors became a royal castle administered from Carmarthen. Surviving pipe rolls and accounts of the chamberlain of West Wales record expenditure on the castle between 1283 and 1295 totalling about £1,140. This was a considerable sum, though small compared with the great castles being built in north Wales at the same time. The work included rebuilding the curtain wall, building the twin-towered gatehouse, repairing the keep, and digging a new rock-cut ditch on the northern approach. The accounts name a master mason, Master Henry of Ludlow, and a carpenter, Ralph le Wyte. Neither is known to have worked at other royal castles in Wales.

A further Welsh rising, led by Rhys ap Maredudd in 1287, reached the castle. A royal garrison of twenty men-at-arms and forty crossbowmen held it successfully, and the Welsh force withdrew after six days.

 = = = Later Middle Ages = = = 

Once Wales was relatively settled in the fourteenth century, the castle's military importance declined. It served chiefly as the administrative centre of a small royal lordship. Courts were held in the hall of the inner ward, and the castle housed a prison used for offenders from the surrounding commotes. In 1343 the lordship was granted to Edward, the Black Prince, as part of the Principality of Wales. A survey taken that year describes the castle as being in "good and sufficient repair", but notes that the roof of the keep "lets in the rain".

During the revolt of Owain Glyndŵr, Welsh forces moved through the Tywi valley in July 1403 and captured several castles in the area. Rhyd-y-Gors was held by a small garrison under its constable, John Skidmore. A letter from Skidmore to the king's council, dated 7 July 1403 and preserved in the National Archives, reports that the castle was "besieged on all sides" and that the outer ward and its buildings had been burned. The inner ward held out until a relief force arrived from Carmarthen later that month. Repairs carried out between 1405 and 1409 are recorded, but they were limited in scope.

During the fifteenth century the castle was granted to a series of royal favourites and stewards. By the end of the century much of it had fallen into decay. John Leland, the antiquary, travelled through the area in about 1538 and described it as "a castel now ruinus, wherin dwellith but the keper of the prison".

 = = = Civil War and slighting = = = 

At the outbreak of the English Civil War in 1642, Rhyd-y-Gors was in poor condition. Even so, a Royalist garrison under Sir Henry Vaughan occupied it in 1644, as it overlooked the routes between Carmarthen and the north. Earthworks to the north of the castle are interpreted as a hastily built artillery battery, and these may date from this period. In April 1646 the garrison surrendered to Parliamentary forces under Major-General Rowland Laugharne. In 1647 Parliament ordered the castle to be made "untenable". The slighting appears to have involved demolishing the upper parts of the gatehouse and breaching the curtain wall on its eastern side. Large fallen sections of masonry from this demolition still lie on the slope below the eastern wall.

 = = = Modern history = = = 

The ruins passed through several private owners from the seventeenth century onwards. In the second half of the eighteenth century they became popular with artists and travellers interested in the picturesque. Paintings and engravings of the castle by artists including Thomas Hearne and the brothers Samuel and Nathaniel Buck helped to establish its reputation as one of the more romantic ruins in the Tywi valley. In 1798 the landowner, Thomas Lloyd of Glangwili, carried out landscaping of the slopes below the castle, planting trees and laying out a walk to the summit.

In 1932 the site was placed in the guardianship of the Office of Works. Consolidation of the masonry was carried out between 1933 and 1938, and further work in the 1960s. Cadw has managed the castle since its creation in 1984.

 = = Architecture = = 

 = = = Layout = = = 

The castle is made up of an inner ward on the highest part of the spur and a larger outer ward to the north, separated from it by a rock-cut ditch. A second ditch, much of which has now been filled in, crosses the ridge further north and marks the outer limit of the defences. The masonry is mainly local sandstone rubble. Dressings of a finer grey sandstone, thought to have been brought from quarries near Llandybie, are used in the windows, doorways and quoins.

 = = = Inner ward = = = 

The inner ward is an irregular polygon of about 45 by 32 metres (148 by 105 ft). Its shape largely follows the line of the earlier ringwork. The curtain wall stands to a height of up to 9 metres (30 ft) on the western side, where it rises directly from the edge of the cliff. Elsewhere it has been reduced, in places to its foundations. The wall is about 2.3 metres (7.5 ft) thick and was originally topped by a wall-walk and parapet. Fragments of the parapet survive at the north-western corner.

Footings of several buildings survive against the inside of the curtain wall. On the southern side was a hall about 18 by 8 metres (59 by 26 ft), with a service range at its western end. The excavations of 1974 identified a central hearth and a later fireplace set into the curtain wall. An adjoining chamber block to the east is thought to have provided private accommodation for the constable. A small chapel, identified by a piscina in its southern wall, stood near the keep. A rock-cut cistern in the north-eastern corner of the ward, about 4 metres (13 ft) deep, may be connected with the failure of the water supply during the siege of 1257.

 = = = Keep = = = 

The keep stands at the highest point of the inner ward, near its north-western corner. It is a cylindrical tower with an external diameter of about 12.5 metres (41 ft) at its base. The base has a pronounced battered plinth, and the walls rise to a surviving height of approximately 15 metres (49 ft). The walls are around 3.5 metres (11 ft) thick at ground level.

Inside, the keep had a basement and three upper floors. The basement was entered only through a trapdoor from the floor above, and was presumably used for storage or as a prison. The main entrance was at first-floor level, reached by an external stair, and was protected by a drawbar and possibly a portcullis. The first floor has a fireplace and two splayed windows. The second floor was the principal chamber; it has a larger fireplace with a hooded chimney and a window seat in the south-facing embrasure. A mural stair inside the thickness of the wall linked the floors and led to the roof. The upper storey and the roof have been lost, but corbels for a timber hoarding can be seen at the top of the surviving wall.

The keep has been compared with the round towers at Dolbadarn and Dinefwr. Its plinth and the arrangement of its stair have led several writers to suggest that the same masons may have been involved, although no documentary evidence confirms this.

 = = = Gatehouse = = = 

The gatehouse on the northern side of the inner ward belongs to the royal building campaign of the 1280s and 1290s. It consists of two D-shaped towers flanking a central passage about 11 metres (36 ft) long. The passage was defended by a drawbridge over the ditch, an outer portcullis, two pairs of doors and an inner portcullis, and there were murder holes in the vault above. Arrowslits in the flanking towers covered the approach. The guardrooms on either side of the passage have fireplaces. One of them contains a latrine.

The upper parts of the gatehouse were largely demolished in 1647. The ground floor and parts of the first floor survive, and the grooves for both portcullises are still visible. A carved head corbel built into the eastern tower, possibly representing a crowned king, has traditionally been identified as Edward I. This identification is not certain.

 = = = Outer ward = = = 

The outer ward measures about 70 by 40 metres (230 by 131 ft) and was enclosed by a curtain wall with three small rectangular towers. Most of this survives only as low footings or earthworks. The ward held stables, a bakehouse, workshops and other ancillary buildings, and was the site of the burning recorded in 1403. A simple gate passage through the northern wall was the outer entrance. A causeway carried a track from the gate across the outer ditch.

 = = Archaeology = = 

The site was excavated on a small scale in 1934, during consolidation by the Office of Works, and more thoroughly by the University College of South Wales in 1974 and 1975. The later excavations established the sequence of the ringwork and the stone castle. They recovered pottery from the twelfth to the seventeenth centuries, including local Dyfed gravel-tempered ware and imported Saintonge ware from south-western France. Other finds included crossbow bolt heads, a copper-alloy harness pendant decorated with the arms of the Chaworth family, a quantity of animal bone, and several lead musket balls of seventeenth-century date. These are now held by Carmarthenshire County Museum.

A geophysical survey of the outer ward and the area to the north was carried out in 2011. It identified a number of possible buildings and a probable road running north from the outer gate. It also found traces of a small settlement or market area, which may correspond to the "town of Rhyd-y-Gors" mentioned in a document of 1295. No town is known to have developed there, and the settlement appears to have been abandoned by the end of the fourteenth century.

 = = Folklore = = 

Several local traditions are attached to the castle. One claims that a tunnel runs from the cistern in the inner ward to the bank of the Tywi, and that a golden harp belonging to the Lord Rhys was hidden there. Another says that the ghost of a woman in a grey dress, known locally as *Y Ladi Lwyd* ("the grey lady"), walks along the western wall on autumn evenings. Nineteenth-century collectors of Welsh folklore recorded both stories, and they also appear in Victorian guidebooks to the area.

 = = Present day = = 

The castle is open to the public throughout the year, and there is no admission charge. It can be reached by a footpath from a car park beside the Gwili, about 600 metres (0.37 mi) from the castle. Interpretation boards installed by Cadw in 2009 describe the castle's history and architecture. Cadw regards the site as one of the most important examples of a Welsh-built round keep in south Wales. The surrounding woodland on the slopes of the spur is managed for wildlife, and colonies of lesser horseshoe bats roost in the basement of the keep, which has been closed to visitors since 2015 to protect them.
