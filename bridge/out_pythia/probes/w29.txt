= Clementina Pell =

Clementina Ashworth Pell (14 March 1821 – 9 November 1889) was an English novelist, essayist and periodical contributor of the Victorian era. She is best known for her industrial novel *The Weavers of Hollin Clough* (1858) and the domestic novel *Agnes Thorold* (1863). Her work was widely read in the middle decades of the nineteenth century and drew on her upbringing in the textile districts of the West Riding of Yorkshire. Her fiction combines close attention to the working lives of mill hands and small tradesmen with an interest in religious doubt and in the legal and economic position of women. After a long period of neglect, her reputation was partly revived in the late twentieth century by scholars of Victorian provincial fiction.

= = Early life = =

Clementina Ashworth was born in Hebden Bridge, Yorkshire, the second of five children of Thomas Ashworth, a fustian manufacturer and Wesleyan Methodist lay preacher, and his wife Ellen (née Greenhalgh), the daughter of a Rochdale bookseller. The family's business suffered heavily in the commercial depression of 1826, and Thomas Ashworth was briefly declared bankrupt. The household moved to a smaller property near Mytholmroyd, where Clementina spent most of her childhood.

She was educated mainly at home by her mother, who had access to the stock of her father's shop, and later for two years at a Moravian school for girls at Fulneck. Letters from this period, later published by her niece, record her reading of Walter Scott, Maria Edgeworth and the *Edinburgh Review*. They also record her early unease with the revivalist Methodism of her father's circle, a theme that recurs in her mature fiction.

In 1838 her elder brother, Joseph, was killed in an accident at a carding mill in Todmorden. Pell later described the event as "the first public grief I understood to be also a private one". Biographers have generally treated it as the origin of her interest in factory conditions. In the same year she took a post as a governess with a family of Unitarian merchants in Manchester. There she came into contact with members of the city's reforming middle class, and she began attending lectures at the Manchester Athenaeum.

= = Marriage and early writing = =

In 1844 she married the Reverend Edwin Pell, a Congregationalist minister nine years her senior, then serving a chapel in Salford. The couple had three children, of whom two survived infancy. Edwin Pell was active in the sanitary reform movement. Through his parish work his wife gained detailed knowledge of housing and public health in the poorer districts of Salford, which she used in several later novels.

Pell's first publications were short sketches and moral tales contributed anonymously to *Howitt's Journal* and to the *Christian Remembrancer for Families* between 1846 and 1849. A collection of these, *Lancashire Hearths*, appeared in 1850 under the pseudonym "C. A." and attracted modest notice. Her first full-length novel, *Marsh Light* (1852), was set in the fen country of Lincolnshire, where her husband had briefly held a living. It concerns a drainage engineer's daughter who breaks off her engagement after discovering her fiancé's involvement in a land enclosure fraud. The novel was published in three volumes by the London firm of Bentley and Hood. It sold steadily but was criticised by the *Athenaeum* for "an excess of hydraulic particulars".

= = Literary career = =

= = = Industrial novels = = =

Pell's reputation was established by *The Weavers of Hollin Clough*, serialised in *Fraser's Household Magazine* from January to November 1857 and issued in volume form the following year. Set in a fictional valley modelled on the upper Calder, the novel follows the decline of a community of handloom weavers as power looms are introduced. It centres on the family of Abel Crowther, a weaver and Chartist sympathiser, and on his daughter Hester, who goes to work at the new mill owned by the Lister family. The novel was praised for its use of dialect and its refusal of a conventional reconciliation between masters and men. It was compared, sometimes unfavourably, with the work of Elizabeth Gaskell. The *Westminster Review* called it "a book without a villain, and the more painful for it".

A second industrial novel, *Brindle Street* (1861), was less successful. Set in Salford during the cotton famine, it was written quickly and published while the events it described were still under way. Several reviewers objected to its sympathetic treatment of a striking spinner, and the circulating library firm of Mudie's reportedly reduced its initial order. Pell later called the book "an honest failure".

= = = Domestic and religious fiction = = =

In the 1860s Pell turned increasingly to domestic subjects. *Agnes Thorold* (1863), discussed in its own article, is generally regarded as her finest work. It treats a clergyman's wife who loses her religious faith and must decide whether to conceal the fact from her husband and congregation. The novel's restrained treatment of doubt drew comparison with the controversies around *Essays and Reviews* (1860). It was the subject of a long and largely favourable notice by the critic Walter Bagshaw in the *North British Review*.

This was followed by *The Second Mrs. Haldane* (1866), a novel of stepmotherhood and inheritance, and *A Quiet House in Bury Street* (1871), which follows the residents of a London lodging house over a single year. The latter was serialised in *Good Words*. It was notable for its structure of twelve monthly sections, each told from the viewpoint of a different lodger, an arrangement contemporary critics found either ingenious or disjointed.

= = = Later works = = =

Edwin Pell died of typhoid in 1868. The financial position of the widow and her children depended thereafter largely on her writing. She moved to Islington, where she lived for the rest of her life, and wrote at a steady pace for periodicals including *Macmillan's Magazine*, *Temple Bar* and the *Cornhill*. Her later novels include *The Inheritance of Silas Brandreth* (1877), a story of a Bradford wool merchant's disputed will, and *Lady Margery's Tenants* (1882), which addresses rural housing in Dorset.

Her final completed novel, *The Ferryman's Account* (1886), returns to the Calder valley of her earliest successes. Its narrator is an elderly ferryman recalling the transformation of the district over fifty years. Several critics have read it as a revision of the hopeful closing chapters of *The Weavers of Hollin Clough*. An unfinished novel, *Ann Meadowcroft*, was found among her papers and published in an incomplete state in 1891.

= = = Non-fiction = = =

Alongside her fiction Pell wrote essays on education, the employment of women, and the Married Women's Property Acts. *Women and Wages* (1874), a pamphlet issued by the Society for Promoting the Employment of Women, drew on interviews with seamstresses and shop assistants in north London. She also produced a short biography of the Quaker philanthropist Hannah Wigram (1879) and a volume of travel sketches, *A Summer on the Moselle* (1880).

= = Personal life and beliefs = =

Pell remained a nominal Congregationalist throughout her life, but her letters show increasing sympathy with Broad Church and Unitarian positions after the 1860s. She was a friend of the social investigator Margaret Holroyd and corresponded with several figures in the women's suffrage movement. She did not, however, publicly sign the suffrage petitions of 1866 or 1867. In a letter of 1870 she described herself as "persuaded of the justice of the claim, and unpersuaded that a novelist's name adds anything to it".

Her surviving son, Arthur Pell, became an engineer with the Midland Railway. Her daughter, Ellen Pell Marsden, edited a selection of her mother's letters in 1903.

Pell suffered from recurrent bronchitis in later life. She died at her home in Canonbury Square, Islington, in November 1889, at the age of sixty-eight, and was buried at Abney Park Cemetery.

= = Style and themes = =

Pell's prose is generally described as plain and unornamented. Critics have noted her preference for long expository chapters on work, money and law over dramatic incident. She made extensive use of Yorkshire and Lancashire dialect in her northern novels, and she supplied a glossary to the second edition of *The Weavers of Hollin Clough* at her publisher's request.

Recurring themes in her work include the effects of economic change on established communities; the legal dependence of married women; the conflict between private conviction and public religious duty; and the moral responsibilities of employers. Unlike many contemporaries, she seldom resolved social conflict through marriage across class lines. The scholar Rosamund Leigh has argued that Pell's endings "decline to close the account", leaving economic and religious questions explicitly unsettled.

= = Reception and legacy = =

During her lifetime Pell was regarded as a reliable and serious novelist of the second rank. Her sales were respectable rather than large. *The Weavers of Hollin Clough* went through six editions before 1890, and *Agnes Thorold* was translated into German and Dutch. Obituaries in the *Times* and the *Academy* praised her integrity and her knowledge of northern life, but predicted, accurately, that her work would be little read by the next generation.

Her novels were largely out of print for most of the twentieth century. Interest revived in the 1970s and 1980s with the growth of scholarship on the industrial novel and on women's writing. A scholarly edition of *The Weavers of Hollin Clough* was published in 1984, and *Agnes Thorold* was reissued in a paperback classics series in 1997. A blue plaque was placed on her Islington house in 2004. A selection of her periodical essays, *Clementina Pell: Social Writings*, appeared in 2012.

= Agnes Thorold =

*Agnes Thorold* is a novel by the English writer Clementina Pell, first published in three volumes in 1863. It tells the story of the wife of an Anglican vicar in a northern market town who gradually loses her Christian faith. Much of the novel concerns her struggle over whether to disclose this to her husband. Generally considered Pell's most accomplished novel, it was noted on publication for its sober handling of religious doubt. Since the 1990s it has been studied as an example of the Victorian "novel of faith and doubt".

= = Background = =

Pell began the novel in 1860, during the public controversy over *Essays and Reviews*. Her letters indicate that she was reading works of German biblical criticism in translation at the time. She told her friend Margaret Holroyd that she wished to write "of a doubt that is neither wicked nor triumphant, only true to the one who holds it". The setting, the fictional town of Wendlow, is thought to be based on Skipton, where Pell had relatives. The vicarage household draws in part on her own experience as a minister's wife, although she insisted that the central character was not a self-portrait.

The novel was written over about two years. It was not serialised, apparently because the editor of *Good Words* considered the subject unsuitable for a family magazine.

= = Plot = =

Agnes Thorold, the daughter of a Leeds solicitor, marries the Reverend Philip Thorold, newly appointed vicar of Wendlow. The early chapters describe her energetic participation in parish life, including a Sunday school and a lending library. Through the library she begins to read works of geology and biblical scholarship, and over several years she comes to disbelieve the doctrines her husband preaches.

The middle section of the novel follows Agnes as she conceals her loss of belief. She continues her parish duties while confiding only in Dr. Samuel Kerr, the town's physician and a known agnostic. Their friendship provokes local gossip, which the novel treats as unfounded. A crisis arrives when the Thorolds' young son, Edmund, falls dangerously ill. Agnes finds herself unable to pray with her husband at the child's bedside.

Edmund recovers, and in the final volume Agnes tells Philip the truth. He is distressed and briefly considers resigning his living, but the couple agree to remain together. Agnes withdraws from the religious side of parish work while continuing her educational and charitable activities. The novel ends without either character changing their beliefs, with the two of them walking to the church door, where Agnes "went no further, and he did not ask her to".

* The Reverend Philip Thorold – her husband, vicar of Wendlow

* Lucy Bray – a mill girl who attends Agnes's Sunday school and later becomes a pupil-teacher

= = Themes = =

Critics have identified the central concern of the novel as the relation between private belief and public role. Agnes's doubt is presented not as moral failure but as the result of honest reading and reflection. The novel nevertheless stresses the social cost of disclosure for a clergyman's family. The subplot of Lucy Bray, whose education Agnes continues after giving up religious teaching, has been read as Pell's suggestion that moral and practical duties can survive the loss of doctrinal belief.

The novel has also been discussed for its depiction of marriage. Philip Thorold is shown as neither tyrannical nor wholly sympathetic. His eventual acceptance of his wife's position, without agreement, has been described by the critic Rosamund Leigh as "one of the few Victorian portraits of a marriage that survives a disagreement it cannot settle".

= = Reception = =

Contemporary reviews were mixed but generally respectful. The *North British Review* praised its "rare candour and rarer moderation". The *Record*, an evangelical newspaper, condemned it as "a quiet apology for infidelity", and some circulating libraries were reported to have kept it off open shelves. The *Saturday Review* found the ending unsatisfactory, complaining that "the reader is left at the church door along with the heroine".

The novel sold better than any of Pell's other books except *The Weavers of Hollin Clough*. A one-volume edition appeared in 1865, and German and Dutch translations followed in 1866 and 1870. Interest declined after Pell's death. Modern critical attention began with an essay by Leigh in 1979, and the 1997 paperback edition brought the novel to a wider readership.

= = Adaptations = =

*Agnes Thorold* was adapted for BBC radio as a four-part serial in 1988. A stage adaptation by the Northern Lantern theatre company toured Yorkshire in 2009.
