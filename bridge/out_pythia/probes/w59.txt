Carrawong Heights is a suburb of Sydney, in the state of New South Wales, Australia. It is located approximately 27 kilometres north-west of the Sydney central business district, in the local government area of the City of Hawkesbridge, and is part of the Greater Western Sydney region. At the 2021 census, Carrawong Heights had a population of 11,482.

The suburb occupies a sandstone ridge between Tullagong Creek to the east and the Wirrin Rivulet to the west, both tributaries of the Parramatta River catchment. It is predominantly residential, with a small commercial centre along Bellbird Road and a light industrial precinct in its southern section.

= = History = =

= = = Aboriginal history = = =

Before European settlement, the area now known as Carrawong Heights was occupied by clans of the Darug people. The ridge formed part of a walking route between the river flats to the south and the hunting grounds of the higher plateau to the north. Several rock engravings and grinding grooves have been recorded along the sandstone outcrops of Tullagong Creek, the most significant of which, a group of engravings depicting a kangaroo, two fish and a series of human footprints, is listed on the New South Wales State Heritage Register. The name "Carrawong" is generally believed to derive from a Darug word, though its precise meaning is disputed. An early surveyor's notebook records it as meaning "place of the black cockatoo", while later linguistic studies have suggested it may refer to a stand of trees or a stony hill.

= = = Colonial settlement = = =

The first European exploration of the district took place in 1791, when a party led by a junior officer of the New South Wales Corps followed Tullagong Creek northward in search of arable land. The report of the expedition described the ridge as "of poor and stony soil, but well timbered". In 1803 the colonial government issued a series of small grants along the creek flats to former convicts and free settlers, the largest being a 120-acre grant to Thomas Ellery, a former marine. Ellery's farm, known as Ellery's Rest, produced wheat and maize for the Parramatta market, and the homestead he built around 1812 survives as the oldest standing building in the suburb.

Timber getting was the principal industry on the ridge itself through the first half of the nineteenth century. Stands of blackbutt, ironbark and turpentine were felled and carted to sawpits along the creek, and a small community of timber workers established itself near the present intersection of Bellbird Road and Ellery Street. A slab-built Wesleyan chapel, constructed in 1838, served both as a place of worship and as a schoolroom until a dedicated school was opened by the Board of National Education in 1851.

= = = Orchards and subdivision = = =

By the 1860s the cleared ridgetop had been taken up for orcharding, and Carrawong became known for citrus, stone fruit and, later, persimmons. The orchards were largely worked by family operations, many of them established by settlers of Cornish and German origin. A post office opened in 1874 under the name "Carrawong", and the suffix "Heights" was added in 1889 following a petition by residents who wished to distinguish the ridge settlement from the creek flats.

The opening of the railway line through the neighbouring district of Wirrin in 1893 brought the area within reach of Sydney commuters, although the absence of a station within Carrawong Heights itself limited growth. A number of the larger orchards were subdivided in the decade before the First World War, the first being the Glenalvon Estate, offered for sale in 1909 with the promise of "pure air, fine views and the convenience of town". Sales were slow, and many of the allotments remained vacant until the 1920s.

= = = Post-war growth = = =

Carrawong Heights remained semi-rural until after the Second World War. In 1951 the Housing Commission of New South Wales acquired approximately 180 hectares in the southern part of the suburb for public housing, and construction of some 900 dwellings took place between 1952 and 1959. Private subdivision of the remaining orchards followed through the 1960s and 1970s, and by 1976 the population had grown to over 8,000. The last commercial orchard in the suburb, operated by the Hessler family, closed in 1983; part of its land was later dedicated as Hessler Park.

A light industrial area was established along Kinsella Road in the late 1960s, attracting small manufacturing businesses, joinery works and, later, warehousing and logistics firms. A shopping village was constructed on Bellbird Road in 1964 and redeveloped in 1997 as the Carrawong Heights Village shopping centre.

= = = Recent history = = =

During the 2000s and 2010s, Carrawong Heights underwent a degree of urban consolidation, with a number of older detached houses replaced by townhouses and low-rise apartment buildings, particularly near the commercial centre. A redevelopment of the former Housing Commission estate, begun in 2014, has replaced some of the original public housing with a mix of social, affordable and private dwellings.

In December 2019 bushfires burned through the Tullagong Creek bushland reserve, destroying two houses and damaging several others on the eastern edge of the suburb. The fires prompted a review of bushfire management in the area and the construction of an expanded asset protection zone along the reserve boundary.

= = Geography = =

Carrawong Heights lies on the Hornsby Plateau, at an elevation ranging from about 40 metres above sea level in the creek valleys to 168 metres at its highest point, Signal Hill, near the northern boundary. The underlying geology is Hawkesbury Sandstone, overlain in places by thin shales of the Ashfield formation, which gave rise to the comparatively fertile soils that supported orcharding on the ridgetop.

The suburb is bounded by Tullagong Creek to the east, Wirrin Rivulet to the west, the Northern Arterial Road to the south and Gilmour Road to the north. Neighbouring suburbs include Wirrin to the west, Ellery Park to the south, Tullagong to the east and Mount Kerrigan to the north.

Substantial areas of bushland remain in the creek valleys, most of which are protected within the Tullagong Creek Reserve and the Wirrin Rivulet Bushland. These reserves contain remnants of Sydney Turpentine-Ironbark Forest, a threatened ecological community, as well as sandstone heath and riparian vegetation. Fauna recorded in the reserves includes the powerful owl, the eastern pygmy possum, the red-crowned toadlet and several species of microbat.

= = = Climate = = =

Carrawong Heights has a humid subtropical climate, with warm summers and mild winters. Owing to its distance from the coast, summer maximum temperatures are typically two to three degrees higher than those recorded in the Sydney central business district, while winter nights are cooler, with occasional light frosts in the creek valleys. Average annual rainfall is approximately 980 millimetres, with the wettest months being February and March.

= = Demographics = =

At the 2021 census, there were 11,482 people in Carrawong Heights. Of these, 50.8 per cent were female and 49.2 per cent were male. Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people made up 2.1 per cent of the population. The median age was 38 years, slightly above the national median of 38 years.

Approximately 58.6 per cent of residents were born in Australia. The most common other countries of birth were India (6.9 per cent), China (4.2 per cent), the Philippines (3.1 per cent), England (2.7 per cent) and Lebanon (1.8 per cent). English was the only language spoken at home by 61.4 per cent of residents; other languages spoken at home included Hindi, Mandarin, Arabic, Tagalog and Punjabi.

The most common responses for religion were Catholic (24.3 per cent), no religion (23.9 per cent), Anglican (11.2 per cent) and Hinduism (7.4 per cent). Of occupied private dwellings, 71.5 per cent were separate houses, 19.8 per cent were semi-detached houses or townhouses, and 8.3 per cent were flats or apartments. The median weekly household income was $2,014, compared with the national median of $1,746.

= = Economy = =

The principal centres of employment within the suburb are the Carrawong Heights Village shopping centre, the Kinsella Road industrial precinct, the local schools and the Carrawong Heights Community Health Centre. The industrial precinct accommodates approximately 140 businesses, including a regional distribution centre for a national hardware chain, a number of trade suppliers and several food processing firms.

Most employed residents work outside the suburb, with the largest concentrations commuting to Parramatta, the Norwest business district and the Sydney central business district. The 2021 census recorded that the most common occupations among residents were professionals (22.1 per cent), clerical and administrative workers (14.7 per cent) and technicians and trades workers (14.2 per cent).

= = Heritage = =

Several sites within Carrawong Heights are listed on heritage registers. These include:

* Ellery's Rest, the former homestead of Thomas Ellery, built about 1812 and extended in the 1840s. It is a single-storey sandstock brick cottage with a hipped roof and encircling verandah, and is now owned by the City of Hawkesbridge and operated as a house museum by the Carrawong Heights Historical Society.
* The Tullagong Creek Aboriginal engraving site, listed on the State Heritage Register.
* St Barnabas' Anglican Church, Bellbird Road, a Victorian Gothic church of sandstone constructed in 1872 to a design by the architect Edmund Blacket's office, together with its adjoining churchyard containing the graves of many early settlers.
* The former Carrawong Public School building of 1880, a brick schoolhouse now used as a community hall.
* The Hessler packing shed, a corrugated iron and timber structure of about 1910 in Hessler Park, retained as a reminder of the suburb's orcharding history.

= = Transport = =

Carrawong Heights does not have a railway station. The nearest stations are Wirrin, approximately 2.5 kilometres to the west, and Ellery Park, on the Sydney Metro Northwest line, approximately 3 kilometres to the south. Bus services operated under contract to Transport for NSW connect the suburb with both stations and with Parramatta, Castle Hill and Hornsby. A dedicated bus lane along Bellbird Road was introduced in 2018 to improve peak-hour journey times.

The Northern Arterial Road along the suburb's southern boundary provides access to the M2 and M7 motorways. Bellbird Road serves as the principal north–south thoroughfare through the suburb, while Ellery Street and Gilmour Road carry most east–west traffic. A shared pedestrian and cycle path, the Tullagong Creek Trail, follows the eastern creek valley and links the suburb with the regional cycle network.

= = Education = =

Carrawong Heights contains three government primary schools: Carrawong Public School, established in 1851 and the oldest school in the district; Carrawong Heights South Public School, opened in 1955 to serve the Housing Commission estate; and Signal Hill Public School, opened in 1974. Secondary education is provided by Carrawong High School, a co-educational comprehensive high school opened in 1962, which has a specialist program in agricultural science that makes use of a small working farm on its grounds.

Non-government schools include St Columba's Catholic Primary School and Hawkesbridge Christian College, an independent school for students from kindergarten to Year 12. The nearest campus of a technical and further education institution is located at Wirrin.

= = Community facilities = =

The Carrawong Heights Library, operated by the City of Hawkesbridge, opened in its present building on Ellery Street in 2006, replacing an earlier branch library established in 1968. The adjoining Carrawong Heights Community Centre provides meeting rooms, a hall and facilities for community groups and the local early childhood health service.

The suburb has a volunteer bushfire brigade of the New South Wales Rural Fire Service, formed in 1937 and housed in a station on Gilmour Road. The brigade played a principal role in defending properties during the 2019 fires.

= = = Parks and recreation = = =

The suburb contains more than twenty public parks and reserves. The largest open spaces are the Tullagong Creek Reserve and the Wirrin Rivulet Bushland, which together contain about 11 kilometres of walking tracks. Hessler Park, on the site of the former Hessler orchard, includes sports fields, a playground and a remnant grove of persimmon trees maintained by volunteers. Signal Hill Lookout, at the suburb's highest point, offers views towards the Blue Mountains to the west and the Sydney central business district to the south-east.

Sporting facilities include the Carrawong Heights Aquatic Centre, opened in 1971 and refurbished in 2015, and the Ellery Oval, home ground of the Carrawong Heights Cricket Club and the Carrawong Hawks junior rugby league club.

= = Culture and events = =

The Carrawong Heights Historical Society, founded in 1967, maintains a collection of photographs, documents and artefacts relating to the history of the district and manages the museum at Ellery's Rest. The annual Persimmon Festival, held each May in Hessler Park since 1986, commemorates the suburb's orcharding heritage and features food stalls, live music and displays by local community groups.

The Carrawong Heights Community Choir and the Bellbird Players, an amateur theatre company that stages productions in the community hall, are among the longest-running cultural organisations in the suburb. A Diwali celebration organised by local community associations has been held in the Village shopping precinct each year since 2012.

= = Politics = =

Carrawong Heights is located within the state electoral district of Hawkesbridge and the federal Division of Mitchell. At the local level, it forms part of the Central Ward of the City of Hawkesbridge, which elects three councillors. Historically, the southern part of the suburb has tended to favour the Australian Labor Party, while the northern part has more often returned majorities for the Liberal Party of Australia.
