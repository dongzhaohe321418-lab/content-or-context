\subsection{Hand-over stitching answers a different question}
\label{sec:diag}
In the classical hand-over setting ($B$ reads the tokens, but every layer-$L_B$ state is replaced by the bridged $A$ state), bridged systems score below $A$ alone on accuracy, here and on two small testbeds (Appendix~\ref{app:small}). Table~\ref{tab:diag} varies optimisation length, bridge capacity and layer pairing for 70M$\to$160M (real-text probe, one seed, 512 test windows). Because this is a single-seed diagnostic with checkpoints chosen post hoc, we read it descriptively.

Three observations. (i) No configuration reaches $B$ alone, and none exceeds $A$'s accuracy, but the longest-trained non-linear bridge has \emph{lower} cross-entropy than $A$ alone (4.535 vs 4.626). The common observation that a stitched system ``performs worse than the sender'' therefore depends on the metric and on training: it is not a bound, and a downstream decoder can in principle use a representation better than the sender's own upper layers do. (ii) Training length and capacity matter: on accuracy, the non-linear bridge at 3000 steps closes 75\% of the gap between the 600-step linear bridge and $A$ alone. At a constant learning rate the linear map is not monotone in training length (23.61 at 1500 steps, 22.69 at 3000), so single-checkpoint comparisons between stitches deserve caution. (iii) $A$'s final hidden state, taken after its output layer norm, is a poor source both here and in the communication setting (\S\ref{sec:where}). We did not test the pre-normalisation final residual state, so this says nothing about the information present before $A$'s last normalisation.

In the communication setting $B$ keeps its own suffix computation and receives only what it lacks, so the question there is not whether a stitched system beats either model but whether the message improves $B$ beyond what any message would. The two settings measure different things.
